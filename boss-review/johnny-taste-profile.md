### Finding 4: density

Each Witty Yeti package carries about **12–18 joke lines**: name, subtitle, a headline, 2–3 panel sections of 2–4 lines each, a seal or badge, and fine print. That **confirms the ~12-slot card.** Today's Shank card has about 60.

### Revised tests (these replace the originals below where they differ)

1. **One fast leap.** *(Changed.)* The reader gets it instantly. A fast pun ("#1 in #2") passes. A two-step chain ("Quieter, too") fails. Stating the act is fine **if it's cartoon-absurd**; a realistic stabbing stays implied ("Not in Kevin").
2. **Short.** *(Unchanged, confirmed.)* Listing lines are 4–12 words; panel bullets stay under about 20.
3. **A second beat must add a new, worse fact, never a comment.** *(Changed.)* "We wanted one too." passes. "Literally." / "We shouldn't know that last part." fail.
4. **Prefer the act to the paperwork.** *(Softened.)* Fallout jokes appear in his work ("Destroy the Evidence," "Your lawyer will be relieved"), but they're absurd and escalating, never procedural.
5. **Cheerfully cold toward a person.** *(Confirmed.)* "Coworkers who shouldn't reproduce (but might)."
6. **NEW: commit to a real retail format and escalate inside it.** How to Use 1–4, Accessories Included, Our Story, protocol steps, "Perfect for" lists, seals. The format is the setup and the last item is the payoff.
7. **NEW: the packaging is louder than the listing.** Headlines can be punny and exclaim. The fine print can be dry.

## The 5 tests

### 1. One small leap, and no violent words
His hits make the reader picture the violence from one ordinary word: "Eventually." "Not in Kevin." "Bathes in red." None of them names a weapon or a crime. "Quieter, too" needs two steps of reasoning (quiet, then stealthy, then murder), and he said it takes too long.

**Test:** name the one word the reader has to hear the dark way. If you can't, or if the line needs a design note or an icon to explain it, it fails.

### 2. Short
His own lines are 6 and 7 words. He said "too long" twice. His one long approved line is a list whose beats are 3–4 words each, so it speeds up as it goes.

**Test:** about 12 words or fewer, or a list whose beats keep getting shorter.

### 3. The copy keeps selling and never reacts to its own joke
"...Not in Kevin. Completely leak-proof design." The last sentence comes after the punchline, and it is why the line works: the brand doesn't notice what it just said. So the rule is not "nothing after the punchline". The rule is "nothing that reacts to the punchline."

**Test:** if the last clause comments on the joke ("Literally." "We shouldn't know that last part." "One of those got past Legal."), cut it. If it goes blindly back to the sales pitch, keep it.

### 4. The act itself, not the legal fallout
He wanted "fingerprints are evidence" darker and cruder. His hits are about blood, Kevin and interns, not trials, juries, HR files or coroners.

**Test:** is the joke about the pen doing the thing, or about what happens in court afterward? Prefer the thing.

### 5. Cheerfully cold toward a real person
"Interns don't count." "Not in Kevin." A person gets treated like a line item, by a brand that is proud of its pen. The warmth is the salesperson's pride, not hedging or chattiness. He went silent on coy ("Look, we all know what this is"), dry spec-speak ("TIP HARDNESS: RATED FOR REPEATED IMPACT") and chatty-long.

**Test:** is the brand proud? Is someone casually reduced to an item on a list?
