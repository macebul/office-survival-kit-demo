# Rap Sheets -- Surface Map (Complete Copy Specification)

**Last Updated:** July 7, 2026
**Purpose:** Every word of copy that goes on the Rap Sheets tuck-end card box, with exact surface placement. This document is self-contained -- no other document is needed to execute the design.
**Box dimensions:** 4.0" wide x 2.5" tall x 0.75" deep
**Box material:** 18pt paperboard, printed all 7 surfaces (6 faces + tuck flap)
**Box type:** Tuck-end
**Box position in briefcase:** Tray compartment (card deck slot)
**Contents:** 15 complaint/grievance cards

---

## FRONT FACE (4.0" x 2.5" -- display face, visible when box sits in tray)

### Element 1: Brand (top of face)

```
WITTY YETI
```

**Design note:** Parent brand, always top. Clean, condensed, institutional.

### Element 2: Division Name (below brand)

```
Department of Interpersonal Corrections
```

**Design note:** Abbreviated as "DIC" -- the abbreviation is intentional and central to the comedy of this item. The abbreviation should appear where natural but the box never acknowledges the double meaning. The institutional voice treats DIC as a completely legitimate department abbreviation.

### Element 3: Division Tagline (below division name)

```
"Every office needs a DIC."
```

**Design note:** In quotes. Reads as a sincere brand statement. The comedy is entirely in the reader's interpretation. The institutional format never cracks. This is the primary tagline for the division and should be visually subordinate to the division name but clearly readable.

### Element 4: Product Name (hero headline -- largest text on the face)

```
RAP SHEETS
```

**Design note:** Large, bold, dominant. This is the product name and should be the first thing the eye reads after the brand block.

### Element 5: Subtitle (directly below product name, smaller)

```
Conflict Resolution Starters
```

**Design note:** CRITICAL VISUAL TREATMENT -- "Resolution" must appear as a handwritten insertion. The design should look as if the original text read "Conflict Starters" and someone realized that was too honest, so they squeezed "Resolution" in above the line with a small caret/arrow (^) pointing up to where "Resolution" is crammed in between "Conflict" and "Starters." The handwritten correction is the joke -- the format reveals the product's true nature ("Conflict Starters") while the hasty fix tries to make it sound constructive. The "Resolution" text should be in a different style (handwritten, slightly tilted, squeezed into the space) to contrast with the clean institutional type around it.

### Element 6: Expectation Line (below subtitle)

```
Will not improve the relationship. Will improve how you feel about it.
```

**Design note:** Two sentences. The parallel "Will not / Will" structure creates the reversal. Should be styled as a secondary product claim -- smaller than the subtitle but clearly readable. Not fine print.

### Element 7: Quantity Line (bottom area of face)

```
Qty: 15. For everything else, there's the Cubicle Shank.
```

**Design note:** Starts as a standard contents count, then cross-references another product in the kit. The MasterCard "for everything else" echo gives it a familiar rhythm. "Cubicle Shank" is the tactical pen item -- the implication is that when complaint cards run out, you escalate to the pen. This rewards buyers who've seen the pen elsewhere in the kit.

## BACK FACE (4.0" x 2.5" -- Incident Summary Report)

### Element 1: Report Title (top of face, prominent)

```
INCIDENT SUMMARY REPORT
```

### Element 2: Division + Context Line (below title, smaller)

```
Department of Interpersonal Corrections -- Field Data
```

### Element 3: Data Block (outcomes -- main visual element of the back face)

**Format:** Three rows of field data, styled as survey results or deployment metrics. Each row has a category label on the left and a value on the right. The first two rows use percentages. The third row breaks the data format entirely.

```
Filed peacefully:                                          12%
Filed aggressively:                                        63%
Filed and then things got weird in the parking lot:        [thumbs up emoji]
```

**Design note:** The percentages establish a data format. The third line breaks it -- instead of a percentage, the value is a thumbs up emoji (or a simple thumbs-up icon if emoji doesn't work in print). The report cannot quantify what happened in the parking lot. It just... approves of it. The format stays institutional while the content goes off the rails. The thumbs up should be rendered as a simple icon/illustration, not text.

### Element 4: Quote (below data block, styled as a pull quote)

```
"Documentation isn't passive-aggressive. It's proactive-aggressive."
```

**Design note:** No attribution. No third sentence. The reversal is in swapping "passive" for "proactive" -- the expected word gets replaced with one that's worse. The quote ends after two sentences. Do not add additional lines.

### Element 5: Customer Review (lower portion of face)

**Format:** Styled as a product review with star rating. The review voice is distinctly different from the institutional voice on the rest of the box -- this is a real person (the "unhinged escalator") whose review starts normal and deteriorates.

```
"Used one on Monday. Used the rest on Monday. Wednesday was
a meeting about Monday. Thursday I worked from home."
```

**Star rating:** Five stars (visual -- use star icons, do not write "five stars")

**Design note:** The repetition of "Monday" IS the escalation -- the reviewer deployed all 15 complaint cards in one day. "Wednesday was a meeting about Monday" implies HR intervened. "Thursday I worked from home" is the understated punchline -- the reviewer was either asked to leave or chose to hide. The five-star rating after describing what's clearly a workplace disaster is the format/content gap. The review should be visually distinct from the institutional copy above it -- different font treatment, quotation marks, perhaps a lighter background or review-card styling.

## LONG SIDE A (4.0" x 0.75")

### Element 1: Primary Text (single line, fills the strip)

```
For disputes you've already lost the high ground on.
```

**Design note:** Conversational tone. Acknowledges that the buyer is past the point of being reasonable -- the product meets them at their pettiest. "The high ground" is a universally understood concept (being the bigger person). This line admits you've already fallen off that cliff. Should be styled as a single clean line of text, centered on the narrow strip. White text on dark background.

## LONG SIDE B (4.0" x 0.75")

### Element 1: Hazard Symbols (left portion of strip)

**Format:** Four small GHS-style hazard diamond symbols arranged in a horizontal row. These are the standard chemical/industrial hazard classification diamonds, but applied to a box of complaint cards.

**Design note:** The hazard symbols should look authentic -- proper diamond shapes with standard iconography. The visual joke is that complaint cards are classified as hazardous materials. The specific symbols can be adapted from standard GHS categories (flammable, toxic, irritant, environmental hazard) but the exact selection is a design decision.

### Element 2: Warning Text (right portion of strip, next to symbols)

```
CAUTION CONTENTS MAY ESCALATE
```

**Design note:** All caps, warning label format. Reads as a shipping/safety warning but the "contents" are complaint cards and "escalate" means the interpersonal situation, not a physical hazard. The hazard symbols to the left visually reinforce the warning format. Together they treat the product like a regulated substance.

## SHORT SIDE A (2.5" x 0.75")

### Element 1: DIC Certification Stamp (centered, fills the surface)

**Format:** Small circular or rectangular certification badge/stamp, styled as an official approval seal.

```
DIC APPROVED
```

**With a checkmark ( ) positioned and angled so it visually serves as the missing letter K.**

**Design note:** This is the key visual gag on this surface. The checkmark is a standard certification mark -- it's "just" a checkmark indicating approval. But its position and angle make it look like it completes the word. The institutional format never acknowledges this. The stamp should look authentic -- clean lines, official seal styling, perhaps a circular border. The checkmark should be prominent enough that the visual double-reading is unavoidable but subtle enough that the format stays straight-faced.

## SHORT SIDE B (2.5" x 0.75")

### Element 1: Header Text (top of surface)

```
ALSO WORKS ON:
```

### Element 2: Three Icons with Labels (arranged horizontally below header)

**Format:** Three small illustrations/icons arranged left to right, each with a text label below. The sequence escalates from reasonable to absurd. The category of target changes each time (item/place to person to animal).

```
[icon: head peeking over a fence]     [icon: person with raised finger, lecturing]     [icon: cat sitting, eyes narrowed]
         Neighbor                              Mother-in-law                                  The cat
```

**Icon design notes:**

- **Neighbor:** Only the top of a head and eyes visible above a fence line. The fence is the universal "neighbor" signifier. Nosy, watching, always there. Simple silhouette.
- **Mother-in-law:** Full figure or upper body, one finger raised in a lecturing/pointing gesture. Arms could be crossed with one hand up. Disapproving posture. Visually distinct from the neighbor (full figure vs. head-only, no fence).
- **The cat:** Cat sitting upright, facing forward, eyes narrowed into judgmental slits. Classic "cat judging you" pose. No extra props -- the expression does the work. Must be clearly legible at small print size.

**Design note:** The escalation is in the target category -- you can file complaints on your neighbor (reasonable), your mother-in-law (personal), and your cat (absurd). The labels are necessary because the icons alone may not communicate the specific targets at this scale. Labels should be small, clean text directly below each icon. "The cat" uses the article "The" because it's not any cat -- it's THE cat. The one that's been judging you. The sequence should be evenly spaced across the 2.5" width.

## TUCK FLAP (~2.5" x ~1" -- interior, first thing seen when box is opened)

### Element 1: DIC Seal (centered, small)

**Format:** Small version of the Department of Interpersonal Corrections seal/logo. No copy.

**Design note:** This is a brand mark, not a copy surface. The tuck flap is a transition from the outside of the box (marketing, comedy, personality) to the inside (the actual cards). A small seal provides a moment of institutional formality before the cards are revealed, without competing with the surfaces that are doing the comedy work. The seal should be simple -- the DIC abbreviation or a department emblem, small, centered, clean. No additional text.

## PARKED LINES (not placed on this box -- available for other items or future use)

These lines were developed during this session but did not earn placement on the final box:

- **"Choose your first victim carefully. Just kidding. Go with your gut. You already know."** -- strong conspiratorial line, too long for tuck flap, no other surface had room
- **"USE BEFORE COOLING OFF"** -- subverts "allow to cool before use" safety instruction, but the reference doesn't land for most readers
- **"YOU KNOW WHO THIS IS FOR."** -- from v01 tuck flap, replaced by DIC seal
- **"Start with Kevin."** -- from box copy v1 tuck flap, replaced by DIC seal
- **"Not legally binding. Emotionally? Devastating."** -- expectation management alternative, not selected
- **"15 cards. Not enough. We know."** -- quantity line alternative, replaced by Cubicle Shank cross-reference
