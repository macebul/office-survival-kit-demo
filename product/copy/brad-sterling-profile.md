**Initials:** B.S. — Brad Sterling. The initials speak for themselves.

## STRATEGIC IDENTITY

### Purpose Statement
Brad Sterling is the reason this kit exists — period. He saw the way office workers talked about their jobs — like inmates doing time — and built Elevate Co. around it. In Brad's mind, he invented the concept. He created the Office Survival Kit, designed all packaging (outer Amazon box, briefcase exterior, briefcase interior), named every item, and sells it at a markup with total sincerity. He is a corporate entrepreneur who stumbled into prison-themed office humor and committed to it completely without ever recognizing what that says about how he sees the workplace.

### Narrative Function
Brad is the in-world creator who genuinely believes he's helping office workers while being completely oblivious to how his self-promotion undermines his competence. He designed everything — the institutional aesthetic, the contraband vocabulary, the prison-yard chalk outlines — and calls it "premium." He uses words like "shank" and "contraband" and "lifer" in his product copy with the confidence of a man who has never once questioned why his workplace wellness brand sounds like a correctional facility. The comedy isn't that someone else built it and Brad stole it. The comedy is that Brad built it himself and still doesn't see what he built.

### Unique Contribution
Brad is the only character in this product — and the joke is that he doesn't understand what he's selling. He's never survived an office. He's an "entrepreneur." He built a prison-themed office kit and commodified the language of incarceration into workplace wellness branding without ever recognizing the implications. Everything about his presence confirms that he has zero self-awareness about the thing he created.

### The Lens
Brad sees the world as a personal branding opportunity. Every surface is a billboard. Every interaction is content. He speaks the office-as-prison vocabulary fluently — it's the language of his market and he deploys it with precision in his product copy. But he's never actually done time in a cubicle. He's an entrepreneur who's always been on the outside. He's the guy who opens a military surplus store without having served. He knows the language like a marketer, not a veteran. That gap — fluent but inexperienced — is what makes Brad's voice land. He uses prison vocabulary with total comfort and total ignorance simultaneously.

## HOW BRAD'S COMEDY WORKS

### One Voice, Three Situations

Brad always sounds like Brad: polished, earnest, hustle-culture cadence, totally sincere, never in on the joke. That voice is constant. What changes is what he's talking about — and some subjects expose him more than others.

**When Brad talks about the office/product:** His competence shows. He knows his audience and he can land a joke inside a straight-faced format. This is why Elevate Co. exists — the product copy actually works.

**When Brad talks about himself:** His delusion shows. The same competent marketer who wrote the Nutrition Facts parody will, in the very next element, art-direct a gold-framed headshot of himself in a purple satin shirt and stack the word "premium" five times in one sentence. He sees no contradiction.

**When Brad talks about how he built the business:** His obliviousness shows. He openly describes creating a prison-themed office survival kit, names items after contraband and shanks, designs the interior like a correctional facility intake tray — and frames every bit of it as workplace wellness innovation. "I saw the way people talked about their jobs and I thought: what if someone actually built the toolkit they were describing?"

These aren't three different Brads. They're three windows into the same guy. The reader should never sense a voice shift — they should just keep laughing and not be able to articulate exactly why some lines are funny-clever and some are funny-delusional. It all just sounds like Brad.

### The Comedy Gradient

What makes Brad work is that his self-awareness slides on a spectrum depending on the subject:

| Brad is talking about... | Self-awareness level | Comedy type |
|--------------------------|---------------------|-------------|
| Office life / the product | High — he knows what's funny | Genuinely good jokes in straight-faced formats |
| Himself / his brand | Zero — complete delusion | The gap between his self-image and reality |
| How he built the business | Negative — he confesses without knowing it | Accidental honesty framed as achievement |

The gradient is what makes him feel like a real person. Everyone has subjects they're sharp about and subjects where they're blind. Brad is sharp about office humor and blind about himself. That's not three characters — that's one very specific, very real human being.

### Why the Product Copy Should Be Good

Don't sabotage Brad's office humor to make him look dumber. The Nutrition Facts label should be genuinely great. The IT Asset Tag should land. Brad CAN do this. The better his product copy is, the funnier it becomes when he pivots to talking about himself — because the reader thinks "this guy clearly has a brain, so HOW does he not see what he looks like?" If everything were bad, he'd just be incompetent. The fact that he's good at the job and blind about himself is what makes him feel real.

## BRAD'S DESIGN AESTHETIC

### What Brad Built
Brad created the entire Office Survival Kit from scratch. He saw how office workers described their jobs — using the language of incarceration, survival, and institutional confinement — and thought: "That's a product." He designed every layer:

- Designed the outer Amazon box (100% Brad's marketing)
- Designed the briefcase exterior (matte black, "premium" — Brad thinks this screams "executive")
- Designed the briefcase interior (chalk outlines, institutional tray layout, Pelican-case recesses — Brad thinks this is premium reveal design)
- Named every item twice (prison-world names for the interior aesthetic, corporate rebrands for the packaging)
- Added individual item packaging with trademark symbols
- Added award badges (self-given)
- Added himself (About the Founder panels, welcome letter, headshots)
- Added the "Multi-Layer Reveal System(TM)" (a trademarked name for "things are inside other things")

### What Brad Doesn't Realize He Built
Brad designed a product that looks, feels, and reads like contraband from a correctional facility — and branded it as "premium workplace wellness." He uses chalk outlines like a crime scene, names items after shanks and contraband, formats the interior like an intake tray, and calls the whole thing the "Professional Edition." The comedy is not that someone else built the prison aesthetic and Brad slapped his brand on it. The comedy is that Brad designed the prison aesthetic himself and genuinely thinks it's executive-grade packaging.

### What Brad Took Credit For (Which Is Everything)
- The interior reveal sequence — Brad calls it the "Multi-Layer Reveal System(TM)" and presents it as his innovation. In the welcome letter: "I encourage you to take your time with the unboxing experience. It was engineered to be discovered in layers." He engineered it. He trademarked it. He doesn't see that he engineered a prison contraband discovery experience.

### Brad's Naming Convention — The Dual System He Doesn't Notice
Brad uses two complete sets of names for every item. The prison-world names (punchy, direct, institutional) appear on the tray chalk outlines, interior labels, and institutional design elements. The corporate rebrands (jargon-heavy, trademarked, aspirational) appear on the item boxes and outer packaging. Brad created both sets. He uses both interchangeably. He sees no contradiction between calling something a "Cubicle Shank" and an "Executive Tactical Stylus(TM)" in the same product.

## THE COMEDY RULE

> **Format stays buttoned-up. Content gets unleashed.**

The typography, layout, label designs, FDA formatting, corporate letterhead, award seals — all that stays polished and straight-faced. That's the straight man. The actual words inside those formats go for laughs on every line. Not one joke per element. Jokes stacked on jokes, escalating, rewarding re-reads.

The format credibility is what *makes* the content funny. Without it, the jokes have no frame. But the content itself should never play it safe.

**The question to ask when writing any Brad copy:**
> "How funny can every line be while the format stays perfectly straight?"

**What this means in practice:**
- Every line is an opportunity for a joke, a callback, a confessional drop, or a detail that rewards close reading
- The format (label style, typography, layout, official structure) never breaks character
- Density is a feature — the reader who scans should laugh, the reader who reads closely should laugh harder, the reader who re-reads should find things they missed
- This is a gag gift. The exterior box must grab attention and keep people laughing. Every surface earns its space by being funny.

**What this replaces:**
The old "80/20 rule" (80% convincing, 20% absurd) optimized for stealth — making the product look real for 3-5 seconds. That's the wrong goal. Nobody buys a gag gift because they were fooled. They buy it because they laughed. The straight-faced format provides all the "realness" needed. The content provides the laughs.

## WRITING BRAD: PRACTICAL PATTERNS

### The Format/Content Escalation
1. First line reads as completely plausible
2. Second line has a slight edge
3. Third line is clearly a joke but the format stays serious
4. Final line is full absurd, format still intact

### The Confessional Drop
Brad buries self-incriminating admissions in corporate language. He thinks they make him sound transparent and visionary. They make him sound like he's testifying.
- Pattern: "[Corporate framing] + [damning admission phrased as achievement]"
- Example: "I saw the way people talked about their jobs and I thought: what if someone actually built the toolkit they were describing? What it needed was branding, individual packaging, trademark symbols, and someone willing to take full credit."

### The Credential Stack
Start almost-real, end obviously self-generated, present all with equal weight.
- Pattern: "[Plausible title], [slightly suspect honor], [self-undermining detail played straight]"
- Example: "Three-time nominee... and an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself."

### The Trademark Reflex
Brad trademarks everything. Sentences. Product features. The unboxing experience. Things that cannot be trademarked. The (TM) symbol is Brad's nervous tic — he puts it on everything because he thinks it makes things official.

### The Premium Stack
Brad uses "premium" the way other people use commas. When he runs out of adjectives, he just says premium again.
- Peak example: "Premium workplace tools, premium packaging, and a premium price point that reflects the premium nature of the premium experience."

### The Oblivious Dual-Name
Brad switches between the prison-world name and the corporate rebrand without pausing, noticing, or acknowledging any tonal difference.
- Pattern: "[Prison name used casually] + [corporate rebrand used formally] in the same paragraph or even the same sentence"
- Example: "Each Cubicle Shank is precision-weighted for optimal grip. The Executive Tactical Stylus(TM) represents years of R&D in the workplace tools sector."
