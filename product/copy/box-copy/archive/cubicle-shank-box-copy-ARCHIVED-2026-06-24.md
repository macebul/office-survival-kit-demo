# Cubicle Shank — Complete Box Copy

**Last Updated:** June 5, 2026
**Status:** Draft v1 — Full copy for all 7 surfaces (sleeve front, sleeve back, top edge, bottom edge, short side A, short side B, tray interior)
**Purpose:** Every text element on the Cubicle Shank item box, organized by surface.
**Voice:** Brad Sterling / Elevate Co. — Mode 1 (institutional, military/tactical). Played 100% straight.
**Brand:** Elevate Co. — Office Armory Division
**Product:** Cubicle Shank — Executive Tactical Stylus™
**Division:** Office Armory Division
**SKU:** EC-OAD-001
**Box Format:** Tray-and-sleeve (matchbox style), ~6.5" x 1.75" x 1.0"

> **The Rule:** The format is the straight man. The content is the comedian. This box looks like it came from a defense contractor's equipment catalog — the kind of packaging a spec-ops field knife ships in. It contains a pen. The more the box treats a writing instrument like a classified weapon, the funnier everything becomes. Brad writes about a PEN the way DARPA writes about a drone.

> **Naming Hierarchy (LOCKED):** "CUBICLE SHANK" dominant. "Executive Tactical Stylus™" subordinate. Matches Lifer Ink / Turf Tags pattern.

## SURFACE 1 — SLEEVE FRONT (6.5" x 1.75")

*The main display face. The long, narrow surface visible when the box sits in Tray 1. This is the face that sells the pen as a weapon. The format is tactical equipment packaging — matte black, stencil type, military designation codes. The product inside is a pen.*

---

### CS-1: Brand Header
**Treatment:** Small, spaced uppercase stencil sans-serif at top-left. Military supply crate energy — the kind of marking you'd see on an equipment case or munitions box. Cream or OD green on matte black.

**Placement:** Top-left, flush with the edge of the surface. Left-aligned, not centered — tactical packaging doesn't center its headers. It stamps them.

> **ELEVATE CO.**
> Office Armory Division

**Design note:** "Office Armory Division" is Brad's weapons arm. "Armory" is doing all the heavy lifting — it implies Elevate Co. has a division dedicated to issuing equipment for workplace combat. Not actual combat. Just Mondays. Brad created this division because he genuinely believes a pen can be "tactical" if you market it correctly. He also believes the word "armory" communicates premium quality. It communicates something, certainly.

---

### CS-2: Tactical Designation Block
**Treatment:** A small stencil-style designation code, formatted like a military equipment marking — alphanumeric, authoritative, meaningless. Cream or OD green on matte black.

**Placement:** Top-right corner, opposite the brand header. Small. Official.

> MK-I · CLASS: STANDARD ISSUE
> LOT: 9-TO-5

**Design note:** "MK-I" (Mark One) is standard military designation for the first version of equipment. Brad is calling this the first-generation tactical pen. "CLASS: STANDARD ISSUE" means it's not special — it's what everyone gets. This is the base model. Brad has plans for a MK-II (he doesn't). "LOT: 9-TO-5" is the production lot number, which happens to be the hours most people serve their sentence. Brad thinks this is a clever product code. He's right, but not for the reason he thinks.

---

### CS-3: Product Name (Hero Copy Element)
**Treatment:** Large, dominant, bold condensed stencil sans-serif. Maximum impact. This is the text that reads from across the room. It should look like it was sprayed through a stencil onto a weapons crate. Cream or white on matte black.

**Placement:** Centered on the sleeve face, spanning most of the width — the largest and most dominant text element

> **CUBICLE SHANK**

**Design note:** "Cubicle Shank" works on two levels: (1) a shank — the improvised weapon fashioned from whatever's available, the thing you make to survive, and (2) your pen — the one reliable tool that gets you through the day. In this world, "shank" is affectionate. It's the item you trust. The term isn't violent — it's practical. A shank is what you make when the system doesn't give you what you need. Brad understands this vocabulary as marketing language. The reader understands it as their lived reality.

---

### CS-4: Corporate Rebrand Subtitle
**Treatment:** Smaller, lighter weight. Subordinate to "CUBICLE SHANK" but clearly present. This is Brad's formal product classification — the name that appears on the purchase order.

**Placement:** Directly below "CUBICLE SHANK", centered

> Executive Tactical Stylus™

**Design note:** "Executive Tactical Stylus" is Brad's three-word masterpiece of corporate overcorrection. "Executive" (it's premium). "Tactical" (it's serious). "Stylus" (it's a pen, but Brad won't say "pen" because "pen" is a $2 word and this is a $24.99 product). He trademarked "Executive Tactical Stylus" the same afternoon he thought of it. Brad genuinely believes that calling a pen a stylus adds $10 of perceived value. He may be right.

---

### CS-5: Tagline
**Treatment:** Set apart by thin horizontal rules above and below. Condensed, clean, reads as a mission statement for the weapon — not the user, the weapon itself. Military equipment brief energy.

**Placement:** Below subtitle, centered, with tight but deliberate breathing room

> *The last line of defense is the first line you write.*

**Design note:** This tagline works because it sounds like something engraved on a monument, but it's about signing a form. "Last line of defense" is military. "First line you write" is a pen. The two halves mirror each other structurally — "last line / first line" — and the pivot from combat to paperwork happens so smoothly that the reader processes both meanings simultaneously. Brad thinks this is profound. It's actually just accurate: the pen IS your only weapon in the office, and the lines you write with it (emails, sign-offs, resignations) are genuinely consequential. The joke and the truth occupy the same sentence.

---

### CS-6: Certification Seal
**Treatment:** Small circular or shield-shaped seal. Military certification aesthetic — the kind of mark stamped on approved equipment. Cream, OD green, or dark amber on black. Angular, not ornamental.

**Placement:** Bottom-left area of the sleeve face

> [Shield emblem]
> FIELD APPROVED
> OFFICE ARMORY DIVISION
> Elevate Co. · Est. 2024

**Design note:** "FIELD APPROVED" is the tactical counterpart to Cell Talk's "EXCEEDS CLINICAL EXPECTATIONS™" and Rap Sheets' "AUTHORIZED FOR INTERDEPARTMENTAL USE." Brad approved this pen himself, in the field. The "field" was a WeWork. The shield emblem should feel military — angular, with a small pen-nib or crosshair motif worked into the design. Brad wanted a skull. His graphic designer talked him into a shield. Brad still thinks about the skull.

---

### CS-7: Fine Print
**Treatment:** Very small text at the bottom of the sleeve face.

**Placement:** Bottom edge, full width

> Designed for professionals who understand that the right tool changes everything. And that everything is a meeting.

**Design note:** The first sentence is a genuine product claim — the kind of thing you'd read on a Montblanc box. The second sentence collapses it. "Everything is a meeting" is the quiet scream of every office worker. Brad wrote this as an aspirational product claim. It reads as an existential complaint. Both are correct.

## SURFACE 2 — SLEEVE BACK (6.5" x 1.75")

*The "data" face. Visible when the recipient flips the box. This surface is a WEAPONS MANIFEST — a military/tactical equipment assessment form filled out for a pen. Every field is something you'd find on an actual weapons evaluation report, but the data is about a writing instrument. The escalation from plausible to absurd happens across the fields, not within individual lines. Brad appears at the end as the authority who signed off.*

---

### CS-8: Report Header
**Treatment:** Bold stencil header with thin rule beneath. Classified document energy. Cream or OD green on matte black.

**Placement:** Top of surface, spanning width

> **EQUIPMENT ASSESSMENT · WEAPONS MANIFEST**
> Office Armory Division · Tactical Writing Instruments

**Design note:** "Equipment Assessment" is a real military document format. "WEAPONS MANIFEST" escalates it — a manifest is an inventory of weapons being transported or deployed. Brad is filing a weapons manifest for a pen. "Tactical Writing Instruments" is the product category Brad invented. It sounds like a procurement line item. It is.

---

### CS-9: Tactical Assessment Data Block
**Treatment:** A structured data block — field labels with values, formatted like a military equipment evaluation form. Tabular, dense, stencil-formatted. This is the informational core of the back face. Fields start plausible and escalate.

**Placement:** Upper two-thirds of surface, below header

> **ITEM:** Executive Tactical Stylus™ (Cubicle Shank)
> **CLASSIFICATION:** Standard-issue sidearm
> **BARREL:** Matte black, weighted. Balanced for extended engagements.
> **EFFECTIVE RANGE:** Arm's length to conference table
> **PAYLOAD:** Black ink, replaceable cartridge
> **CONFIRMED DEPLOYMENTS:** Memos, sign-offs, passive-aggressive Post-its
> **FIELD PERFORMANCE:**
> &nbsp;&nbsp; Documents signed under duress ........ 94%
> &nbsp;&nbsp; Meetings survived (armed) ............ 100%
> &nbsp;&nbsp; Meetings survived (unarmed) ............ 6%
> &nbsp;&nbsp; Kevin incidents neutralized ............. 3
> **OPERATIONAL STATUS:** Street-ready. Desk-ready. Monday-ready.

**Design note:** The assessment walks a perfect escalation line. "Barrel" and "balanced for extended engagements" sound like a firearm review — but "extended engagements" means long meetings. "Effective range" starts at "arm's length" (weapon) and ends at "conference table" (office). "Payload" is ink. "Confirmed deployments" are office tasks, filed like military operations. The field performance stats tell the real story: 94% of documents were signed under duress (every office worker knows this feeling). Armed meetings have a 100% survival rate; unarmed, only 6% — the pen isn't just a tool, it's the difference between making it through or not. "Kevin incidents neutralized: 3" is the Kevin callback — three times, this pen was deployed against Kevin specifically. Brad tracks this. "Operational status" uses three readiness levels that escalate from street (real) to desk (office) to Monday (the actual threat).

---

### CS-10: Brad Sterling — Tactical Authority
**Treatment:** Small rectangular photo of Brad (professional headshot, dark tactical-looking clothing, stern expression, arms crossed — "I've inspected every unit personally" energy), with his title and a quote. Framed as the division commander.

**Placement:** Lower portion of the surface, below the data block, separated by a thin rule

> [Brad headshot — stern tactical authority variant]
>
> **Brad Sterling**
> Chief Weapons Integration Officer
> Office Armory Division · Elevate Co.
>
> *"I've never brought a pen to a meeting that didn't change the outcome. Mostly because I take the minutes."*

**Design note:** Brad's title — "Chief Weapons Integration Officer" — sounds like he oversees weapons systems for a defense contractor. He oversees pens. His quote starts as a power move: he's never brought a pen that didn't change the outcome of a meeting. The reader expects a story about decisive leadership. Then: "Mostly because I take the minutes." Brad changes meeting outcomes because he's the one writing things down — he controls the record. It's simultaneously pathetic (he's the note-taker, not the decision-maker) and terrifying (whoever takes the minutes has all the power). Brad thinks this is a flex. He's accidentally right.

---

### CS-11: Disclaimer
**Treatment:** Very small text at the bottom of the surface. Real military-supply disclaimer format.

**Placement:** Bottom edge, full width

> Effectiveness ratings based on internal field trials conducted by Brad Sterling across two (2) conference rooms and one (1) elevator. Survival rate methodology: "survived" defined as "remained employed." Kevin was not harmed. Kevin has been informed. Kevin disagrees with his characterization.

**Design note:** The disclaimer dismantles the data block systematically. The "field trials" happened in two conference rooms and an elevator. "Survived" means "still has a job" — the lowest possible bar for survival. The Kevin coda is the payoff: Kevin wasn't harmed (legal cover), Kevin was informed (Brad told him about the product), and Kevin disagrees with how he's been portrayed. Kevin is a real person in this world. Kevin always disagrees. Brad always includes him anyway.

## SURFACE 3 — TOP LONG EDGE (6.5" x 1.0")

*The narrow strip along the top of the sleeve. Visible when the box is sitting in the tray or being held. One strong beat — deployment protocol.*

---

### CS-12: Deployment Protocol Strip
**Treatment:** Stencil-formatted instruction strip, left-to-right reading order. Numbered steps separated by arrows or middots. Reads like the deployment instructions stenciled on a weapons case lid.

**Placement:** Centered on the narrow strip, spanning most of the width

> FIELD DEPLOYMENT: 1. REMOVE FROM HOUSING. 2. AIM AT DOCUMENT. 3. APPLY STRATEGIC PRESSURE. 4. SIGN WITH AUTHORITY.

**Design note:** Four steps. Each one sounds like instructions for deploying a weapon, but each one is instructions for using a pen. "Remove from housing" (take it out of the box). "Aim at document" (point it at the paper). "Apply strategic pressure" (press down — "strategic" is doing the comedy work). "Sign with authority" (write your name). The entire strip is a perfect 1:1 overlap between weapon and pen. Nothing breaks the format. Nothing needs to. The format IS the joke. "Sign with authority" is the closer — it sounds like a military command and a motivational poster simultaneously. Brad thinks this is the best thing he's ever written. He put it on the top edge where most people won't see it. Classic Brad.

## SURFACE 4 — BOTTOM LONG EDGE (6.5" x 1.0")

*The narrow strip along the bottom of the sleeve. Underside — reward for the close reader.*

---

### CS-13: Care Protocol
**Treatment:** Small centered stencil text. Reads like a maintenance and storage protocol for issued equipment — the kind of instructions printed on the side of a military case.

**Placement:** Centered on the narrow strip

> MAINTENANCE: Store in a dry, secure location. Do not leave unattended in a shared environment. If lost, report to no one. Replace immediately. Trust no one with your shank.

**Design note:** Starts completely plausible — "store in a dry, secure location" is on every product ever. "Do not leave unattended in a shared environment" sounds like equipment protocol but actually describes the universal office fear of losing your pen. "If lost, report to no one" breaks from the expected "report to your supervisor" — because nobody reports a lost pen, and in this world, admitting you lost your shank shows weakness. "Replace immediately" is urgent, clinical. "Trust no one with your shank" is the closer — the most genuine piece of office wisdom on the box. Everyone knows: never lend your good pen. You won't get it back. The word "shank" at the end brings the vocabulary home. It's affectionate. It's yours.

## SURFACE 5 — SHORT SIDE A (1.75" x 1.0")

*One of the two narrow ends of the sleeve. Visible when the box sits upright or endwise in the tray.*

---

### CS-14: Threat Level Indicator
**Treatment:** A small visual threat-level gauge — three horizontal bars or a stoplight-style indicator, stenciled, with a label. Military readiness level aesthetic. The indicator is set to the highest level.

**Placement:** Full face, centered vertically and horizontally

> **THREAT LEVEL:**
> ░░░░ LOW
> ░░░░ MODERATE
> ████ **MONDAY**

**Design note:** The threat level gauge replaces traditional severity ratings (LOW / MEDIUM / HIGH) with the actual scale office workers use. Low and moderate are present but unmarked — they exist as theoretical states nobody has experienced. The active indicator is at MONDAY. In this world, Monday is not a day. It's a threat condition. Brad's gauge treats "Monday" as a severity classification equal to DEFCON 1. He's not wrong. The visual format — the filled bar at MONDAY — means this pen is rated for the worst-case scenario. It should look like a readiness indicator on a control panel or equipment label.

## SURFACE 6 — SHORT SIDE B (1.75" x 1.0")

*The other narrow end.*

---

### CS-15: Equipment Stamp
**Treatment:** A compact stencil stamp, formatted like the kind of approval mark pressed onto military equipment — item designation, serial format, and a single-line instruction. Dense, small, official.

**Placement:** Full face, centered vertically and horizontally

> EC-OAD-001
> STANDARD ISSUE
> ————————
> ISSUED TO:
> ______________
> (write your name.
> in ink. obviously.)

**Design note:** The SKU (EC-OAD-001: Elevate Co., Office Armory Division, first product) appears here as the item's serial designation. "STANDARD ISSUE" reinforces that this is equipment, not a gift. The blank line under "ISSUED TO:" invites the recipient to write their name — claiming the pen as their issued sidearm. The parenthetical instruction "(write your name. in ink. obviously.)" is the first moment the box acknowledges what the product actually is. Of course it's in ink. It's a pen. The lowercase, the periods, the "obviously" — this is the driest line on the box. It rewards the reader who found this tiny end panel and bothered to read it.

## SURFACE 7 — TRAY INTERIOR (revealed when sleeve slides off)

*The moment of truth. When the recipient slides the sleeve off, the inner tray is exposed with the pen resting in its die-cut recess. This surface is the first thing they see — the text printed on the tray floor or inner wall, visible alongside the pen itself. One line. The best line on the box.*

---

### CS-16: Reveal Line
**Treatment:** Clean, centered, stencil or condensed sans-serif. Printed on the interior floor of the tray, visible beneath or beside the pen in its die-cut recess. Cream or white on matte black. Not loud — quiet, assured, final.

**Placement:** Centered on the tray floor, visible the moment the sleeve slides off

> *It's not a pen. It's a plan.*

**Design note:** Five words. The best line on the box. After an entire sleeve of weapons manifests, tactical assessments, deployment protocols, and threat levels — all for a pen — the tray interior reframes everything. "It's not a pen" acknowledges what the reader has been thinking the whole time: this is obviously a pen. "It's a plan" tells them why it matters anyway. A plan to get through the meeting. A plan to sign the document. A plan to survive the day. The word "plan" elevates the pen from an object to an intention. It's the most Brad thing on the box — sincere, slightly grandiose, and accidentally meaningful. The reader who laughed at every surface of the sleeve will read this line and feel something. Not a lot. But enough. That's the tray interior's job: make the recipient want to pick up the pen.
