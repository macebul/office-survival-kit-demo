# PART 1: THE COMEDY WRITING SYSTEM

This is the most important section. Everything else serves this. Read it completely before writing a single word.

---

## 1.1 THE WORLD

**The office is a prison. This is not a metaphor.**

Nobody in this universe thinks the office/prison comparison is clever or funny. There's nothing to compare -- the office just IS this way. You serve your sentence. You do your time. You survive your shift. The language of incarceration IS the language of corporate life. It's the water everyone swims in.

This is what makes the product work: the reader recognizes their OWN reality, not a joke about their reality.

### Shared Vocabulary (used without comment, as the default language)

| This world's term | What it refers to | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| Doing time / serving your sentence | Working at a company | Matter-of-fact |
| Your cell | Your cubicle or desk | Normal |
| The yard / yard time | Break room, common areas, breaks | Normal |
| Mess hall | Break room / kitchen | Normal |
| Solitary | Working alone, noise-canceling headphones, bathroom hiding | Normal |
| Contraband | Anything that makes the day bearable but isn't officially sanctioned | Normal |
| Lifers | Long-tenured employees | Respectful, even |
| Doing a bid | A stint at a company | Normal |
| Release day | Last day, retirement, quitting | Aspirational |
| Parole | Notice period, transitioning out | Normal |
| The warden | Upper management, HR, the boss | Normal |
| Intake / processing | Onboarding | Normal |
| Commissary | Vending machines, the office supply closet | Normal |
| Shank / shiv | Your pen, your one reliable tool | Affectionate |
| Ink | Tattoos that mark your time served | Earned |
| Turf | Your desk, your claimed spaces | Territorial |
| Snitch | The person who reports everything to management | Known hazard |
| Rap sheet | Your performance review history | Dreaded |

**Rules:**
- This vocabulary is used EVERYWHERE -- item names, packaging labels, Brad's marketing, product descriptions. Stepping outside it should feel like stepping out of character.
- The world is played straight. Nobody winks. Nobody says "get it?" The moment anyone acknowledges the comparison as a comparison, the world breaks.
- Absurdity comes from **specificity within the premise**, not from the premise itself. The premise is the ground floor. The jokes live in the specific details, the specific items, the specific ways Brad monetizes it.
- New terms can emerge naturally. The test: would someone in this world use it without thinking twice?

### The World's Tone

**Sardonic, not cynical.** The people in this world haven't given up -- they've adapted. Brad saw a market. The reader is getting through it with humor and solidarity. Nobody is nihilistic. The attitude is: "this is absurd, we all know it's absurd, and here's how we get through it together."

That warmth underneath the dark humor is what makes this a gift someone gives to a coworker they actually like. It's not mean. It's validating.

## 1.2 THE TWO BRADS

Brad Sterling operates in two distinct modes, and the comedy comes from the fact that both exist on the same product, written by the same person, with zero self-awareness about the gap between them.

### Brad Mode 1: Product Copy Brad
**When Brad is writing about the office, the product, the jokes**

This Brad is actually **competent**. He knows his audience. He can write a sardonic Nutrition Facts label, a deadpan IT Asset Tag, a perfectly absurd Care & Storage warning. This is Brad the marketer -- he understands what's funny about office life and packages it well. This is why Elevate Co. exists at all. The product works.

**Voice:** Dry, institutional, straight-faced. The humor comes from the format (official labels, FDA layouts, QC stickers) played totally straight while the content escalates into absurdity. Brad executes this competently.

**Examples (from existing exterior copy):**
- "PROPERTY OF: NOBODY ANYMORE"
- "Serving Size: 1 Monday / Servings Per Container: Too Many"
- "DO NOT REMOVE UNDER PENALTY OF MONDAY"
- "Not a significant source of work-life balance, upward mobility, or the feeling that any of this matters."

**Why it works:** The reader laughs WITH the copy. It's genuinely funny. Brad knows how to sell a gag gift.

### Brad Mode 2: Personal Brand Brad
**When Brad is writing about Brad**

This Brad is a disaster. The moment the subject becomes himself -- his story, his vision, his credentials, his photo -- every ounce of self-awareness evaporates. He becomes a hustle-culture LinkedIn post given physical form. He is completely, unshakably sincere. He never winks. He thinks this is what leadership looks like.

**Voice:** Aspirational, self-mythologizing, motivational-speaker cadence. Everything is "premium," "vision," "disrupt," "elevate." Trademark symbols on everything (Brad's nervous tic).

**Examples:**
- "He didn't see contraband. He saw potential."
- "Premium workplace tools, premium packaging, and a premium price point."
- "Three-time nominee for the International Workplace Innovation Awards and an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself."
- "A Brad Sterling Experience(TM)"

**Why it works:** The reader laughs AT Brad.

### The Gap IS the Joke

The single funniest thing about the product is that both Brads are on the same packaging. The reader encounters competent product humor AND unhinged self-promotion, realizes the same person created both, and that realization is its own punchline.

**CRITICAL: Don't sabotage the product copy to make Brad look dumber.** The Nutrition Facts label should be genuinely great. The IT Asset Tag should land. Because the BETTER the product copy is, the MORE absurd Brad's founder bio becomes by contrast. If everything were bad, Brad would just be incompetent. The fact that he's good at the job and terrible at self-awareness is what makes him a real (and funnier) character.

### Brad's Comedy Gradient

Brad always sounds like Brad. What changes is what he's talking about:

| Brad is talking about... | Self-awareness level | Comedy type |
|---|---|---|
| Office life / the product | High -- he knows what's funny | Genuinely good jokes in straight-faced formats |
| Himself / his brand | Zero -- complete delusion | The gap between his self-image and reality |
| How he built the business | Negative -- he confesses without knowing it | Accidental honesty framed as achievement |

### Brad Writing Patterns

**The Format/Content Escalation:**
1. First line reads as completely plausible
2. Second line has a slight edge
3. Third line is clearly a joke but the format stays serious
4. Final line is full absurd but still formatted perfectly

**The Confessional Drop:** Brad buries self-incriminating admissions in corporate language. He thinks they sound transparent and visionary. They sound like testimony.
- Pattern: "[Corporate framing] + [damning admission phrased as achievement]"
- Example: "What it needed was branding, individual packaging, trademark symbols, and someone willing to take full credit."

**The Credential Stack:** Start almost-real, end obviously self-generated, present all with equal weight.
- Example: "Three-time nominee... and an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself."

**The Trademark Reflex:** Brad trademarks everything. Sentences. Product features. The unboxing experience. Things that cannot be trademarked. The (TM) symbol is his nervous tic.

**The Premium Stack:** Brad uses "premium" the way other people use commas.

**The Oblivious Dual-Name:** Brad switches between the prison-world name and corporate rebrand without noticing any tonal difference. "Each Cubicle Shank is precision-weighted for optimal grip. The Executive Tactical Stylus(TM) represents years of R&D."

### Brad NEVER:
- Winks at the camera or acknowledges the joke
- Shows genuine expertise about office survival (he's never worked in one)
- Recognizes the prison aesthetic for what it is
- Appears in handwriting -- Brad is always printed, polished, designed
- Questions why his "workplace wellness brand" sounds like a correctional facility

## 1.3 THE COMEDY MECHANICS

### The Core Rule

> **"Format stays buttoned-up. Content gets unleashed."**

The typography, layout, label designs, FDA formatting, corporate letterhead, award seals -- all stay polished and straight-faced. That's the straight man. The actual words inside those formats go for laughs on every line. The format credibility is what MAKES the content funny.

### The 6 Humor Elements

Every piece of copy needs **at least 2** of these 6 elements. "Recognizable" should almost always be one of them:

| Element | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Clever** | Wordplay, structural comedy, double meanings, hidden layers | A badge that works on 3 levels simultaneously |
| **Bizarre** | Unexpected combinations, absurd juxtapositions | A coroner reviewing a pen and giving it 5 stars |
| **Cruel** | Dark humor, uncomfortable truths, schadenfreude | "Grip tested across relaxed hands, writing hands, and one we'd describe as 'determined.'" |
| **Naughty** | Taboo-adjacent, things you shouldn't say, the loophole | "We put the pen in penitentiary." |
| **Recognizable** | "That's MY office" -- specific details the reader has lived | Kevin "borrowing" your labeled lunch |
| **Cute** | Charm, warmth, unexpected tenderness inside dark comedy | "Bold. Brave. Washable." (deflation with affection) |

### The Escalation Pattern

Line 1: Completely plausible
Line 2: Slight edge
Line 3: Clearly a joke, format stays serious
Line 4: Full absurd, format still perfectly intact

**The format NEVER cracks.** The moment the format acknowledges it's being funny, the comedy dies.

### The 7 Mechanical Rules

1. **Joke lands on the LAST word or phrase.** Nothing after the punchline. (If the line was funnier when it ended three words earlier, cut those three words.)
2. **Setup is a REAL product copy format.** Feature bullet, spec, disclaimer, FAQ, care instruction, badge, stamp, etc.
3. **Punchline word must be CONCRETE and UNCOMFORTABLE.** Not abstract concepts. Specific nouns, actions, situations. "Denim" is funny. "Materials" is not.
4. **Talk TO the buyer.** "Your ___." "You'll ___." Make the reader complicit.
5. **Be CHEERFUL about something you shouldn't be cheerful about.** Warm, enthusiastic about something alarming.
6. **Never instruct the reader to be threatening.** Describe the product so the threat is OBVIOUS without directing it.
7. **Rule of three when using lists.** Two normal items, third breaks the pattern with a REAL THING. (Three from the same category isn't funny. Two normal + one crazy IS funny.)

### Additional Mechanics

- Strongest lines are 5-13 words
- Ratio: ~60-70% normal product copy, 30-40% absurd
- The second sentence makes the first worse
- Short beats long
- No puns -- Brad is dry
- No exclamation points -- keep it institutional and flat
- Every insult needs absurd specificity. "You're annoying" is nothing. "You have the energy of a wasp trapped in a car that everyone's pretending isn't there" is copy.

## 1.4 CRAFT STANDARDS

### Sentences
- Simple. Like a witty email to a friend.
- Prune words that don't serve the humor.
- Short beats long. Always.

### Imagery
- Write visually. The reader should see a scene.
- Only include details that make the picture funnier.
- Leave room for the reader's imagination.

### Word Choice
- Use funny words: familiar but rarely used, often with hard consonants.
- Use pop culture references the target audience (25-45 office workers) recognizes.
- Use animal analogies when stuck.

### Exaggeration
- Find the worst case, multiply by ten.
- The exaggeration must have near-logic underneath it.
- If the reader can't imagine it almost working, the exaggeration is random, not funny.

### Density
- Every surface should reward re-reading.
- Surface scan = one laugh. Close read = escalating laughs.
- Hidden details, Easter eggs, and callbacks are features.

### Callbacks
- End pieces by referencing something that already got a laugh.
- Callbacks create a sense of craftsmanship and closure.

### Specificity Over Puns
- Humor comes from specific details and observations, not wordplay.
- "Kevin took your lunch again" is specific. "Lunch and munch" is a pun. Kill the pun.

## 1.5 THE VOICE -- WITTY YETI ENERGY

### What the consumer-facing voice IS

A **warm, cheerful, slightly conspiratorial friend** who is genuinely excited to sell you something ridiculous. Think: your fun, slightly irresponsible buddy who found something amazing and can't wait to show you.

The brand **KNOWS what it's selling.** It found a loophole. It's cheerfully proud. It's not confused, coy, or playing dumb.

**Tone markers:** "Look," "Seriously," "Trust us," "We're not going to ask," "We believe in you," "Which is to say." The brand talks TO you, not AT you.

### What the voice is NOT
- NOT dry, clinical, or neutral
- NOT professional or corporate-sounding
- NOT a one-liner comedian rattling off disconnected jokes
- NOT confused about its own product
- NOT instructing the reader to be threatening
- NOT observational office humor ("meetings suck," "survived another Monday")

### The Peppiness Requirement (CRITICAL -- read this twice)

We repeatedly fall into writing copy that's too professional, too serious, too neutral. It reads like a competent copywriter instead of an enthusiastic friend. The copy needs:

- **Enthusiasm and energy** -- the brand is EXCITED about this product
- **Warmth and personality** -- it feels like a person wrote it, not a company
- **Levity** -- even the dark jokes should feel light and fun, not ominous
- **Casual, conversational delivery** -- not stiff product copy with a twist at the end

**TOO NEUTRAL:** "Won't bleed through paper. Can't speak for anything else."
**CLOSER TO RIGHT:** "Other pens leak in your pocket. This one doesn't leak anywhere. Not in your pocket. Not in your hand. Not in Kevin. Completely leak-proof design."

The second version is chatty, building, listing things off excitedly. Kevin just happens to be on the list. That's the energy.

### How the Two Voices Interact

**Brad's voice (Mode 1 and Mode 2)** is the in-world voice -- it appears on the packaging as if Brad created it. The **Witty Yeti voice** is the consumer-facing voice that appears in descriptions, feature bullets, and anywhere the real brand talks directly to the buyer.

On most item boxes, you're writing **Brad Mode 1 (Product Copy Brad)** -- competent, dry, institutional. The Witty Yeti energy bleeds through in how charming and warm the overall copy reads. Brad doesn't know he's being warm. He thinks he's being professional. But the reader feels the warmth because the writing underneath is genuinely enthusiastic about what it's selling.

Brad Mode 2 (Personal Brand) shows up in specific locations: the About the Founder panel, any self-promotional content, any "premium" branding language, the welcome letter.

## 1.6 RECURRING CHARACTERS

Three characters exist alongside Brad. None have a voice -- they are referenced, never heard.

### Kevin
The coworker everyone has. Known hazard. Uses other people's things. No concept of personal property.

**How to use:** Reference by first name, casually, as if everyone knows who he is. Never introduce or explain. Kevin is pre-existing, assumed knowledge.

**Target density:** 0-1 references per item box. 4-6 across the full kit.

**Example:** "Do not leave unattended in shared spaces. Kevin has been talked to. It didn't help."

### Gary
Quality inspector. Brad's guy. Exists ONLY on QC stamps, inspection badges, and compliance stickers. Never in prose.

**How to use:** Stamps and badges only. "INSPECTED BY: GARY", "QC APPROVED -- G. [ILLEGIBLE]"

**Target density:** 2-3 across the kit.

### Diane (HR)
Implied through policy language and handbook citations. Never named directly.

**How to use:** Her existence is felt in bureaucratic caution, places where the tone shifts as if legal reviewed it.

**Target density:** 1-2 across the kit.

### Character Interaction Rules
1. Kevin and Gary never interact.
2. Diane and Brad never directly conflict.
3. Only Kevin is named in prose. Gary = stamps only. Diane = policy references only.
4. None of these characters undermine Brad. They all serve Brad's world.
5. Density discipline matters. Scarcity makes each reference land.

## 1.7 KILL ON SIGHT

Automatic disqualification. If you wrote it, delete it:

- **Puns.** Brad is dry.
- **Exclamation points in product copy.** Institutional and flat.
- **Winking at the reader.** No "get it?" No nudge-nudge.
- **Actual workplace advice.** This is comedy, not a self-help product.
- **Making Brad self-aware.** He never realizes the contradiction.
- **Generic insults.** Every insult needs absurd specificity.
- **Acknowledging the office/prison comparison as a comparison.** The world breaks.
- **Buttons/tags/reactions after the punchline.** Nothing follows the funny part.
- **Abstract/corporate punchline words.** "Concerns," "responsibility" -- too vague. "Denim," "Kevin," "coroner" -- concrete.
- **Observational office humor.** "Meetings suck" isn't copy. It's a tweet.
- **Three items from the same category in a rule-of-three.** Two normal, one crazy.
- **Leading with violence and pivoting to product.** Lead with product.
- **Repeating the same structural trick across variations.** Vary the angle.
- **Explaining the joke.**
- **Writing the brand as confused about its own product.**

# PART 2: PACKAGING COMPONENT PLANNING

Before generating any copy, you need to figure out what goes ON the packaging. Real product packaging has specific recurring elements that make it look and feel authentic. The comedy comes from executing these elements perfectly while the content escalates.

---

## 2.1 THE PLANNING PROCESS

When starting a new item, BEFORE writing copy:

### Step 1: Study Real Packaging

Identify 3-5 real products in the same physical category as your item (e.g., for a pen box: real pen packaging; for a tattoo pack: real temporary tattoo packaging; for a card deck: real card game packaging). Note what elements appear on those packages:

- Brand identity elements (logo, tagline, division name)
- Product identification (name, subtitle, variant)
- Trust/credibility elements (badges, seals, certifications, awards)
- Information hierarchy (what's biggest, what's smallest)
- Regulatory/compliance elements (warnings, age ratings, safety info, ingredients)
- Instructional elements (how-to, application method, care instructions)
- Marketing/social proof (quotes, reviews, comparison charts, features)
- Visual elements (photos, icons, illustrations, diagrams)
- Fine print (disclaimers, legal text, SKU, barcode area)
- Structural elements unique to that product type

### Step 2: Build the Component Inventory

Create a table listing every packaging element the item box needs. For each element, note:

| Component | Real-World Source | Brad's Version | Surface | Comedy Angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: Safety warning | Real pharmaceutical warning | "IF SWALLOWED, CONTACT YOUR SUPERVISOR. NOT BECAUSE IT'S DANGEROUS. BECAUSE WE HAVE QUESTIONS." | Bottom edge | Format fidelity -- pharma warning on a tattoo box |
| Example: Care symbols | Textile care symbols (laundry tags) | All four symbols with X through them -- on a non-textile product | Short side | Wrong product category entirely |

### Step 3: Assign Comedy Formats

Each component gets a "real format" it's parodying. The format IS the straight man. Map each one:

| Category | Real Formats to Parody |
|---|---|
| Trust/credibility | Good Housekeeping seal, UL listing, Board certification, As Seen On TV, FDA approval, industry council approval |
| Regulatory | FDA Nutrition Facts, pharmaceutical warnings, CPSC notices, age ratings, ingredient lists |
| Instructional | IKEA assembly diagrams, airline safety cards, medical application instructions, textile care symbols |
| Social proof | Amazon verified reviews, celebrity endorsements, institutional endorsements (coroner, HR, legal), professional association quotes |
| Identity | Government agency naming, military division naming, hospital department naming, corporate subdivision naming |
| Data | Clinical trial results, field deployment statistics, customer satisfaction surveys, comparison charts |
| Legal | Terms of service, warranty cards, liability disclaimers, fine print footnotes that undermine everything above them |

### Step 4: Identify the Item's Institutional World

Each item box lives in a specific institutional parody world that Brad created as a "division" of Elevate Co. This world determines the format language for every element on the box.

**Established divisions:**
- Cubicle Shank: Office Armory Division (weapons/tactical)
- Lifer Ink: Center for Dermal Achievement (body modification/military commendation)
- Turf Tags: Bureau of Territorial Affairs (territorial governance/IKEA)
- Cell Talk: Center for Normative Optimization (behavioral therapy/self-help)
- Rap Sheets: Interpersonal Accountability Division (complaint filing/legal)
- Sweet Nothings: Ambient Relations Bureau (passive communication/diplomacy)

The division determines Brad's title on that box, the institutional language used, and the specific real-world format being parodied. A body modification division uses medical/military formats. A territorial affairs bureau uses government/regulatory formats. Consistency within each item's world is what makes the comedy land.

## 2.2 STANDARD BOX SURFACES AND ELEMENTS

### Tuck-End Box (Cell Talk, Rap Sheets, Sweet Nothings, Lifer Ink, Turf Tags)
- **Front face** (largest surface): Brand header, product name (hero), subtitle, tagline, classification badge, quantity/contents, authorization seal, fine print
- **Back face**: Secondary format parody (data block, survey results, comparison chart, etc.), Brad photo + quote + title, disclaimer
- **Long side A** (top edge): Instructional strip OR single-line copy
- **Long side B** (bottom edge): Safety warning / single-line comedy
- **Short side A**: Icon strip, stamp, or single visual element
- **Short side B**: Icon strip, stamp, or single visual element
- **Tuck flap** (interior, seen on opening): Opening moment -- one punchy line

### Tray-and-Sleeve Box (Cubicle Shank)
- **Sleeve front**: Same as tuck-end front face
- **Sleeve back**: Same as tuck-end back face
- **Sleeve long sides** (top/bottom edges): Narrow strip copy
- **Sleeve short sides** (end caps): Narrow strip copy
- **Tray interior**: Copy visible when sleeve is removed -- the product reveal moment
- **Sleeve interior** (optional): Hidden copy on the inside of the sleeve

### Carded Blister Pack (if applicable)
- **Card front**: Hero surface -- brand, product name, tagline, badge, contents
- **Card back**: All remaining copy -- features, description, data block, Brad, fine print, barcode
- **No side panels, no interior tray, no sleeve**

## 2.3 WHAT MAKES PACKAGING FEEL AUTHENTIC

The comedy technique in this product is **professional packaging executed at production quality, with content that escalates into absurdity.** The packaging looking real IS the joke. The more it looks like a product you'd see at Target, the funnier the content becomes.

**Authenticity markers that make the comedy work:**
- Real barcode/SKU area (even if the numbers are jokes)
- Proper hierarchy (brand > product name > subtitle > tagline)
- Badge/seal placement that mirrors real certification marks
- Fine print that actually looks like fine print (size, placement, density)
- Consistent division/brand identity across all surfaces
- Instructional icons that look like real icons
- Data blocks that use real formatting (dot leaders, tabular data, field labels)
- Brad's photo presented like a real founder photo (not a joke photo -- Brad thinks it's professional)
- Consistent SKU taxonomy (EC-[DIVISION]-[NUMBER])

**Things that break authenticity:**
- Obviously joke-only elements with no real-format anchor
- Inconsistent branding between surfaces
- Missing expected elements (a box with no barcode area, no fine print, no quantity statement)
- Comedy that lives in the layout instead of the content (the layout should be straight; the content does the work)

- Each uses a real product copy format as the straight man
- Each has warmth and personality -- they sound like someone talking, not writing
- Each trusts the reader to get it without explanation
- Each ends on the funny part

## 4.3 BOSS FEEDBACK HISTORY (applies to all items)

### Approved with enthusiasm
- "Other pens leak in your pocket. This one doesn't leak anywhere. Not in your pocket. Not in your hand. Not in Kevin. Completely leak-proof design." -- "really good"
- CUSTOMER QUESTION: "Does it come in red?" ANSWER: Eventually. -- "excellent"

### Structural feedback
- Rule of three: "Normal, normal, crazy. Three of the same category isn't funny. Two normal things followed by one crazy thing IS funny."
- Verified purchaser reviews: "A user who is using the product normally, and then it turns [dark] is the right setup/structure. You really want to surprise them, in a good way."
- Boss wants it DARK, delivered with warmth. Not tame.

### Universal rejection
- v01 of all items was rejected: "not funny enough, doesn't meet the standards set by existing Witty Yeti products." Too serious, too professional, too neutral, not enough personality.

### Lessons from Cubicle Shank sessions
- **Badges need layered comedy.** "Good Shanking Seal of Approval / Janitor Verified" works because of THREE simultaneous layers: Good Housekeeping format parody + janitor = housekeeper pun + janitor cleans up after shankings. Single-mechanic labels don't clear the bar.
- **Quantity lines need subversion.** The cross-category comparison works because each competitor has a specific, funny flaw and the product wins. Just listing a number with a dark implication isn't enough.
- **Feature bullets need playful energy.** Writing dark and ominous without warmth repeatedly fails. Absurd material escalation works: "through the sentence, through the page, or through denim."
- **Stop at the punchline.** If the line was funnier three words earlier, cut those three words. "Determined" was the punchline -- everything after killed it.
- **Physical product facts are non-negotiable constraints.** If there's no cap on the pen, every cap joke is dead. Know the physical product.
- **Second lines can diminish the first.** "We put the pen in penitentiary" is complete on its own. Sometimes the best revision is deleting the problem.

# PART 5: THE ITERATIVE WORKFLOW

## 5.1 HOW TO START A NEW ITEM

### Step 1: Confirm the Item

Get the item name, physical product, box format, and any constraints (dimensions, contents count, physical features).

### Step 2: Research Real Packaging (The Authenticity Pass)

Before writing a word:
1. Identify what real-world product category this item falls into
2. List the standard packaging elements that category uses
3. Map each element to a comedy format (what real format is it parodying?)
4. Identify the item's institutional world / Brad division
5. Note Brad's title for this division
6. Check which existing kit surfaces already use Kevin, Gary, Diane -- track density

Present the complete component inventory to the user for confirmation before generating copy.

### Step 3: Generate Variations

For each element, generate **5-10 variations**. For each variation:
- **Number every entry** (e.g., SUB-1, SUB-2, TAG-1, TAG-2, FB-1, FB-2)
- **Add a brief note** explaining the comedy mechanic -- what the line is doing and why it's supposed to be funny
- **Vary the angle** -- don't repeat the same structural trick. If three options all use the same word trick, you need different approaches.
- **Run the checklist** (Section 1.8) before presenting. Kill anything that doesn't pass.

### Step 4: User Reviews

The user responds with feedback referencing the IDs:
- "SUB-3 -- lock this one"
- "TAG-1 -- has potential but the last line needs work"
- "FB-2, FB-5 -- keep these, reject the rest"
- "Badge sub-text -- none of these work because [reason]"

### Step 5: Refine and Regenerate

Based on feedback:
- **Lock** entries the user loves (done)
- **Keep** entries with potential (may refine later)
- **Revise** entries with specific problems identified
- **Regenerate** entire categories where nothing worked -- use the user's WHY to change approach
- **Drop** anything explicitly rejected

### Step 6: Repeat

Continue generate > review > refine until the user is satisfied. Multiple rounds per element is normal and expected.

### Step 7: Compile Boss Review Document

When ready, compile all LOCKED and KEPT material into a shareable document organized by surface, with status tags (LOCKED / KEEP / MAYBE), numbered entries, and specific questions for the boss.

## 5.2 SESSION RULES

1. **Number everything.** Every variation gets an ID. The user should never have to describe a line to reference it.
2. **Explain why something is funny.** Brief mechanic notes help the user give targeted feedback.
3. **When a category fails, understand WHY before regenerating.** Don't write more lines in the same vein. The rejection feedback tells you what's missing.
4. **Physical product constraints matter.** No cap = no cap jokes. Blister card = no side panels. Ask early.
5. **Each item has its own premise.** Don't recycle comedy from other items. Stay in the item's lane.
6. **Don't get precious.** The user will reject 70-80%. That's the process working. Volume creates quality.
7. **Save the working doc.** Create or update a working document at `product/copy/box-copy/[item-name]-working-doc.md`.
8. **Save the box copy doc.** The final surface-by-surface copy goes in `product/copy/box-copy/[item-name]-box-copy.md`.
