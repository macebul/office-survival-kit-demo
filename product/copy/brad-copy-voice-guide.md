# Brad Sterling — Copy Voice Guide
## For Writing All Product Packaging & Interior Elements

> **Updated April 2026 — Brad Sterling is sole voice. OG character removed.**

---

## THE TWO BRADS

Brad operates in two distinct modes, and the comedy comes from the fact that they exist on the same product, written by the same person, with zero self-awareness about the gap between them.

### Brad Mode 1: Product Copy Brad
**When Brad is writing about the office, the product, the jokes**

This Brad is actually competent. He knows his audience. He can write a sardonic Nutrition Facts label, a deadpan IT Asset Tag, a perfectly absurd Care & Storage warning. This is Brad the marketer — he understands what's funny about office life, and he packages it well. This is why Elevate Co. exists at all. The product works.

**Voice:** Dry, institutional, straight-faced. The humor comes from the format (official labels, FDA layouts, QC stickers) played totally straight while the content escalates into absurdity. Brad executes this tone competently.

**Examples (from existing exterior copy):**
- "PROPERTY OF: NOBODY ANYMORE"
- "Serving Size: 1 Monday / Servings Per Container: Too Many"
- "DO NOT REMOVE UNDER PENALTY OF MONDAY"
- "Not a significant source of work-life balance, upward mobility, or the feeling that any of this matters."

**Why it works:** The reader laughs WITH the copy. It's genuinely funny. Brad knows how to sell a gag gift.

---

### Brad Mode 2: Personal Brand Brad
**When Brad is writing about Brad**

This Brad is a disaster. The moment the subject becomes himself — his story, his vision, his credentials, his photo — every ounce of self-awareness evaporates. He becomes a hustle-culture LinkedIn post given physical form. He is completely, unshakably sincere. He never winks. He thinks this is what leadership looks like.

**Voice:** Aspirational, self-mythologizing, motivational-speaker cadence. Sentences that sound like they belong in a keynote address at a conference with 30 attendees. Vocabulary borrowed from startup culture, TED Talks, and lifestyle brand copy. Everything is "premium," "vision," "disrupt," "elevate."

**Examples:**
- "He didn't see contraband. He saw potential."
- "Premium workplace tools, premium packaging, and a premium price point."
- "Three-time nominee for the International Workplace Innovation Awards and an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself."
- "A Brad Sterling Experience™"

**Why it works:** The reader laughs AT Brad. The comedy is that this man wrote perfectly good Nutrition Facts jokes on one surface and this delusional founder bio on another, and he sees absolutely no contradiction.

---

## THE GAP IS THE JOKE

The single funniest thing about the product is that both Brads are on the same packaging. The reader encounters competent product humor AND unhinged self-promotion, realizes the same person created both, and that realization is its own punchline.

This means: **don't sabotage the product copy to make Brad look dumber.** The Nutrition Facts label should be genuinely great. The IT Asset Tag should land. The sardonic institutional voice should work. Because the BETTER the product copy is, the MORE absurd Brad's founder bio becomes by contrast. If everything were bad, Brad would just be incompetent. The fact that he's good at the job and terrible at self-awareness is what makes him a real (and funnier) character.

## BRAD'S COPY VOICE: PRACTICAL REFERENCE

### Product Copy (Mode 1) — How to Write It

**Format is the straight man. Content is the comedian.**
Take a real format (nutrition label, asset tag, QC sticker, care instructions, evidence tag) and fill it with escalating office absurdity. The format never breaks. The content gets progressively more unhinged.

**Escalation structure:**
1. First line reads as completely plausible
2. Second line has a slight edge
3. Third line is clearly a joke but the format stays serious
4. Final line is full absurd but still formatted perfectly

**Example — IT Asset Tag:**
> ASSET TAG: #000001 *(plausible)*
> PROPERTY OF: NOBODY ANYMORE *(edge)*
> DEPT: N/A *(dry)*
> STATUS: DEPLOYED *(still plausible)*
> DO NOT REMOVE UNDER PENALTY OF MONDAY *(full absurd, format intact)*

**Word choice:**
- Institutional vocabulary: "deployed," "authorized," "issued," "designated"
- Bureaucratic precision: case numbers, dates, serial numbers, item codes
- Office-specific pain points named with clinical detachment
- "Kevin" as a recurring proper noun treated as a known hazard

**What to avoid:**
- Puns (too cute — Brad's product copy is dry, not clever)
- Exclamation points (the format is always flat and institutional)
- Breaking the format (the form never acknowledges it's a joke)
- Winking at the reader (the straight face IS the technique)

---

### Personal Brand Copy (Mode 2) — How to Write It

**Brad is writing his own legend and he believes every word.**

The key is sincerity. Brad is not trying to be funny. Brad is trying to be inspiring. Everything he writes about himself is what he genuinely believes a visionary founder sounds like, informed by every LinkedIn thought-leader post, every startup origin story, every Gary Vee clip he's ever absorbed.

**Sentence structures Brad loves:**
- Origin myth framing: "When [Brad] discovered [thing], he didn't see [obvious interpretation]. He saw [grandiose reinterpretation]."
- Pivot-as-epiphany: "That's when everything changed."
- The false-humble brag: "Brad is an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself." (Brad wrote this line. Brad thinks this is impressive.)
- Premium stacking: Three adjectives minimum. "Premium workplace tools, premium packaging, and a premium price point." Brad genuinely thinks saying "premium" three times makes something premium.
- The credential drop: Awards that sound almost real. Organizations that might exist. Titles that are definitely self-given.

**Word choice:**
- "Vision," "disrupt," "elevate," "curate," "experience"
- "Whitespace," "thought leader," "brand," "premium"
- "Professional Edition" (Brad thinks adding this to anything makes it luxury)
- His own name (Brad refers to Brad in the third person in marketing copy)

**What to avoid:**
- Making Brad aware of the joke (he never is)
- Having Brad be mean or cynical (he's earnest, not sarcastic — his product copy is sarcastic, but HIS BRAND is dead serious)
- Going too far into parody (Brad should feel like a real person you've met, not a cartoon)

---

### The Credentials Escalation Pattern

Brad's credentials are a comedy goldmine. They follow a specific pattern: they start almost-credible and slide into obviously self-generated, but Brad presents them all with the same level of pride.

**The pattern:**
1. Real-sounding title: "Founder & CEO, Elevate Co." *(Sure, okay)*
2. Almost-real award: "Three-time nominee for the International Workplace Innovation Awards" *(Hmm... is that real?)*
3. Self-undermining detail played straight: "...and an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself" *(There it is)*

**More credential-style lines to pull from:**
- "Featured in [publication that might be his own blog]"
- "Host of [podcast name], a top voice in workplace disruption" (11 listeners, 9 are bots)
- "Named one of [city]'s Most Innovative Founders Under 50 by [award he created]"
- "Author of the forthcoming [book title that will never be written]"
- "Brad has been described as 'relentless' — Brad Sterling, describing himself"

## WRITING BRAD'S "ABOUT THE FOUNDER" PANEL

This is the single most important Brad element. It's where both Brads collide — the guy who made a pretty good gag gift AND the guy who thinks he's a visionary. Guidelines:

### Structure
1. **Origin myth** (2-3 sentences): How Brad created the kit. Frame it as Brad's epiphany moment. He uses language that makes a simple idea sound like innovation.
2. **Vision statement** (1-2 sentences): What Brad built from it. Premium, premium, premium.
3. **Credentials** (1-2 sentences): The escalating-absurdity credential stack. Starts almost-real, ends obviously self-generated.
4. **Optional tagline or sign-off**: Something that makes it clear this is a Brad Sterling Production.

### Photo Direction
- Brad art-directed this photo himself
- Big cheesy teeth-too-white smile — this is his "I'm winning you over" face
- Purple satin shirt, oversized gold rope chain (comically large — think 80s rapper), aviators, pinky ring
- The lighting, framing, and styling are what Brad genuinely thinks looks powerful
- Gold frame or premium border treatment — Brad chose this

### Tone
100% sincere. Zero irony. Brad is proud of this panel. He thinks it elevates the product. He showed it to people before printing and they all said "wow, Brad" and he took that as a compliment.

## THE COMEDY HIERARCHY

When writing any piece of exterior copy, the comedy should come from these sources, in this order:

1. **The format/content contrast** — official format, absurd content (this is the backbone)
2. **Brad's self-image vs. reality** — most concentrated in the founder panel but present in any "premium" branding language
3. **The cumulative effect** — by the time the reader has seen all the elements, the density of institutional parody labels on a matte black briefcase with a duct tape handle is itself the joke. It's too much. It's perfect. It's Brad.

## RECURRING CHARACTERS

Three characters exist in the world of the kit alongside Brad Sterling. None of them have a voice — they are referenced, never heard. They exist the way real workplace figures exist: as a name that triggers a shared understanding.

---

### Kevin

**Who he is:** The coworker everyone has. Kevin is a known hazard. He uses other people's things. He violates workspace boundaries. He does not mean harm — he has no concept of personal property or professional norms. Kevin is not a villain. Kevin is a force of nature. Every office has a Kevin. The reader already knows their Kevin. The name does the work.

**Target density:** 4-6 references across the full kit (packaging, card decks, document sets, item boxes, hidden surfaces).

**How to use Kevin:**
- Reference him by first name, casually, as if everyone knows who he is
- Never introduce or explain Kevin. He is pre-existing. He is assumed knowledge.
- Kevin is a reason things are the way they are. He is why you need the kit.
- He can appear in warnings, care instructions, institutional labels, card copy, and document sets
- Kevin is always referenced in the third person. Kevin never speaks.

**Usage examples:**

In an item box care instruction:
> Do not leave unattended in shared spaces. Kevin has been talked to. It didn't help.

On a sticker (Turf Tags):
> KEVIN-PROOF ZONE

On a tray surface tagline:
> Replacement unit. Previous issue lost to Kevin.

In a card deck (Sweet Nothings passive-aggressive card):
> I noticed my [item] migrated to your desk. I'm sure it walked there on its own.

In Doc Set 2 (Activity Guide) bingo card square:
> Kevin borrowed something and "forgot"

In a Care & Storage label:
> Store in a secure location. Not the break room. Kevin has access to the break room.

---

### Gary

**Who he is:** Quality inspector. Brad's guy. Gary approves things Brad tells him to approve. Gary exists only on QC stamps, inspection badges, and compliance stickers. He never appears in prose. He never speaks. He is a bureaucratic ghost — a name on a rubber stamp that implies an entire quality control department that consists of one person who may or may not exist. Gary is Brad's attempt to manufacture institutional credibility.

**Target density:** 2-3 references across the kit.

**How to use Gary:**
- Gary appears ONLY on stamps, badges, stickers, and inspection marks — never in sentences or narrative copy
- His format is always institutional: "INSPECTED BY: GARY", "QC APPROVED — G. [ILLEGIBLE]", "GARY — DEPT. OF QUALITY ASSURANCE"
- The comedy is in the repetition and the fact that the same single name appears on every quality checkpoint
- Gary's last name is never fully legible. His employee number changes between appearances (or doesn't exist).

**Usage examples:**

On the briefcase exterior (already exists):
> INSPECTED BY: GARY

On an item box (any item):
> QC PASS — G. [ILLEGIBLE] — DEPT. OF QUALITY

Inside the mug compartment or on the compartment cover:
> FINAL INSPECTION: APPROVED
> GARY / BADGE #[SMUDGED]

On Doc Set 1 or a card box flap:
> This product meets Elevate Co. quality standards as verified by our independent quality assurance department.
> [Small stamp:] GARY

---

### Diane

**Who she is:** HR. The unseen authority. Diane is never named directly in copy — she is implied. She exists in the negative space of warnings, disclaimers, and policy references. When copy says "per Section 4 of the employee handbook," that is Diane's domain. When a disclaimer says "this product has been reviewed and approved by the appropriate department," Diane is the appropriate department. She is the reason certain things are phrased carefully. She is the reason there are disclaimers at all.

**Target density:** 1-2 references across the kit. Less is more. Diane's presence should be felt, not seen.

**How to use Diane:**
- Diane is NEVER named. She is referenced obliquely through policy language, handbook citations, and HR-flavored disclaimers.
- Her existence is implied by the bureaucratic caution in certain copy — the places where the tone shifts from Brad's confident voice to something slightly more careful, as if legal reviewed it
- The comedy is that even Brad — who named himself Chief Vision Officer and trademarked everything — defers to some unseen authority in certain contexts

**Usage examples:**

In a disclaimer on a card deck box:
> Elevate Co. assumes no liability for interpersonal outcomes resulting from card deployment. Usage subject to applicable workplace conduct policies (ref: Employee Handbook, Section 7.3).

On the belly band or outer box fine print:
> This product has been reviewed by the appropriate department and is approved for distribution within guidelines established by company policy.

In Doc Set 2 (Activity Guide), small footnote:
> * Some activities may require prior approval. Consult your department handbook before initiating any territory-marking protocols.

---

### Character Interaction Rules

1. **Kevin and Gary never interact.** Kevin is a hazard the copy warns about. Gary is a stamp that appears on things. They exist in different layers of the product.

2. **Diane and Brad never directly conflict.** Brad does not complain about HR. He simply... defers, in certain carefully worded passages, to unnamed authority. The reader infers the rest.

3. **Only Kevin is named in prose.** Gary exists on stamps only. Diane exists in policy references only. Kevin is the only character the copy addresses by name in running text.

4. **None of these characters undermine Brad.** Brad created this product. Kevin is a reason the product exists. Gary validates it. Diane's implied presence makes it feel like a real company made it. They all serve Brad's world, even when they make it funnier.

5. **Density discipline matters.** Kevin at 4-6, Gary at 2-3, Diane at 1-2. If every surface mentions Kevin, he stops being a recurring joke and becomes a bit. Scarcity is what makes each reference land.
