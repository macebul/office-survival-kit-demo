## VERDICT CALLOUT

*Magazine editorial callout box — the verdict. Styled as a bold pull-out element with high visual weight. The magazine declares a winner with total conviction despite the evidence pointing the other direction.*

### ELEVATE'S PICK: YOUR CUBICLE

Look, we're not going to pretend this is close. Yes, the cell offers more square footage. Yes, it has a window. Yes, there are enforced quiet hours and a private bathroom and what appears to be a genuinely comfortable reading nook. We see all of that.

But the cubicle has something the cell never will: *community.* Those five-foot walls aren't a design flaw — they're an invitation. You can hear your neighbor's phone calls. You can smell their lunch. You can make eye contact with someone fourteen desks away who is also wondering what happened to their afternoon. That's not a workspace. That's a neighborhood.

The extra square foot? Keep it. We'll take the one with a shared printer and a chair that sort of reclines if you lean back hard enough.

## Copy Notes

### Voice Calibration

This spread is **magazine editorial voice** — enthusiastic, positive, persuasive. The magazine has done legitimate research and is presenting a property comparison with genuine conviction. The humor comes from the magazine's unshakable belief that the cubicle is the better option despite it losing on nearly every metric.

The verdict callout is where the comedy engine runs hardest. The magazine acknowledges the cell's advantages (window, privacy, quiet hours, more space) and then dismisses them in favor of "community" — which is just a euphemism for having no walls and no privacy.

### Comedy Beats (in order of appearance)

1. **"Choose Your 6×8"** — Framing it as a genuine choice, and the headline itself says 6×8 (the cubicle dimension), already tipping the scales
2. **"One Square Foot"** — The difference between the two properties is one square foot. Played as if this is a meaningful distinction
3. **Specs table** — Every row the cell wins, the cubicle column reframes the loss. "Open concept" = no walls. "Adjacent" = nowhere near. "Yes" = the noise level answer
4. **"Panoramic neighbor views"** — Real estate language for "everyone can see you and you can see everyone"
5. **"At-will (theirs, not yours)"** — Tenure comparison kicker
6. **"Someone just left"** — Availability is immediate because of turnover
7. **Verdict acknowledges the cell wins** — The magazine lists every advantage the cell has, then dismisses all of them
8. **"Community"** — The reframe. No privacy becomes "an invitation"
9. **"Smell their lunch"** — The escalation within the community argument
10. **"A chair that sort of reclines if you lean back hard enough"** — The cubicle's closing argument
11. **Brad quote** — "Square footage is a mindset" is peak Mode 2 Brad. The armrest-less chair detail grounds it
12. **DID YOU KNOW** — Data as punchline. The magazine presents damning information with zero editorial comment
13. **Kicker line** — The original 6×8 / 6×9 comparison that started everything

### Decisions Made

- **Headline:** "CHOOSE YOUR 6×8" — uses the cubicle dimension in the headline, which subtly biases the comparison before the reader even reads the specs. The cell is 6×9. The headline has already chosen sides.
- **Brad's title:** "Space Optimization Consultant" — a self-given credential that sounds like something a WeWork pitch deck would include.
- **"Panoramic neighbor views"** added to the specs table as a Privacy row — this wasn't in the handoff doc's spec list but it's too good a real estate reframe to skip.
- **The thermostat war line** in the editorial body — grounded in a specific, relatable detail (second Obama administration) rather than a generic "it's always cold."
- **DID YOU KNOW retained from handoff doc** — this line was originally on page 6 in v1. It fits perfectly on this spread and may not need to appear elsewhere.
