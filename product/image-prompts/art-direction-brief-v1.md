# Art Direction Brief — Doc Set 1 Booklet (Photorealistic)

**Version:** 1.0
**Created:** May 15, 2026
**Status:** Active — replaces mid-century modern direction (archived at `archive/mid-century-art-direction/`)
**Applies to:** All Doc Set 1 booklet images (pages 1-16+). Does NOT supersede image prompts for hero photos, packaging surfaces, or product shots — those are separate documents.

---

## 1. The Core Idea

**Corporate stock photography played completely straight, with absurdity buried in the details.**

Every image should look like it was pulled from a real corporate training manual, onboarding deck, or stock photography library. The lighting is clean. The people are real. The office environment is believable. The viewer's first instinct should be: "This looks like something from my company's intranet."

Then they look closer.

The comedy lives in the gap between the format (authoritative, institutional, photorealistic) and the content (escalating absurdity treated as completely normal by everyone in the frame). This mirrors the booklet's copy rule: **format stays buttoned-up, content gets unleashed.**

A photorealistic image of someone in a hazmat suit responding to a ballpoint pen is funnier than a cartoon of the same scene because the viewer's brain processes it as plausible for a split second before the joke lands. That split second is where the comedy lives.

---

## 2. Photography Genre

### Default: Corporate Stock Photography

The baseline look for most images in the booklet.

**What this means:**
- Clean, even, slightly flat lighting — the kind of light in every corporate office photo ever taken
- Real office environments: drop ceilings, fluorescent panels, cubicle partitions, glass-walled conference rooms, break room bulletin boards, elevator lobbies
- People in business casual or business formal attire, looking like actual employees
- Compositions that feel slightly posed but natural — the "we hired a photographer for the company website" aesthetic
- Color palette driven by the environment (beige walls, gray carpet, blue-gray cubicle fabric, warm wood conference tables) rather than imposed from outside
- Skin tones, hair, clothing, and lighting should all read as photographically real — no stylization, no filters that flatten people into illustrations

**What this is NOT:**
- Not Instagram-filtered or color-graded
- Not fashion photography (no dramatic shadows, no beauty lighting)
- Not photojournalism (not gritty, not handheld-feeling)
- Not AI-generated-looking (no waxy skin, no uncanny smoothness, no hyperreal detail that screams "this is synthetic")

**Reference touchstones (conceptual, not literal):**
- Getty Images "business" and "office" categories — the platonic ideal of corporate stock
- Corporate training manual photography from the 2000s-2010s
- The kind of photos that appear in employee handbooks, safety manuals, and HR onboarding decks
- WeWork marketing photography (modern, clean, slightly aspirational but generic)

### Elevation: Brad's Cinematic Lens

When Brad is the hero of a scene — when a spread is about Brad's self-image, Brad in action, or Brad at his most delusional — the photography subtly shifts toward **cinematic/film-still** quality.

**What changes:**
- Slightly more dramatic lighting (key light + fill, visible depth, maybe a rim light)
- Shallow depth of field (background softens, Brad stays sharp)
- More intentional composition (rule of thirds, leading lines, negative space used for drama)
- Color temperature might warm slightly — Brad sees himself in golden light
- The overall feeling is: this image was directed, not just captured

**What stays the same:**
- Still photorealistic — real people, real environments
- Brad's expression and behavior are still played straight
- The absurdity level doesn't increase — only the production value of how it's shot
- No overt stylization (no Wes Anderson color palettes, no film grain for its own sake)

**Why this matters:** The visual quality gap between "how the office looks" and "how Brad sees himself" is a comedy layer. Everyone else gets stock photography. Brad gets a cinematographer. He doesn't know why — it's just how the world looks when he's in it.

**Usage:** Reserve cinematic elevation for 2-3 spreads maximum. The cover, and 1-2 Brad hero moments. If overused, it stops being a contrast and becomes the norm.

## 3. The Absurdity Model

### Layer 1: First Glance (Plausible)

The main subject and overall composition read as a normal corporate photo. Someone scrolling past should think "office stock photo" for 1-2 seconds.

**Examples:**
- A woman presenting at a whiteboard → (normal)
- A man at his desk working on his computer → (normal)
- Employees lined up at a security checkpoint → (normal)
- Someone adjusting their badge in a mirror → (normal)

### Layer 2: Second Glance (Something's Off)

The primary action or subject has something slightly wrong — played completely straight by everyone in the frame. This is the main joke. The viewer catches it on second look.

**Examples:**
- The whiteboard presentation has a flow chart titled "ESCAPE ROUTES — Q3 REVIEW"
- The man at his desk is wearing a full hazmat suit. His coffee is still there. His posture is completely normal.
- The security checkpoint has an "APPROVED" sign and someone is placing a medieval helmet on the conveyor belt like it's a laptop bag
- The badge in the mirror reads "INMATE 0847" and the person doesn't seem to notice

### Layer 3: Deep Read (The Background Jokes)

For the viewer who really studies the image. Small details in the background, on surfaces, in the margins. These are the details that make the image shareable — the things people screenshot and circle.

**Examples:**
- A motivational poster in the background reads "SYNERGY: Two People Can Move a Body Faster"
- Someone's desk nameplate says "CELL 14B"
- A sticky note on a monitor reads "Day 847"
- The fire extinguisher case has a label that says "IN CASE OF ALL-HANDS MEETING"
- A coffee mug reads "World's Most Adequate"
- A wall clock has "WHY" where "12" should be
- Exit signs replaced with "PAROLE HEARING — ROOM 4C"

### Density Target

Every image should have:
- 1 primary joke (Layer 2) — visible within 3 seconds
- 2-4 background jokes (Layer 3) — visible on close inspection
- 0 Layer 1 violations — the overall scene must read as photographically real and environmentally plausible

### The Cardinal Rule

**Nobody in the frame reacts to the absurdity.**

Every person in every image treats everything as completely normal. No mugging, no winking, no "can you believe this?" expressions. The security guard watching someone put a katana through the X-ray machine looks bored. The hazmat team responding to a pen looks professional and focused. The woman walking past a ransom note on the conference room wall looks mildly inconvenienced, not terrified.

This mirrors the world rule from the copy: "The office is a prison. This is not a metaphor." In these images, the absurdity is not a metaphor. It's Tuesday.

## 4. Environment Design

### The Office

Every image takes place in (or references) the same generic corporate office environment. This should feel like a real place — not a set, not a concept, not a collage.

**Consistent environmental elements:**
- **Lighting:** Overhead fluorescent panel lighting. Occasionally supplemented by desk lamps or window light. Always flat, always institutional.
- **Walls:** Beige, off-white, or light gray. No accent walls. No personality.
- **Flooring:** Gray commercial carpet tile or beige linoleum.
- **Furniture:** Standard-issue: gray/blue fabric cubicle panels, light wood or laminate conference tables, black mesh office chairs, metal filing cabinets.
- **Ceiling:** Drop ceiling with acoustic tiles and recessed fluorescent panels. This ceiling is in every office in America.
- **Glass:** Conference rooms have glass walls. Interior windows exist. Fish-bowl visibility is part of the environment.
- **Details:** Whiteboards (some written on), corkboard bulletin boards, fire extinguisher cases, exit signs, badge readers, security cameras, water coolers, vending machines.

**What the office is NOT:**
- Not a tech startup (no ping pong tables, no exposed brick, no neon signs)
- Not a luxury firm (no dark wood, no leather, no dramatic architecture)
- Not a warehouse or industrial space
- Not a home office
- Not aspirational — this is the office where people serve time

### Lighting Philosophy

The default office lighting should feel slightly unflattering — the way real fluorescent lighting actually looks. Not ugly, but not glamorous. The light source should feel like "ceiling panels" in most shots.

Exceptions:
- Brad hero shots: warmer, more directional
- Dramatic moments (the riot, the trunk reveal): slightly moodier, but still within the range of "what office lighting actually does"

## 5. Character Design

### Realism Standard

All characters should look like real people you'd see in an actual office. No model-beautiful faces. No exaggerated features. No costumes (unless the absurdity calls for one). People should look like they commute, eat lunch at their desk, and have opinions about the thermostat.

**Diversity:** The cast across the full booklet should reflect a real American corporate office — diverse in race, age, gender, and body type. Not performatively diverse (not every image a perfect rainbow), but naturally representative. Specific casting per character per spread.

**Wardrobe:**
- Business casual is the default: button-downs, blouses, slacks, skirts, blazers
- Some variation by role (security guard in uniform, facilities in polo, etc.)
- Nobody is dressed perfectly — a rolled sleeve, a slightly loose tie, a badge that's crooked
- The "lived-in" quality of real workwear, not the "just unwrapped" quality of stock photography wardrobe

**Expressions:**
- **Default state:** Slightly tired, neutral, the face of someone who's been here a while. Not miserable — just... present.
- **Nobody mugs.** No exaggerated expressions. The funniest face in any image is the one that treats the absurd situation as completely unremarkable.
- **Boredom is the baseline.** People in these images have seen it all. A hazmat team in the break room? Sure. Someone rappelling from the ceiling? Let them work.

### Brad Sterling

Brad is the only character who gets a locked visual identity in this first phase. Other recurring characters (Kevin, Gary, Diane) will get their own character reference documents later.

**Brad's new photorealistic look — to be developed:**
The mid-century version had purple satin shirt, oversized gold rope chain, aviator sunglasses, gold signet pinky ring. The ENERGY of this needs to translate to photorealistic, but the specific wardrobe may need adjustment to read as "real person who dresses like this" rather than "costume."

**Brad's visual identity brief (for character reference doc development):**
- Late 30s white male
- Dresses like he thinks a "founder" dresses — slightly too much, slightly too polished, slightly too intentional for the environment he's in
- His clothes are nicer than everyone else's but in a way that reveals effort, not taste
- He is the only person in any image who looks like he chose his outfit today. Everyone else just got dressed.
- He is never self-conscious. He is never in on the joke. He is always sincere.
- When he appears in the stock-photo default scenes, he stands out — not because of a costume, but because he's the only person who looks like he's performing
- When he appears in cinematic-elevation scenes, he looks like the protagonist of a movie only he knows is being filmed

**Brad's character reference doc is a separate deliverable.** It will include: locked wardrobe, hair, accessories, build, posture, expression range, reference images, and generator-specific consistency instructions. This brief provides the creative direction; the character doc locks the execution.

### Character Consistency Within Spreads

When the same character appears in multiple images on the same spread (e.g., Gary on pages 6-7), they should be recognizably the same person — same face, same build, same wardrobe, same hair. Cross-spread consistency is ideal but not mandatory for non-Brad characters in this phase.

## 6. Composition & Framing

### Default Compositions

Most vignettes are small (~2" x 2" at print size) within a page that also carries copy. Images need to read clearly at small scale.

**Principles:**
- **Character-forward:** In most frames, one or two people are the primary subject. They should fill at least 50-60% of the frame.
- **Clear focal point:** The viewer should know where to look immediately. The primary joke (Layer 2) should be in the visual center of attention.
- **Background jokes in the periphery:** Layer 3 details should live in the margins, on walls, on surfaces — places the eye travels to after absorbing the main subject.
- **Generous negative space where needed:** Some compositions should breathe. Not every frame needs to be packed.
- **No collage compositions:** Each vignette is a single photographic frame, not a composite. One camera angle, one moment in time.

### Aspect Ratios

- **Standard vignette:** 1:1 (square) — matches existing generator format
- **Wide scene (rare):** 16:9 or 5:4 — for establishing shots or panoramic scenes
- **Full-page or spread images (rare):** 4:5 or 3:4 portrait — for high-impact moments

### Text in Images

Some spreads call for readable text within the image (signs, nameplates, banners, forms, posters).

**Rules:**
- Text should look like it belongs in the environment — printed on a sign, written on a whiteboard, displayed on a screen, stamped on a form
- Short, all-caps phrases render most reliably: "APPROVED," "INMATE 0847," "MR C SHANK"
- Complex text (full sentences, small print, handwriting) should be added in post-production via InDesign, not baked into the generated image
- When in-image text is critical to the joke, the prompt must specify it explicitly and the post-production plan must include a fallback for patching if the generator garbles it

## 7. Color & Tone

### Office Palette (Environmental)

The office itself drives the color story. These are not imposed — they're what real offices look like:

- **Walls:** Beige (#D4C5A9), off-white (#F5F0E8), light warm gray (#C8C3BC)
- **Carpet:** Gray (#8B8680), charcoal (#4A4845)
- **Furniture:** Cubicle blue-gray, laminate wood, black mesh, chrome accents
- **Lighting:** Warm fluorescent — slightly yellow-green cast on whites, the way real fluorescents read on camera
- **Accents:** The occasional pop of color from a personal item — a red stapler, a blue coffee mug, a green plant — but never dominant

### Skin Tones

Skin tones should be photographically accurate. No stylization, no unified palette, no "house style" applied to skin. Each character's skin should look the way it would under the office's lighting conditions.

### Brad's Color Shift

In cinematic-elevation shots, the palette warms slightly:
- Whites become creamier
- Light takes on a golden quality
- Shadows are richer but not dramatic
- Brad's wardrobe provides the accent color (whatever his locked palette turns out to be)

This shift should be subtle — noticeable if you compare two images side by side, but not jarring within the flow of the booklet.

## 8. What to Avoid

### Visual Anti-Patterns

| Avoid | Why |
|-------|-----|
| Cartoon or illustration style | The entire direction pivot. Photorealistic only. |
| AI-generated "uncanny valley" look | Waxy skin, too-smooth faces, symmetrical-to-a-fault features. Push for imperfection. |
| Dramatic/moody lighting as default | Breaks the stock-photo container. Reserved for Brad hero moments only. |
| Color grading or filters | No Instagram looks, no teal-and-orange, no desaturation-for-mood. Real office lighting. |
| Collage or composite layouts | Each image is one photographic moment. No split screens, no floating elements. |
| Characters reacting to the absurdity | The cardinal rule. Nobody mugs. Nobody winks. Nobody breaks. |
| Overcrowded compositions | Images print small. Clarity at 2" x 2" is mandatory. |
| Explicit violence or cruelty | The product is a comedy gift. Prison framing stays in the metaphor — no actual harm, no blood, no real threat. The darkest it gets is the trunk gag (Gary's legs sticking out), which works because it's obviously slapstick in a photorealistic frame. |
| Real brand logos or identifiable IP | No Nike, no Apple, no recognizable brands. Generic everything. The only brand is Elevate Co. |

### Generator-Specific Pitfalls

These will be detailed in the Image Prompt Specification document, but the brief-level concerns are:
- Hands and fingers: a known weak point in AI image generation. Compositions should minimize reliance on detailed hand poses.
- Text rendering: short all-caps text in standard signage/nameplate positions is reliable. Long sentences, cursive, or small print should go to post-production.
- Character consistency: generating the same person across multiple images is challenging. Brad's character reference doc will include consistency techniques specific to Nano Banana 2.
- Crowd scenes: more than 4-5 people in a frame increases the risk of artifacts. Keep scenes focused.

## 9. The Comedy Test

Every image in the booklet should pass this checklist before it's approved:

- [ ] **Stock photo first impression** — Would someone scrolling past think "corporate stock photo" for at least 1 second?
- [ ] **Primary joke lands** — Is the Layer 2 absurdity visible within 3 seconds?
- [ ] **Background rewards** — Are there 2+ Layer 3 details that reward close inspection?
- [ ] **Nobody breaks** — Does every character in the frame treat the scene as completely normal?
- [ ] **Copy/image complement** — Does the image provide something the copy doesn't? (Not repeating, completing.)
- [ ] **Small-scale readability** — Does the primary joke still read at 2" x 2"?
- [ ] **Shareability** — Would someone screenshot this and send it to a group chat?
- [ ] **Recognition humor** — Does this depict something the viewer has actually experienced (through the prison lens)?

## 11. Summary

The booklet's images should feel like someone raided a corporate stock photo library, but every third image has something deeply wrong with it that nobody seems to have noticed. The realism is the setup. The absurdity is the punchline. The straight faces are the delivery.

Brad is the only person who gets good lighting.
