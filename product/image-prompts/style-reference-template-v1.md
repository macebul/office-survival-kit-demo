**Version:** 1.0
**Created:** May 19, 2026
**Author:** Style reference built per JL SOP ("How to Build a Style Reference Template," 02/01/26)
**Companion to:** `art-direction-brief-v1.md` (what to make) + `image-prompt-spec-v1.md` (technical format)
**Purpose:** This is the "how should it feel?" document. It gives AI visual direction on the specific aesthetic we want — lighting, composition, color, mood, setting — so it stops defaulting to generic photorealistic output.

## Overall Aesthetic Direction (Thesis Statement)

**We are making images that look like they were pulled from a real corporate employee handbook, onboarding deck, or internal training manual — except every third image has something deeply wrong with it that nobody seems to have noticed.**

The photographs should feel like they were taken by someone from the Facilities department with a decent camera and a modest budget. Not a professional photographer. Not a stock photo agency. The person who takes the headshots for the company directory and photographs the holiday party for the newsletter.

The visual register is "Internal Corporate Communications Photography" — a specific sub-genre of stock photography that appears in employee handbooks, HR compliance slides, safety posters, and company intranet banners. It is the most deadpan photographic language that exists, and it is immediately recognizable to every office worker alive.

**The comedy engine:** Photography cannot be ironic. A photograph simply shows what is in front of the lens. When the content is absurd and the photograph treats it as mundane, the comedy writes itself. The viewer discovers the joke; it is not delivered to them.

**Reference touchstones:**
- Despair Inc. Demotivators (despair.com) — beautiful stock photography in motivational poster format with devastating captions. The image never signals "comedy."
- The Onion's photography department — their art director's rule: "We always try to lean towards reality." They make "delicate portraits of absurdity."
- Every employee handbook you've ever been handed on your first day.

## DIMENSION 1: LIGHTING

### The Look We Want

**Overhead fluorescent panel lighting. Flat, even, slightly unflattering. The light that exists in every American corporate office.**

This is the single most important dimension. Lighting determines whether an image reads as "stock photography" or "art photography." We want stock photography lighting — the kind that makes everyone look the same amount of okay.

### Specific Characteristics

- **Source:** Overhead recessed fluorescent panels (2x4 troffer lights in a drop ceiling). This is not negotiable for default scenes.
- **Quality:** Flat and diffused. Minimal shadows. No dramatic falloff. The kind of light that eliminates depth and makes everything look equally important (and equally unimportant).
- **Color temperature:** Warm fluorescent, approximately 3500-4200K. Slightly yellow-green cast on whites — the way real fluorescents read on camera. NOT pure white. NOT warm golden. NOT cool blue.
- **Shadow behavior:** Soft, minimal shadows directly beneath subjects. No hard directional shadows. No rim lighting. No backlighting. The light comes from everywhere above and nowhere specific.
- **Skin rendering:** Skin should look the way it looks under office fluorescents — slightly flat, slightly washed, realistic. Not glamorous. Not ugly. Just... office.

### What This Is NOT

- Not studio lighting (too controlled, too dramatic)
- Not window/natural light (too warm, too directional, too flattering)
- Not moody/cinematic (reserved ONLY for Brad hero moments, max 2-3 per booklet)
- Not ring light / beauty light (too even, too flattering, reads as selfie or YouTube)
- Not dramatic side lighting (reads as film noir or portrait photography)

### The Exception: Brad's Cinematic Lens

When Brad is the hero of a scene (his self-image moments), lighting shifts to:
- Slightly warmer color temperature (golden quality)
- More directional key light with subtle fill
- Visible depth through lighting (foreground/background separation)
- The feeling of "someone hired a cinematographer for this one shot"

This should be used for maximum 2-3 images in the entire booklet. The contrast between how the office looks (fluorescent flat) and how Brad sees himself (cinematic warm) is a comedy layer.

### Annotation for AI

When prompting, describe lighting as:
> "overhead fluorescent panel office lighting, flat even illumination from recessed ceiling fixtures, warm fluorescent color temperature with slight yellow-green cast, minimal shadows, the lighting quality of a real corporate office photograph taken for an employee handbook"

## DIMENSION 2: COMPOSITION & FRAMING

### The Look We Want

**Slightly awkward corporate staging. The composition of a photo taken by someone who knows the rule of thirds but doesn't always follow it.**

These should look like the company hired an okay photographer (or deputized someone from Marketing) to take photos for internal materials. The compositions are competent but not artful. Subjects are centered or slightly off-center. There's adequate headroom but not dramatic negative space. It's the visual equivalent of "gets the job done."

### Specific Characteristics

- **Subject placement:** Character-forward. One or two people fill 50-60% of the frame. They are the obvious subject.
- **Camera height:** Eye level or slightly above (the "standing in front of someone" perspective). Not dramatic low angles. Not overhead unless the scene specifically calls for a desk/table surface.
- **Distance:** Medium shot (waist-up) is the default for vignettes at ~2"x2" print size. Close enough to read expressions. Far enough to see environment.
- **Depth of field:** Moderate. Background is slightly soft but not dramatically blurred. The feeling of a decent DSLR on auto, not a cinema lens at f/1.4. Background details (Layer 3 jokes) need to be discoverable — shallow DOF that blurs the background kills the "hidden object" comedy.
- **Framing:** Simple, direct. No dutch angles. No extreme perspectives. No forced symmetry. The camera is where a person would stand to take this photo.
- **Negative space:** Adequate but not generous. These photos are functional, not artistic. They're trying to show a thing, not create a mood.

### What This Is NOT

- Not fashion/editorial composition (too dynamic, too intentional)
- Not architectural photography (too precise, too geometric)
- Not photojournalism (too candid, too handheld-feeling)
- Not social media (too close, too filtered, too personality-driven)

### Small-Scale Readability Rule

Every image prints at approximately 2"x2" in the booklet. At that size:
- The primary subject must be immediately identifiable
- The primary joke (Layer 2 absurdity) must be visible within 3 seconds
- Background jokes (Layer 3) must be discoverable but can require close inspection
- Text in the image must be legible if it's part of the joke

### Annotation for AI

When prompting, describe composition as:
> "medium shot from eye level, the subject centered in frame, the composition of a corporate staff photograph or internal training manual image, competent but not artful framing, moderate depth of field with background details visible but slightly soft"

## DIMENSION 3: COLOR PALETTE & MOOD

### The Look We Want

**The palette of the real American corporate office. Beige, gray, and muted everything — with color coming from the environment, not imposed from outside.**

These images should look like they exist in the world's most average office. The color palette is driven by what that office actually looks like, not by a designer's mood board. The mundanity of the palette is itself part of the comedy — the absurdity happens in a place that looks like it was decorated by committee in 2009.

### The Office Palette (Environmental)

| Element | Color | Hex Reference |
|---------|-------|---------------|
| Walls | Beige / off-white / light warm gray | #D4C5A9 / #F5F0E8 / #C8C3BC |
| Carpet | Gray / charcoal commercial tile | #8B8680 / #4A4845 |
| Cubicle fabric | Blue-gray / muted teal | Standard corporate panel fabric |
| Furniture | Laminate wood, black mesh, chrome | Mixed neutrals |
| Ceiling | White/off-white acoustic tile | Standard drop ceiling |
| Fluorescent cast | Slight yellow-green on whites | The way fluorescents read on camera |

### Color Accents

Color enters the frame through personal items and environmental details — not through lighting or post-production:
- A red stapler on a desk
- A blue coffee mug
- A green desk plant
- Someone's colored blouse or tie
- A bulletin board with colored papers
- Safety signage (yellow caution, red fire extinguisher)

These pops of color should feel incidental, not designed. They're there because someone brought them to work, not because a photographer placed them.

### Skin Tones

Photographically accurate under the office lighting conditions. No stylization. No unified palette. Each character's skin should look the way it would under warm fluorescent lighting — slightly flat, slightly washed, real.

### Emotional Tone / Mood

The overall mood is: **institutional mundanity.** The feeling of being inside a building where the thermostat is set to 72, the carpet was installed during the Clinton administration, and the most exciting thing that happened today was someone brought donuts.

This is NOT:
- Not depressing (the office isn't sad, it's just... there)
- Not aspirational (nobody is having their best day)
- Not warm and cozy (the light is too flat for warmth)
- Not cold and clinical (it's a carpet office, not a hospital)
- Not stylized in any emotional direction

### Brad's Color Shift

In his 2-3 cinematic-elevation moments:
- Whites become creamier
- Light takes on a golden quality
- Shadows are richer
- Brad's wardrobe provides the accent color (purple satin is the strongest chromatic element when he's on screen)

### Annotation for AI

When prompting, describe color as:
> "natural office color palette driven by the environment, beige walls, gray commercial carpet, standard corporate furniture, warm fluorescent lighting with slight yellow-green cast, no color grading, no filters, no imposed color scheme, the image looks like it was taken in a real office with no post-processing"

## DIMENSION 4: BACKGROUND & SETTING

### The Look We Want

**A generic American corporate office that could be in any city, any company, any decade from 2005-2020. The kind of office where 40 million Americans serve their time.**

The office is a character in this booklet. It needs to feel like a real, specific place while also being universally recognizable. Every viewer should think "I've been in this office" or "I work in this office."

### Consistent Environmental Elements

These elements should appear across all images to build the sense of a single, coherent office:

- **Drop ceiling:** White acoustic tiles with recessed fluorescent panels. The ceiling that exists in every American office.
- **Cubicle partitions:** Gray or blue-gray fabric panels, approximately 5 feet tall. The standard-issue variety.
- **Conference rooms:** Glass-walled rooms with laminate tables and mesh chairs. Fish-bowl visibility.
- **Break room:** Bulletin board, water cooler, vending machine, microwave, round table with mismatched chairs.
- **Hallway:** Industrial carpet, beige walls, fire extinguisher case, exit signs, badge readers.
- **Desks:** L-shaped or straight, laminate surface, monitor(s), keyboard, phone, generic office supplies.
- **Personal effects (environmental storytelling):** Family photos in cheap frames, desk plants in varying states of health, coffee mugs with messages, stress balls, fidget items. These should feel accumulated, not arranged.

### What the Office Is NOT

- Not a tech startup (no ping pong tables, exposed brick, neon signs, standing desks, beer fridges)
- Not a luxury firm (no dark wood, leather, dramatic architecture, floor-to-ceiling windows)
- Not a co-working space (no trendy furniture, no instagram-friendly corners)
- Not brand new (the carpet has seen things; the chairs have been sat in by many people)
- Not a home office

### Layer 3 Background Details

The background is where the "hidden object game" lives. These are the details that make images shareable — the things people screenshot and circle:

- Motivational posters with devastating sub-text
- Desk nameplates with prison-coded names
- Sticky notes with day counts
- Signs with double meanings
- Clocks with "WHY" where numbers should be
- Exit signs redirected to "PAROLE HEARING"
- Safety posters with absurd incident descriptions

**Critical rule:** These details must be present but not dominant. The viewer discovers them; the photograph doesn't point at them. The background treats them as completely normal office decoration.

### Annotation for AI

When prompting, describe setting as:
> "standard American corporate office interior, drop ceiling with recessed fluorescent panels, beige walls, gray commercial carpet tile, cubicle partitions in gray-blue fabric, standard laminate desks and black mesh office chairs, the environment of a mid-size company that last renovated during the Obama administration, lived-in and functional, not aspirational"

## DIMENSION 5: PEOPLE, WARDROBE & EXPRESSION

### The Look We Want

**Real people who look like they commute, eat lunch at their desks, and have opinions about the thermostat.**

### People

- Natural human proportions and realistic facial features with natural imperfections
- Diverse casting across the booklet (race, age, gender, body type) — naturally representative, not performatively arranged
- Nobody is model-beautiful. Nobody is ugly. Everyone looks like someone you'd see in an actual office.
- The casting should feel like "HR rounded up employees for the company photo," not "we hired actors for the shoot"

### Wardrobe

- **Default:** Business casual. Button-downs, blouses, slacks, khakis, skirts, blazers. The clothes people actually wear to an office.
- **Lived-in quality:** A rolled sleeve. A slightly loose tie. A badge that's crooked. A blazer that's been worn many times. NOT the "just unwrapped" quality of stock photography wardrobe.
- **Variation by role:** Security guard in uniform. Facilities in a polo. IT in slightly more casual. Management in slightly more formal. But nobody stands out dramatically.
- **Nobody is dressed for a catalog shoot.** These are people who got dressed this morning without thinking about it too hard.

### Expression

This is where the deadpan lives.

- **Default state:** Slightly tired, neutral, present. The face of someone who's been here a while. Not miserable — just... at work.
- **Nobody mugs.** No exaggerated expressions. No wide eyes. No hands-on-cheeks shock faces. The funniest face in any image is the one that treats the absurd situation as completely unremarkable.
- **Boredom is the baseline.** A hazmat team in the break room? Sure. Someone rappelling from the ceiling? Let them work. The security guard watching someone put a katana through the X-ray machine looks like he's seen it before.
- **The cardinal rule applies to expressions:** Nobody in the frame reacts to the absurdity. Period.

### Brad Sterling (The Exception)

Brad is the only character who gets:
- Broader expression range (confident, earnest, presenting, focused, his big showman grin)
- Intentional wardrobe (his purple satin / gold chain / aviators visual DNA)
- Better lighting (cinematic elevation in his hero moments)
- Main-character energy in every frame he occupies

Brad's specific photorealistic look is defined in a separate character reference document. The key principle: he is the only person in any image who looks like he chose his outfit today. Everyone else just got dressed.

### Annotation for AI

When prompting, describe people as:
> "real office workers with natural features and natural imperfections, business casual wardrobe that looks worn and comfortable not new, neutral slightly tired expressions, nobody reacts to anything unusual in the scene, everyone treats the situation as completely normal, the energy of employees posing for a company newsletter photo — cooperative but not enthusiastic"

## DIMENSION 6: PROPS & STYLING

### The Look We Want

**Nothing is styled. Everything is placed the way it would be in a real office — by the people who use it, not by a photographer.**

### Prop Philosophy

Props in these images should feel accumulated, not arranged. A desk has stuff on it because someone works there, not because someone dressed the set. The randomness of real office desks is itself part of the authenticity.

### Standard Office Props (Always Available)

- Coffee mugs (various, never matching)
- Monitors, keyboards, mice
- Phones (desk phones and smartphones)
- Manila folders, binders, paper stacks
- Pens, Post-its, staplers
- Lanyards with ID badges
- Family photos in cheap frames
- Desk plants (varying health)
- Water bottles, snack wrappers
- Bulletin boards with pinned papers

### Comedy Props (Scene-Specific)

These are the absurd items that the photograph treats as normal:
- A hazmat suit worn at a desk
- A medieval helmet adjusted like a tie
- A katana carried like an umbrella
- War paint applied like moisturizer
- A fire extinguisher in a security bin

**The rule:** Comedy props are treated with the same visual weight as office props. The photograph does not draw special attention to them. They exist in the frame the same way a coffee mug exists in the frame.

### Annotation for AI

When prompting, describe props as:
> "standard office desk props arranged naturally as if by the person who sits there, nothing styled or placed for the camera, the accumulated clutter of a real workspace, [specific comedy prop] treated as a completely normal part of the scene with no special emphasis"

## DIMENSION 7: OVERALL VIBE

### The Look We Want

**"Someone from HR took this photo for the company intranet, and they didn't notice that something is very wrong."**

The overall vibe is institutional normalcy with buried absurdity. The images should feel corporate, competent, slightly boring — and then the viewer catches the joke. The split-second where the brain processes "this is a normal office photo" before catching the absurdity IS the comedy.

### The Vibe Spectrum

```
Too boring ← ← ← [WE ARE HERE] → → → Too interesting
(No joke visible)    (Joke is there     (Joke is obvious,
                      but the format     art-directed, the
                      doesn't notice)    photo "knows" it's
                                         funny)
```

We sit just slightly right of center. The joke is absolutely there — but the photograph is completely oblivious to it. The format has no idea it's in a comedy.

### Comparable Vibes

| Reference | What to Take From It |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Despair Inc. Demotivators | The commitment to beautiful, straight-faced photography paired with devastating content. The image never winks. |
| The Onion photography | "Delicate portraits of absurdity." Images that lean toward reality so hard that the absurd content is funnier by contrast. |
| Employee handbook photos | The specific visual register of internal corporate communications. Slightly flat, slightly awkward, immediately recognizable. |
| "The Office" documentary camera | The camera observes everything with the same neutral eye. It doesn't zoom in on the funny thing. It just... sees it. |
| Safety compliance posters | Official formatting applied to content that becomes absurd through the framing. |

### What the Vibe Is NOT

| Wrong Vibe | Why It Doesn't Work |
|------------|-------------------|
| Instagram/social media | Too personality-driven. These aren't anyone's photos. They're the company's photos. |
| Fashion editorial | Too intentional. Every choice is visible. The format has opinions. |
| Meme aesthetic | Too self-aware. Memes know they're jokes. Our photos don't. |
| Documentary/photojournalism | Too gritty, too handheld, too "captured." Our photos are posed (badly). |
| Commercial product photography | Too polished. Too much production value. Too glossy. |
| Wes Anderson / stylized | Any visible style is the format having an opinion. The format should be invisible. |

## HYBRID EXCEPTION: Technical Illustration Pages

For 2-4 pages that function as "tactical equipment diagrams" (e.g., the Loadout spread on pages 8-9), the booklet switches to **technical illustration / instructional diagram style.**

### Why This Works

The format shift itself reinforces the joke — the booklet is so committed to being a real corporate document that it switches visual registers the way a real manual would. Employee handbooks have photos AND diagrams. So does this one.

### Technical Illustration Characteristics

- Clean line work on white background
- Numbered callout lines pointing to parts of the object
- Labels in bold uppercase text (DELIVERY SYSTEM, GRIP ZONE, CONCEALMENT)
- The object presented like military field manual equipment or IKEA assembly diagrams
- Clinical, precise, generous negative space
- No characters — object only
- The mundane item (coffee cup, headphones, laptop, pen) presented with the seriousness of classified military hardware

### Annotation for AI

> "technical equipment diagram, clean precise line art on white background, thin callout lines extending from labeled parts, bold uppercase text labels, the visual style of a military field manual or IKEA assembly instruction, clinical and informational, generous negative space, no characters"

## PRODUCTION NOTES

### AI Imperfections as Feature

The "Internal Corporate Communications Photography" sub-style has a built-in advantage: AI's slight imperfections (slightly awkward hand poses, slightly unnatural staging, minor lighting inconsistencies) actually ENHANCE the authenticity rather than breaking it. Real corporate photos taken by non-photographers have these same qualities. A slightly stiff pose reads as "HR made me stand here" rather than "AI generated this."

### Text Compositing Rule

All text that is critical to a joke (signs, nameplates, posters, banners) should be composited in post-production via InDesign/Photoshop. Never rely on AI for legible text longer than 2-3 short words. AI can render the OBJECT that holds the text (a sign, a nameplate, a poster frame) but the actual words go in during post.

### Character Consistency Pipeline

- Brad Sterling requires a locked character reference (separate document) with consistency techniques specific to the generator being used
- Non-Brad characters need consistency within a spread (same face/build/wardrobe across images on the same pages) but not necessarily across spreads
- Diverse casting is assigned per character per spread — specific skin tone and demographic descriptions in each prompt, not generic defaults

### Generation Budget

Budget 3x generations per final image. For every keeper, expect to generate and reject at least two others. Curation is where quality lives. The comedy test in the art direction brief (section 9) is the approval checklist.

## STYLE REFERENCE CHECKLIST

Before generating any booklet image, verify it aligns with:

- [ ] **Lighting:** Flat overhead fluorescent, not dramatic or directional (unless Brad cinematic moment)
- [ ] **Composition:** Eye-level medium shot, slightly awkward corporate staging, reads at 2"x2"
- [ ] **Color:** Environmental palette (beige/gray/muted), no filters or grading, no imposed color scheme
- [ ] **Setting:** Generic American corporate office, 2005-2020 renovation vintage
- [ ] **People:** Real-looking, business casual, slightly tired, nobody reacts to absurdity
- [ ] **Props:** Natural desk clutter, comedy props treated with zero special emphasis
- [ ] **Vibe:** "HR took this photo for the intranet and didn't notice something is very wrong"
- [ ] **Absurdity model:** Layer 1 plausible at first glance, Layer 2 joke within 3 seconds, Layer 3 background rewards
