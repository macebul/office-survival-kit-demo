01 Basic Product Profile

Product Name

  • Confiscated Stapler

Brad Sterling's Corporate Rebrand

  • The Minimalist Desk Sculpture™ - "A bold artistic statement piece celebrating negative space and the beauty of absence"

Parent Product

  • Office Survival Kit (Item 8 of 7 + 1 empty recess)

Brand

  • Witty Yeti (Amazon brand)
  • Elevate Co. (in-universe fictional company, appears in product copy)

Product Type

  • Empty die-cut recess in the shape of a small stapler - chalk outline only, no item present

Role in Kit

  • The absence IS the joke - the only empty slot in the entire kit
  • A visual punchline that lands the moment Tray 2 is opened
  • The highest comedy-to-cost ratio of any "item" in the kit ($0 COGS, guaranteed laugh)
  • A crime scene that connects the Office Space universal (everyone's had a stapler stolen) to the prison theme (confiscated contraband)

Physical Specs

  • What it is: Empty die-cut recess shaped like a small stapler silhouette
  • Recess dimensions: ~4.0" x 1.5" (working estimate)
  • Visual: White chalk-outline silhouette printed at the bottom of the recess
  • Label: Printed adjacent to recess on tray floor
  • COGS: $0 (the recess is die-cut into the tray mold that already exists)

Tray Recess Label - DRAFT

  • "CONFISCATED - WEAPONIZED OFFICE EQUIPMENT. INCIDENT #147. DO NOT REISSUE."

Character Treatments

  • Brad Sterling (Mode 1 - corporate): Calls it "The Minimalist Desk Sculpture™" and insists it's an intentional design feature celebrating negative space
  • The OG (defaced product guide): Covers Brad's entire description with a sticky note reading "They took it. I don't want to talk about it. - OG"
  • Gary (QC inspector, exterior box): "Everything checks out. I don't want to talk about the stapler."

Activity Guide Treatment

  • Pages 9-10 of the Activity Guide are dedicated to the Confiscated Stapler
  • "The Moment of Silence" - when someone finds the chalk outline, they understand. No activity needed. This is a crime scene
  • The OG's "incident report" for this item - what happened, why it was taken, who was involved
  • "The Shrine" - invite the recipient to leave a small office supply in the empty recess as a tribute

Discovery Context

  • Tray 2, alongside the three card deck boxes (Cell Talk, Rap Sheets, Sweet Nothings)
  • Discovered after Tray 1 items have been explored (pen, tattoos, stickers)
  • Recipient opens Tray 2 expecting more items, finds three card boxes AND an empty recess
  • The chalk outline is immediately recognizable as a stapler shape
  • The deadpan label completes the joke without over-explaining it

02 Strategic Overview

Core Value Proposition

  • An intentionally empty recess that generates one of the biggest laughs in the kit at zero additional cost
  • The comedy comes from the collision of expectation (there should be something here) and reality (there is nothing here except a chalk outline and incident report)
  • It's the only item in the kit that doesn't exist, and that absence makes it more memorable than many of the items that do

Strategic Position Within the Kit

  • The Confiscated Stapler sits in Tray 2 alongside the three card decks
  • By the time the recipient reaches Tray 2, they've seen the pen, tattoos, and stickers in Tray 1 and established a pattern: every recess contains something
  • The empty recess breaks that pattern, and the pattern break IS the joke
  • It's also the first time the recipient encounters the kit's darker comedy register - confiscated contraband, an incident report, a chalk outline

Why This Item Matters

  • At $0 COGS, it's the most cost-efficient comedy beat in the entire product
  • The chalk outline creates a universally understood visual gag (everyone's stapler has been "stolen" at work)
  • It echoes the Red Stapler from Office Space in the cultural subconscious without explicitly referencing it
  • Multiple characters reference the stapler independently (Brad, the OG, Gary), which creates the sense of a shared institutional trauma
  • The "incident report" in the Activity Guide adds a narrative layer that rewards recipients who read the guide

Key Strategic Tension

  • The empty recess must feel intentional, not like a manufacturing error or missing component
  • The chalk outline and label need to communicate "this is the joke" within 2-3 seconds of visual discovery
  • If the recipient doesn't register the stapler shape or doesn't read the label, the comedy moment is lost
  • The recess can't be too prominent (it would overshadow the card decks in the same tray) or too subtle (it would be missed entirely)

Multi-Character Comedy Device

  • The Confiscated Stapler is the only item referenced by three different characters, each with a distinct reaction
  • Brad's reaction (corporate spin): "This is a design feature! It's art! It's intentional!"
  • The OG's reaction (raw truth): "They took it. I don't want to talk about it."
  • Gary's reaction (haunted inspector): "Everything checks out. I don't want to talk about the stapler."
  • The fact that nobody will discuss what happened is funnier than any explanation could be

Revenue Contribution

  • Zero direct COGS contribution - pure margin
  • The empty recess adds perceived complexity and design depth to the kit
  • It creates a shareable moment that appears in reviews and social posts
  • "There's a chalk outline where a stapler USED to be" is the kind of detail that sells kits through word of mouth
  • It's the detail that makes people say "whoever made this thought of everything"

03 Feature/Benefit Analysis

Recess-Level Analysis

Empty Die-Cut Stapler Silhouette

  • Feature: A recess die-cut into the tray in the exact shape of a small stapler, with nothing inside
  • Functional benefit: None (that's the point)
  • Emotional benefit: The visual gap between "there should be something here" and "there is nothing here" creates an instant laugh
  • Strategic role: The absence makes the recipient look closer, read the label, and engage more deeply than they would with an actual item

White Chalk Outline at Bottom of Recess

  • Feature: A white chalk-outline silhouette printed on the tray floor inside the recess
  • Functional benefit: Clarifies that the empty recess is a stapler shape (not ambiguous)
  • Emotional benefit: The chalk outline is a universally understood crime scene reference - it transforms a missing item into a crime scene
  • Strategic role: The visual gag works before the recipient reads a single word. It's a pre-verbal joke that transcends language

Incident Label - "CONFISCATED - WEAPONIZED OFFICE EQUIPMENT. INCIDENT #147. DO NOT REISSUE."

  • Feature: Deadpan institutional text printed adjacent to the recess
  • Functional benefit: Confirms the empty recess is intentional (not a packaging error)
  • Emotional benefit: The bureaucratic treatment of a missing stapler as a weapons incident is absurd. The incident number implies an entire documented history of stapler-related violence
  • Strategic role: The label adds a second comedy layer on top of the visual gag. First you see nothing, then you read WHY there's nothing

Stapler Shape Recognition

  • Feature: The recess is shaped like a universally recognizable office stapler
  • Functional benefit: Instant identification without explanation
  • Emotional benefit: Everyone has had a stapler stolen at work. The shape triggers personal recognition before the joke even lands
  • Strategic role: The Red Stapler from Office Space lives in the cultural subconscious. The shape activates that association without needing to make an explicit reference

Character-Level Analysis

Brad Sterling's "Minimalist Desk Sculpture™" Treatment

  • Feature: Brad's corporate description of the empty recess as an intentional art piece celebrating negative space
  • Functional benefit: None (comedy device)
  • Emotional benefit: Brad's inability to admit something is missing is character comedy at its purest. His spin is so aggressively corporate that it becomes funnier than the empty recess itself
  • Strategic role: This is the clearest example of Brad's Mode 1 voice (dead-serious corporate veneer) in the entire kit. It demonstrates the comedy pattern that runs through all his copy

The OG's Sticky Note - "They took it. I don't want to talk about it."

  • Feature: A sticky note in the defaced product guide that covers Brad's entire Minimalist Desk Sculpture description
  • Functional benefit: Introduces the two-voice narrative conflict to the recipient
  • Emotional benefit: The OG's refusal to discuss the stapler implies something genuinely upsetting happened. The contrast between Brad's spin and the OG's raw emotion is the comedy engine
  • Strategic role: This is the moment the recipient realizes there are two competing voices in the kit. It's a narrative milestone disguised as a joke about a missing stapler

Gary's QC Sticker Note - "Everything checks out. I don't want to talk about the stapler."

  • Feature: A line in Gary's exterior box inspection notes referencing the stapler
  • Functional benefit: Creates pre-unboxing curiosity (the buyer sees Gary's note before opening the kit)
  • Emotional benefit: A third character independently refusing to discuss the stapler implies a shared institutional trauma. The conspiracy of silence is funnier than any explanation
  • Strategic role: Gary's note plants a seed before the kit is even opened. When the recipient finally finds the empty recess, they remember Gary's warning and the payoff is stronger

04 Buyer Psychology

Primary Psychological Role

  • The Confiscated Stapler is the kit's proof of design intelligence
  • An empty recess where a stapler should be is the detail that convinces both buyers and recipients that every element of the kit was intentional
  • It's the single best indicator of the kit's design depth because it takes MORE design thinking to make something funny by its absence than by its presence

Emotional Triggers (Buyer - Seeing It in the Listing)

  • Curiosity - If the listing hints at the chalk outline or the "INCIDENT #147" label, buyers want to know the story
  • Design confidence - A kit that bothers to create a joke out of empty space signals attention to detail across the entire product
  • Differentiation - No other gag gift kit has an intentionally empty slot. This detail alone tells the buyer "this is different from everything else"
  • Shareability preview - Buyers can already imagine the recipient's reaction to finding a chalk outline where a stapler should be

Emotional Triggers (Recipient - During Discovery)

  • Expectation violation - Every other recess has something in it. This one doesn't. The brain registers the gap before it understands the joke
  • Recognition - "Wait, is that a stapler shape?" The chalk outline forces active pattern recognition, which makes the joke land harder than passive reading
  • Escalation - The label takes the gag from visual to narrative. "WEAPONIZED OFFICE EQUIPMENT" is an escalation that makes people laugh aloud
  • Conspiracy awareness - If they've seen Gary's note on the exterior box, finding the empty recess completes a story thread they didn't know they were following

Decision-Making Role in Purchase

  • The Confiscated Stapler doesn't directly drive purchase decisions because most buyers won't know about it in advance
  • However, if the listing teases "the incident" or shows the chalk outline in images, it becomes a curiosity hook that differentiates the listing
  • For buyers who read reviews, the stapler is frequently mentioned as a standout detail ("I love the empty stapler slot with the chalk outline")
  • It signals design quality that raises confidence in all other kit elements

Risk Mitigation Role

  • The Confiscated Stapler is risk-free for the buyer because it costs nothing and adds nothing physical
  • There's no quality concern - it's an empty space. It can't be broken, cheap, or disappointing in a material sense
  • The only risk is that the recipient doesn't register the joke, but the chalk outline and label minimize this
  • It's the one element in the kit that has zero downside and meaningful upside

Cognitive Biases in Play

  • Expectation violation - The human brain expects every recess to contain an item. The empty one creates a surprise response disproportionate to its simplicity
  • Completeness bias - The missing stapler makes the kit feel like it SHOULD have 8 items but one was "taken." This makes the kit feel larger than it is - like there's a hidden 8th item story
  • Narrative transportation - The incident label invites the recipient to construct a backstory. They fill in the details themselves, which makes the joke more personal and memorable
  • Social proof through absence - The fact that three characters independently refuse to discuss the stapler creates a sense of shared experience without any actual explanation
  • Mere exposure effect - The stapler gets referenced multiple times across the kit (Gary's note, the OG's sticky note, the Activity Guide). Each mention reinforces the joke without repeating it

Awareness Context

  • In listing images, the chalk outline should be visible but not the focal point - it's a discovery moment for the unboxing
  • Gary's "I don't want to talk about the stapler" note on the exterior box creates a pre-purchase Easter egg
  • The most effective listing treatment is a brief text reference like "7 items + 1 incident" that creates curiosity without explaining the joke

08 Objections & Purchase Barriers

Note: Most purchase objections apply to the kit as a whole. These objections are specific to how the Confiscated Stapler element influences or creates hesitation. Because this is an empty recess rather than a physical item, the objections are uniquely about perception rather than product quality.

Objection 1 - "Wait, there are only 7 items? The listing says 7 items and an empty slot?"

  • Root cause: Buyers who count items to assess value may perceive the empty recess as padding or a missing component
  • Evidence needed: The listing must frame the kit as "7 items + 1 incident" or similar phrasing that positions the absence as intentional
  • Mitigation: The empty recess should never be presented as an "item" in the count. It should be treated as a feature of the briefcase design. The listing should communicate that finding the chalk outline is a discovery moment, not a gap in the product

Objection 2 - "Is the empty slot a manufacturing defect they're trying to spin?"

  • Root cause: Cynical buyers may suspect the empty recess is a cost-cutting measure dressed up as a joke
  • Evidence needed: The chalk outline and incident label must be clearly printed and visually intentional. Rough or low-quality printing would undermine the "this was designed" signal
  • Mitigation: The die-cut stapler shape, the clean chalk outline, and the typeset label all communicate deliberate design. If the recess looks like a random empty space with a sticker slapped on it, the joke fails. The execution quality must match the concept quality

Objection 3 - "The joke might not land - what if they just think something's missing?"

  • Root cause: Gift-givers worry that the recipient won't understand the joke and will feel shortchanged
  • Evidence needed: The chalk outline and "CONFISCATED" label need to communicate the joke within 2-3 seconds of visual discovery
  • Mitigation: The chalk outline is universally understood as a crime scene reference. Combined with the word "CONFISCATED" in institutional caps, the visual and textual cues work together to eliminate confusion. If the recipient reads "WEAPONIZED OFFICE EQUIPMENT" and doesn't laugh, they were never going to laugh at anything

Objection 4 - "One fewer real item means less value for the price"

  • Root cause: Buyers mentally pricing each kit component may feel shortchanged by an empty slot
  • Evidence needed: The remaining 7 items must carry enough perceived value to justify the kit price independently
  • Mitigation: The pen ($10-15 perceived), the mug ($8-15 perceived), and the card decks ($5-8 each perceived) already exceed the $25-30 kit price. The empty recess is a bonus comedy beat, not a substitution for a real item. Frame it as "you get 7 real items AND the funniest empty space in consumer packaging"

Objection 5 - "The chalk outline might be too subtle or too small to register"

  • Root cause: If the recess is tucked into a corner or the chalk outline is faint, the joke could be missed entirely
  • Evidence needed: Physical mockup testing to confirm the chalk outline is visible at arm's length when Tray 2 is opened
  • Mitigation: The stapler silhouette should be printed with enough contrast to be immediately recognizable. The recess should be positioned where the eye naturally goes when Tray 2 is opened. If it's in a corner behind the card boxes, visibility drops significantly

10 Competitive Differentiation

The Gag Gift Kit Landscape and Empty Space

  • No competitor gag gift kit on Amazon uses intentionally empty space as a comedy device
  • Standard gag kits fill every available space with items to maximize the perceived item count
  • Empty space in competitor products is always unintentional - manufacturing gaps, poor packing, or cost-cutting
  • The Confiscated Stapler inverts this entirely: the empty space IS the highest-engagement design element

How Competitors Handle Packaging Space

  • Cheap gag kits: Items thrown loosely in a box with generic filler. Every gap feels accidental
  • Mid-range gag kits: Items organized in foam or cardboard inserts. No narrative, no design beyond containment
  • Premium gift kits: Custom inserts with branded tissue paper. Thoughtful but no comedy from the packaging itself
  • None of them treat empty space as content. None of them create a joke that costs nothing to produce

11 Non-Obvious Angles

Angle 1 - The Empty Recess Is the Kit's Design Resume

Every real item in the kit proves the team can source products, write copy, and design boxes. The empty recess proves they can THINK. It's the element that shows the kit was designed by people who understand comedy architecture, not just people who can fill a briefcase with stuff. The stapler chalk outline is doing more for perceived design quality than any single physical item because it demonstrates intentionality at a level competitors never attempt.

Angle 2 - Three Characters, One Refusal

Brad spins it. The OG shuts down. Gary pretends it's fine. Three different characters, three different coping mechanisms, and all three refuse to explain what happened. This is structurally brilliant comedy because the recipient never gets an answer, and the absence of an answer is funnier than any explanation could be. The mystery of Incident #147 becomes the recipient's story to tell, and they get to fill in the details however they want.

Angle 3 - The Best Review Detail in the Kit

When customers write Amazon reviews, they gravitate toward details that feel clever and hard to describe. "There's a chalk outline where a stapler used to be" is the exact kind of sentence that appears in 5-star reviews. It's visual, it's specific, it's unexpected, and it demonstrates the kit's design depth. The empty recess doesn't need to be the best item (it's not an item) - it needs to be the most interesting SENTENCE someone writes about the kit.

Angle 4 - The COGS-to-Comedy Masterclass

The Confiscated Stapler costs exactly $0 beyond the tray die-cut that would exist anyway. The chalk outline is a small addition to the tray printing. The incident label is a few words of typeset text. For essentially zero marginal cost, the kit gets one of its biggest laughs, a multi-character narrative thread, a pre-unboxing tease (Gary), an Activity Guide spread, and a shareable review detail. There is no better return on zero investment in consumer product design.

Angle 5 - The Universal Experience Made Specific

Everyone has had a stapler stolen at work. Everyone knows the frustration of reaching for something that should be there and finding it gone. The chalk outline activates this universal experience but reframes it through the prison/office parallel: your stapler wasn't stolen, it was CONFISCATED. As WEAPONIZED OFFICE EQUIPMENT. After an INCIDENT. The escalation from mundane reality to institutional absurdity is the comedy mechanism, and it works because the starting point is so relatable.

Angle 6 - Pre-Unboxing Foreshadowing via Gary

Gary's note on the exterior box ("I don't want to talk about the stapler") is foreshadowing. The buyer or recipient reads it before the kit is opened. It plants a question: what happened to the stapler? When they eventually find the chalk outline in Tray 2, the payoff is stronger because it was seeded. This is Chekhov's gun applied to packaging design - if you mention the stapler on the outside, you must show where it used to be on the inside.

Angle 7 - The Item That Costs Nothing and Can Never Be Cheapened

Every physical item in the kit is vulnerable to COGS pressure. The pen might get lighter. The mug might switch from enamel to stainless. The cards might lose a few from each deck. But the empty recess cannot be cheapened because it's already at zero cost. It will always be a die-cut shape, a chalk outline, and a label. This is the only element in the kit that is permanently immune to cost optimization degradation.

12 The Big Idea

The Confiscated Stapler isn't a missing item. It's the proof that someone was here.

Every crime scene tells a story through what's no longer there. The chalk outline doesn't show you the stapler - it shows you that the stapler EXISTED, that it was CONFISCATED, and that nobody will tell you what happened. The empty recess isn't a gap in the kit. It's evidence.

The Confiscated Stapler proves that the Office Survival Kit was designed by people who understand that the best joke doesn't require a product, a box, a print run, or a budget. It requires a die-cut silhouette, a six-word label, and the restraint to let the absence do the talking.

Every other item in the kit shows the buyer what they're getting. The chalk outline shows them what they're NOT getting - and that's funnier.

Implication for all content creation

  • Never explain what happened to the stapler. The mystery is the joke. Any explanation would be less funny than the silence
  • Treat the three-character refusal (Brad, the OG, Gary) as a running gag that appears across multiple kit touchpoints
  • The listing should reference "INCIDENT #147" or the empty recess obliquely, never with a full explanation
  • Let the chalk outline speak for itself. Over-explaining the joke kills it. The visual is the setup AND the punchline

13 Assumptions & Gaps

Assumptions Made in This Profile

1. The chalk outline will be immediately recognizable as a stapler - The die-cut shape and printed outline need to read as "stapler" within 2 seconds. If the silhouette is ambiguous (could be a tape dispenser, a phone, a hole punch), the joke doesn't land.

2. The recess will be visible when Tray 2 is opened - Assuming the empty recess is positioned where the eye naturally goes when the tray is revealed. If it's hidden behind the card deck boxes, discovery rate drops.

3. The incident label will be readable at arm's length - The label text needs to be legible when the recipient looks down into the tray. If the type is too small, they'll see the empty space but miss the punchline.

4. Gary's exterior box note will be noticed by some buyers - The foreshadowing payoff only works for buyers/recipients who read the QC sticker on the outside of the box. Not everyone will, and that's fine - the chalk outline works independently.

5. The chalk outline won't be confused with a product slot for a future variant - The outline and label must communicate "this was taken" not "insert stapler here." If it reads like a placeholder for a product they forgot to include, the comedy fails and the buyer feels cheated.

6. The Activity Guide will include the Incident #147 backstory - This profile assumes pages 9-10 of the Activity Guide deliver the OG's incident report. If the guide gets cut from the kit or the stapler pages are removed, a narrative payoff layer is lost.

7. Recipients will discover Tray 2 before the hidden mug compartment - The joke's position in the unboxing sequence assumes Tray 2 comes before the Mug Shot reveal. If someone discovers the hidden compartment first, the Tray 2 discovery loses some impact.

Known Gaps

1. Recess position in Tray 2 not finalized - The exact placement relative to the card deck boxes affects visibility. The recess needs to be in a position where it's noticed naturally, not hidden in a corner.

2. Chalk outline print method not specified - White printed outline on the tray floor surface. Print method (screen print, offset, digital) and contrast level against the tray material need testing.

3. Incident label typography not designed - The label text exists as a draft but the font, size, spacing, and position adjacent to the recess haven't been designed.

4. Activity Guide incident report not written - The concept exists (OG's deadpan police report about Incident #147) but no actual copy has been drafted.

5. No physical mockup tested - The chalk outline's visual clarity, the label's readability, and the overall discovery moment have not been tested with real people at actual size.

6. Tray material color not confirmed - The chalk outline's visibility depends on the tray surface color and material. White outline on a dark tray reads clearly. White outline on a light tray may not have enough contrast.

7. Brad's "Minimalist Desk Sculpture" copy location not determined - Brad's spin appears in the defaced product guide but the exact layout and the OG's sticky note covering it haven't been designed.

8. Gary's exterior box note placement not finalized - Gary's "I don't want to talk about the stapler" line exists in the exterior copy document but the QC sticker's final position and design on the actual box haven't been locked.

14 Final Recommendations

Recommendation 1 - Position the Recess for Immediate Visibility in Tray 2

  • The empty recess should be in a position where the recipient sees it naturally when Tray 2 is opened
  • If the three card deck boxes are arranged across the tray with the stapler recess alongside or between them, it will be noticed
  • If the recess is tucked in a back corner behind the boxes, it will be missed by many recipients
  • Test with a physical mockup: open the tray and note where your eye goes first. The chalk outline should be in that zone

Recommendation 2 - Ensure Maximum Contrast for the Chalk Outline

  • The white chalk-outline silhouette must be clearly visible against the tray floor
  • If the tray material is light-colored, consider a different outline color (gray, off-white with shadow) or a darker tray floor in the recess area
  • The outline should be recognizable as a stapler from arm's length (roughly 2 feet)
  • Print a test at actual size and check visibility under indoor lighting - not just on screen

Recommendation 3 - Never Explain Incident #147

  • The comedy power of the Confiscated Stapler depends on the mystery never being resolved
  • The Activity Guide should reference the incident but tell the story through deadpan bureaucratic details that raise more questions than they answer
  • Brad, the OG, and Gary each refuse to explain what happened. This refusal is funnier than any explanation
  • If someone on the team wants to write the "real" backstory, resist the urge. The audience's imagination is always funnier than the reveal

Recommendation 4 - Coordinate the Three-Character References Across Touchpoints

  • Gary's exterior box note, Brad's product guide description, and the OG's sticky note are three touchpoints that reference the same missing item
  • These should be developed together, not independently, so the tone and timeline feel consistent
  • Gary's note comes first (exterior box, pre-opening). Brad and the OG come second (product guide, during unboxing). The chalk outline comes third (Tray 2)
  • Each touchpoint escalates the mystery without resolving it

Recommendation 5 - Write the Activity Guide Incident Report in Deadpan OG Voice

  • Pages 9-10 of the Activity Guide should read like a police incident report filed by someone who witnessed the stapler confiscation
  • Deadpan tone, institutional formatting, specific but absurd details
  • Include "The Moment of Silence" and "The Shrine" activities from the recommendation document
  • This is the most narrative-dense treatment the stapler gets - make it count

Recommendation 6 - Test the 2-Second Recognition Threshold

  • The chalk outline + label should communicate "this is a joke about a confiscated stapler" within 2-3 seconds
  • Show the empty tray to 3-5 people who haven't seen the kit and time how long it takes them to understand
  • If it takes longer than 3 seconds, either the outline isn't clear enough or the label isn't prominent enough
  • The goal is: see empty space, recognize stapler shape, read CONFISCATED, laugh. All within 2-3 seconds

Recommendation 7 - Use the Confiscated Stapler as a Listing Differentiation Hook

  • In the Amazon listing, a brief reference to the empty recess (without explaining the full joke) creates curiosity
  • Consider phrasing like "7 items, 1 classified item, and 1 incident we can't discuss" in the bullet points
  • Gary's "I don't want to talk about the stapler" could appear in a listing image if the exterior box is photographed
  • The goal is to create enough mystery that buyers want to discover what the "incident" is, not to explain the joke in advance
