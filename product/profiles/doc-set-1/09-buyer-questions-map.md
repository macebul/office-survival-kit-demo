# Artifact 09: Buyer Questions Map

> These questions are specific to the booklet as it appears within the kit listing and unboxing experience.

---

## Questions the buyer asks while evaluating the listing

- "What is that booklet thing sitting on top of everything?"
  - Triggered by hero and content-spread listing images
  - Must be answered by visible cover design and at least one interior spread in the image gallery
- "Is there actual content in there or is it just a card?"
  - Page count and interior photography must be visible enough to distinguish this from a greeting card or single-sheet insert
- "Will they actually read it or just toss it aside?"
  - Answered by lifestyle images showing someone engaged with the booklet, not just holding it
- "Is the humor going to make sense to the person I'm buying for?"
  - Listing images showing interior pages let the buyer preview the voice and self-select
  - If the buyer laughs at the listing, they have their answer
- "Does this add real value or is it padding?"
  - The gold foil cover visible in photography answers this faster than any copy can

---

## Questions the recipient asks during unboxing

- "Wait, what is this on top?"
  - The booklet is the first object encountered - this question is inevitable and desirable
  - Cover design must reward the pause with visual intrigue, not confusion
- "Should I read this now or open the other stuff first?"
  - The booklet's placement creates this tension intentionally
  - Pages 2-3 must hook fast enough that reading wins over rummaging
- "Who is Brad Sterling?"
  - Answered within the first two pages through voice, visual DNA, and character commitment
  - The question itself is part of the entertainment
- "Is this a real company?"
  - The production quality and total character commitment make this question land as comedy, not confusion
  - The answer reveals itself gradually through escalating absurdity

---

## Questions the group asks while watching the unboxing

- "What does it say? Read it out loud!"
  - The booklet creates a second audience - people watching the unboxer read
  - Pages must be written for read-aloud performance, not silent reading only
- "Wait, go back - what was that part about the loadout?"
  - Format subversions (tactical diagram, decoder card) create rewind moments
  - These moments extend unboxing duration and group engagement
- "Is there more? Keep reading!"
  - The three-act structure creates forward momentum that the group reinforces
  - Each format change resets attention and earns the next page turn
- "Let me see that fine print"
  - The back cover fine print becomes a group activity - someone always grabs the booklet to read it themselves
  - This is the highest-shareability moment in the entire booklet

---

## Questions asked after the unboxing is complete

- "Wait, does the booklet actually reference the stuff in the kit?"
  - The indirect item references (Conference Room B, "earned your ink," "put it in writing") gain meaning retroactively
  - This realization drives a second read-through with the items laid out
- "Can I keep the booklet?"
  - Signals the booklet succeeded as a standalone entertainment object
  - The answer should be yes - it has value independent of the kit items
- "Where can I get more of this Brad Sterling stuff?"
  - The character commitment creates curiosity beyond the booklet
  - This is a future content opportunity, not a current listing concern
- "Did you read the part about [specific page]?"
  - Individual pages become reference points in post-unboxing conversation
  - Format variety means different people remember different sections

---

## Questions the buyer asks post-purchase

- "Did they read the booklet or skip it?"
  - Buyers want confirmation their purchase delivered entertainment, not just items
  - The booklet is the most visible proof of engagement when the recipient shares their reaction
- "Was the booklet funny or was it too weird?"
  - This concern resolves when the recipient quotes the booklet or references Brad
  - The voice is calibrated to be absurd enough to be clearly satirical, not ambiguously serious
- "Would this work for a different person too?"
  - A positive booklet experience drives repeat purchases for other recipients
  - The comedy is rooted in universal office experience, not niche references
- "Did they notice the fine print?"
  - The back cover fine print is the most frequently mentioned detail in word-of-mouth
  - If the buyer asks this, someone told them about it - the booklet did its job
