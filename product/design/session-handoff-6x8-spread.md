## THE WORLD RULES (CRITICAL — READ FIRST)

**The office is a prison. This is not a metaphor.** In this universe, nobody thinks it's clever to compare the office to prison. The comparison doesn't exist because there's nothing to compare — the office just IS this way. The language of incarceration is the language of corporate life. It's the water everyone swims in.

**The world is played straight.** Nobody winks. Nobody says "get it? because the office is like a prison!" The moment anyone acknowledges the comparison AS a comparison, the world breaks.

**Shared vocabulary (used naturally, never explained):**
- Doing time / serving your sentence = Working at a company
- Your cell = Your cubicle or desk
- The yard = Break room, common areas
- Solitary = Working alone, noise-canceling headphones, bathroom hiding
- Contraband = Anything that makes the day bearable
- Lifers = Long-tenured employees
- Release day = Last day at a job
- The warden = Upper management, HR, the boss
- Intake / processing = Onboarding
- Commissary = Vending machines, office supply closet
- Shank / shiv = Your pen
- Ink = Tattoos marking time served
- Turf = Your desk, claimed spaces

## BRAD STERLING'S VOICE

Brad operates in two modes:

**Mode 1 (Product Copy Brad):** Competent. Sardonic. Institutional format played straight with escalating absurd content. Dry, never winks. Format is the straight man, content is the comedian. The reader laughs WITH this copy. No puns, no exclamation points, no breaking the format.

**Mode 2 (Personal Brand Brad):** Delusional hustle-culture LinkedIn post given physical form. 100% sincere. Uses words like "vision," "disrupt," "elevate," "curate," "premium." Refers to himself in third person. The reader laughs AT Brad. Purple satin shirt, gold rope chain, aviators, pinky ring.

**The gap between Mode 1 and Mode 2 is the joke.** The better the product copy is, the more absurd Brad's personal brand becomes by contrast.

## MAGAZINE VOICE (FOR EDITORIAL PAGES)

The magazine voice is **enthusiastic, upbeat, playful, persuasive** — a willing, polished participant in the insanity. Think of a magazine editor who is THRILLED about every piece of content. The comedy engine: **everyone is aggressively enthusiastic about the horror.** The format is SELLING you the insanity.

The tone is **sardonic, not cynical.** The people in this world haven't given up — they've adapted. The attitude is: "this is absurd, we all know it's absurd, and here's how we get through it together." That warmth underneath makes it a gift someone gives to a coworker they actually like.

## PAGES 6-7: "THE UPGRADE" — THE SPREAD TO BUILD

### Concept

A full two-page spread presenting a side-by-side comparison between a prison cell (6x9 ft) and an office cubicle (6x8 ft). Presented as a completely serious magazine property comparison — like HGTV's "Which Home Should They Choose?" or a Zillow side-by-side. The magazine genuinely believes the cubicle is the better deal and makes a passionate, enthusiastic argument for it.

### The Comedy Engine

The magazine is making a substantive argument about why the cubicle is the better option — highlighting features in absurd ways to make them seem ultra-positive and optimistic. The kicker: the cell is literally one square foot bigger, has a window, has a private bathroom, and has enforced quiet hours. The cubicle loses on almost every metric. But the magazine spins every loss as a win.

### The Core Joke

"A cubicle is 6x8. Death row is 6x9. We like to think of it as an upgrade." — This line already exists in the copy doc and has been the setup for this concept from the beginning.

### Visual Concept

**Diagonal split across the spread.** One side shows a prison cell photographed and staged like a real estate listing (warm lighting, neatly made bed, tidy). The other side shows a cubicle photographed and staged the same way. Both shot with identical aspirational energy — the joke is that they're presented as equivalent options, and the cell might actually look slightly nicer.

The headline concept: **"CHOOSE YOUR 6x8"** — framing it as a genuine choice between two properties.

### Specs Comparison (Real Estate Listing Format)

Both properties listed with the same spec categories, side by side:

**THE CELL (6x9):**
- Dimensions: 6x9 ft (54 sq ft)
- Layout: Private enclosed unit
- Natural light: One window (barred, but present)
- Climate: Regulated
- Storage: Built-in shelving, under-bed storage
- Amenities: En-suite facilities
- Noise level: Controlled (enforced quiet hours)
- Tenure: Determined at sentencing
- Availability: By assignment only

**YOUR CUBICLE (6x8):**
- Dimensions: 6x8 ft (48 sq ft)
- Layout: Open concept (walls stop at 5 feet)
- Natural light: Adjacent (30 feet from nearest window)
- Climate: Aggressive air conditioning, April through eternity
- Storage: One overhead bin, one file drawer (jammed since 2019)
- Amenities: Shared printer (see Disputed Territory)
- Noise level: Yes
- Tenure: At-will (theirs, not yours)
- Availability: Immediate (someone just left)

### Spread Elements to Write

1. **Headline** — "CHOOSE YOUR 6x8" or similar
2. **Deck/subhead** — Sets the comparison frame
3. **Property specs** — Side-by-side comparison in real estate listing format
4. **Verdict/callout** — The magazine declares the cubicle the winner despite it losing on every metric
5. **Brad quote** — Something about square footage being a mindset
6. **"DID YOU KNOW?" callout** — "The average new hire fills out 54 pages of paperwork on their first day. The average inmate fills out 12. We're not saying anything. We're just sharing data."
7. **Any additional editorial copy** needed to fill the spread

### Voice for This Spread

The magazine voice — enthusiastic, positive, persuasive. This is a real property comparison piece. The magazine has done its research and is presenting its findings with total conviction. The cubicle wins because the magazine WANTS it to win. Every disadvantage is reframed as a feature. "Open concept" means no walls. "Natural light adjacent" means nowhere near a window. The magazine sees no irony.

- Photorealistic editorial/real estate photography style

## LESSONS LEARNED (FROM THIS SESSION AND PRIOR)

### Image Prompts
- Each image needs something absurd/funny happening — not just a well-photographed mundane thing
- Don't just illustrate the joke in the caption — the image should have its own visual joke
- Find the middle ground: "absurd in commitment to the bit," not dark/edgy or over-the-top
- The listing agent has gone too far staging photos — that's the comedy frame
- Add "brand names, logos, brand labels" to negative prompts when food/drink items are present to avoid recognizable brands
- When items need to fit in a space (like bottles in a drawer), make sure they physically make sense
- Add visual cues to make the source of things obvious (e.g., a tipped coffee mug next to the coffee stain)

### Copy
- Names should sound like branded amenity names a coworking space would use (positive, optimistic)
- Names should connect to prison vocabulary when possible
- Names should be funny when you learn what the space actually is
- Panic Pod is the benchmark for naming quality
- Two-word names work best in the layout
- The comedy formula: aspirational language + mundane/awful reality = joke
- Don't be tame, don't be over-the-top — be absurd in commitment to the bit
- Format stays buttoned-up, content gets unleashed

### Layout
- Photo-heavy pages need typographic variety on facing pages
- Charcoal band with white text works for the Related Properties section
- Alternating Warm Gray 15 tint bands work for property details tables
- 1:1 AR for related property images, 4:3 AR for photo grid images
- Don't let pages feel like "leftovers" — every page needs its own reason to exist
