# Design Brief: Office Survival Manual (Doc Set 2)

## Project Overview
- **Project:** Design and editorial layout for a 16-page saddle-stitched booklet — the "Office Survival Manual" — included inside the Office Survival Kit gag gift
- **Date:** May 13, 2026
- **Requester:** Product owner (internal)
- **Designer(s):** TBD
- **Complexity:** Complex/significant (16 pages of editorial design, interactive elements, content strategy, Brad's voice across two modes, must contrast with Doc Set 1)

---

## GOAL
*What needs to be different in the world because of this design.*

**The Task vs. The Goal:**
- **Task (what's being made):** A 16-page saddle-stitched booklet (~5.5" x 4.25" quarter-letter landscape), 2-color, that serves as an activity guide / survival manual for the kit items
- **Goal (what changes in reality):** The recipient transitions from passively admiring the kit items to actively imagining using them in their own office — and laughing at the idea of doing so. The manual turns a display-shelf gag gift into something the recipient actually engages with, writes in, and shares with coworkers.

**Before State -> After State:**
- **Before:** The recipient has unpacked Tray 1 and Tray 2, seen all the items in their boxes, and is impressed but hasn't mentally connected the items to their actual daily life. The kit is a clever object to look at.
- **After:** The recipient sees each item as a prop in a shared joke they can deploy at work. They've read at least a few activities, laughed, and either mentally bookmarked one to try or physically shown a page to someone nearby. The manual becomes the piece they return to — the re-readable content engine that keeps the kit alive past the unboxing.

**Downstream Goal:**
- Transform the kit from a display piece into an active, shareable, repeatable experience by giving each item a comedic use case the recipient can imagine (or actually attempt) in their own office

**Upstream Goal Chain:**
- Manual engages recipient with activities -> recipient uses/references items at work -> coworkers see items and ask about them (Goal 4: Shareability) -> "Where did you get that?" conversations -> organic word-of-mouth -> buyer feels like a hero for giving it (Goal 1: Buyer Confidence) -> repeat purchases and reviews (Goal 6: Commercial Viability)
- Manual is read cover-to-cover -> recipient discovers density of jokes -> re-reads and finds more -> lasting engagement beyond unboxing (Goal 5: Lasting Engagement)

**What Success Looks Like:**
- Recipient reads at least 4-6 pages during the first unboxing session (not just the cover)
- Recipient shows at least one page to someone else within 24 hours
- Recipient writes their name in the fill-in section (emotional ownership)
- The bingo card or a specific page ends up photographed and shared
- Recipient references an activity from the manual when using a kit item at work
- The manual is the piece people describe when telling others about the kit ("and there's this little booklet that tells you how to use everything...")

**What Failure Looks Like:**
- Recipient flips through it once and puts it back without reading
- The tone feels like actual instructions rather than comedy
- It reads as a catalog of items they already just unboxed (redundant, not additive)
- The design feels cheap or like an afterthought compared to the item boxes and Doc Set 1
- Brad's voice is inconsistent — either too competent (reads like real corporate training) or too absurd (breaks the straight-faced format)
- The interactive elements feel forced or embarrassing rather than inviting

**Risk of Misalignment:**
- Optimizing for joke density at the expense of readability. If every line tries to be the funniest line, the reader gets fatigued and stops reading — which kills engagement (Goal 5) and shareability (Goal 4). The manual needs rhythm: setup, escalation, payoff, breathing room, repeat.
- Over-designing for "premium" when the spec is 2-color on uncoated stock. This piece should feel different from Doc Set 1 (which is full-color, glossy, portrait, polished). If the designer fights the 2-color constraint instead of embracing it, the result will feel like a worse version of Doc Set 1 rather than a deliberately different artifact.

## AUDIENCE
*The specific humans whose state this design is trying to change.*

**Buyer vs. User:**
- **Are they the same person?** No
- **The Buyer:** Office worker (25-45) purchasing a gag gift for a coworker, friend, or family member. They will never see the manual — it's discovered during unboxing. The buyer's needs are served by the outer Amazon box, product listing, and overall perceived quality.
- **The User:** The gift recipient — an office worker who has just unboxed the kit and is now holding this booklet. They've already seen Doc Set 1 (Brad's polished brand book), the item boxes, the chalk outlines, and the empty stapler recess. They have full context for the world.
- **Decision Influencer:** None for the manual specifically. The buyer already purchased; this piece serves the recipient only.
- **Optimizing for:** The recipient (user). This piece exists entirely for the person holding it. The buyer never sees it before purchase. Any positive effect on the buyer comes indirectly — through the recipient's reaction, reviews, and word-of-mouth.

**Primary Audience: "The New Inmate"**
- **Who they are:** Office worker, 25-45, currently employed in a corporate or semi-corporate environment. Has endured meetings that should have been emails, has opinions about the thermostat, and knows exactly which coworker is "Kevin." Culturally literate — gets references, appreciates dry humor, values cleverness over slapstick.
- **Before State:**
  - *What they know:* They've unpacked the entire kit. They know the world (office-as-prison), the character (Brad Sterling), the items, and the joke. They've spent 5-15 minutes in the unboxing experience. They're warmed up.
  - *What they believe/assume:* "I've seen everything." They think the kit is the items. The manual is about to prove the kit is also an experience. They may assume this is a product catalog or instruction manual — something to skim and discard.
  - *What they're trying to do:* Deciding whether to keep exploring or put the kit on a shelf. They're in the "deceleration" phase of the unboxing — the big reveals (Tray 1, Tray 2, stapler recess) are done. They need a reason to keep going.
- **The Gap:** Medium. The recipient already likes the kit — they just need a nudge from "this is a cool object" to "this is something I'll actually use." The manual needs to cross the gap between admiring and imagining. The activity ideas do the heavy lifting: they plant specific, funny, achievable scenarios in the recipient's mind.
- **What would make them say "this is for me":** A page that describes their exact office situation with clinical precision — the coworker who microwaves fish, the meeting that runs 20 minutes over, the printer that works for everyone except them. Recognition is the hook.
- **What would make them say "this isn't for me":** Jokes that are too generic ("offices are boring, am I right?"), activities that feel like homework, writing that talks down to them, humor that punches down, or a design that feels disposable.
- **Hidden Agenda:** They want permission to be a little rebellious at work. The manual gives them that permission through comedy — it frames tiny acts of desk territory-marking and passive-aggressive card deployment as sanctioned behavior. The institutional format (Brad's "official" publication) is the permission slip.
- **Skepticism / Resistance:** "This is just padding." The recipient may approach the manual skeptically — they've seen a lot of gag gifts that pad the box with filler content. The design and first page must signal immediately that this is worth reading, not filler.

**Secondary Audience:**
- Coworkers who see individual pages when the recipient shares them. They encounter the manual out of context — a single page shown on a phone, a bingo card pinned to a cubicle wall. Each page/spread needs to work as a standalone comedic beat, not just as part of a sequence.

**Key Psychological Insight:**
- The recipient has already been entertained by the unboxing — they don't need to be sold on the kit. What they need is a bridge from "this is a funny thing I received" to "this is a funny thing I do." The manual's job is to plant a specific mental image: the recipient at their desk, deploying a kit item, and the coworker's reaction. If the reader can see themselves doing it, the kit transcends the shelf.

## CONTEXT
*The specific circumstances under which the audience will encounter this design.*

**Physical / Digital Environment:**
- **Where:** At home (most likely — gifts are typically opened at home or at a party), at a desk, or in a break room. Indoor, seated, with good lighting.
- **Medium/Device:** Physical printed booklet. Held in hands. Experienced as a tactile object — the paper weight, the fold, the size all matter. Quarter-letter landscape is unusual — it will feel distinctive, like a pocket field guide rather than a standard pamphlet.
- **Environmental conditions:** Relatively calm. The recipient has chosen to keep unboxing — this is a voluntary, relaxed engagement moment. No time pressure. Likely smiling or laughing from previous reveals.

**Sought vs. Served:**
- **Sought — conditionally.** The recipient is actively exploring the kit and discovers this booklet on the compartment cover after lifting Tray 2. They chose to keep going. They're curious. But they haven't specifically sought a manual — it's a positive surprise within a sought experience.
- **Implication:** The design starts with moderate goodwill but zero expectation. The recipient expects another reveal moment (an object), not a reading experience. The cover must signal "this is worth your time" in under 3 seconds — before the recipient decides to skip it and go straight for whatever's underneath the compartment cover.

**Attention Available:**
- **Time window:** Extended — but earned, not given. The recipient has 2-3 seconds to decide whether to open and read the booklet or set it aside. If the cover earns the open, each spread gets 10-30 seconds. If the content is strong, a full read-through is 3-5 minutes. The bingo card and fill-in page may get revisited.
- **Competing stimuli:** The compartment cover with its teaser copy is visible beneath the booklet. The hidden mug compartment is the next reveal. The manual is competing against the recipient's curiosity about what's underneath — it must be compelling enough to delay that curiosity.
- **Decision mode:** Leisure / exploration. Not task-completion or decision-making. The recipient is in "delight discovery" mode.

**Emotional / Mental State:**
- Positive, amused, engaged. They've been having fun for 5-15 minutes. They've laughed. They're in a generous state — willing to give this booklet a chance if it earns it. They're also building toward the finale discovery (mug compartment), so there's a low hum of "what else is in here?"

**Social Setting:**
- Likely 50/50 alone vs. with others present. If others are present (gift-giving context), the manual becomes performative — the recipient may read bits aloud, show pages, or narrate. Design should support both silent reading and "show this page" sharing.

**Technical / Physical Constraints:**
- **Size:** ~5.5" x 4.25" (quarter-letter landscape). Small — every element must be sized for this format. Body copy minimum ~8pt to remain readable.
- **Pages:** 16 (including cover and back cover). 14 interior pages. Saddle-stitched (2 wire staples). Pages must work as spreads when the booklet is opened flat.
- **Ink:** ~~2-color only~~ **LIKELY FULL COLOR** (pending confirmation). Original spec was 2-color (black + accent), but this has shifted. If full color is confirmed, photography and illustration become available. If it reverts to 2-color, the institutional constraint-as-style approach applies.
- **Paper:** 80-100gsm uncoated interior, 80-100lb cover stock. Matte, slightly rough texture. Should feel analog/institutional — like a government-issued document, not a marketing brochure.
- **Binding:** Saddle-stitched. Center spread will open fully flat. Gutter margins needed for readability at the spine on inner pages.
- **Production cost target:** Well under $1/unit at overseas bulk (1,000+ units)

**Context Shifts:**
- **Primary context:** First discovery during unboxing (hands, quiet environment, positive mood)
- **Secondary context:** Re-reading at work desk (different emotional state — the recipient is now actually in the "prison," and the humor hits differently when they're looking at the coworker described in the manual)
- **Tertiary context:** Individual pages shared via photo/social (must work as standalone images — typography and layout must be readable when photographed on a phone)
- Design for the primary context first; ensure pages are photographable for tertiary context.

## PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION & PRIORITIZATION

**All problems this design must solve:**
- Earn the open — cover must signal "worth reading" in under 3 seconds
- Differentiate from Doc Set 1 — must feel like a fundamentally different artifact, not "part 2 of the same thing"
- Bridge from "cool object" to "thing I'll use" — activities must plant specific mental images
- Sustain reading across 16 pages — rhythm, pacing, variety must prevent fatigue
- Work within 2-color constraint — must feel intentional, not cheap
- Support Brad's two modes across 16 pages — Mode 1 (competent product humor) dominates, Mode 2 (delusional branding) provides comedic peaks
- Include interactive elements (checkboxes, fill-ins, bingo card) that feel inviting, not forced
- Each spread must work standalone for sharing/photography
- Balance joke density with breathing room — avoid fatigue
- Connect each activity to a specific kit item — justify the manual's existence as additive, not redundant
- Navigate the "Volume II" naming implication — what is this? Why isn't Volume I here?

**Causal Chain (Attention > Message > Action):**

1. **ATTENTION: The cover must earn the open**  *(If we fail here, nothing else matters)*
   - The recipient has just lifted Tray 2 and sees this booklet sitting on the compartment cover. They're curious about what's under the cover, not about a booklet. The cover has 2-3 seconds to say "read me first."
   - Competition: the compartment cover's teaser copy and the anticipation of the final reveal. The manual is literally sitting on top of the thing they want to see next.
   - The cover must feel like a discovery, not an obstruction. It should look like something that was placed here intentionally — a document that belongs in this specific position, at this specific moment.

2. **MESSAGE: "This will change how you use the kit"**
   - The first spread (inside cover + page 1) must communicate: this isn't a product catalog. This is a playbook. It tells you what to DO with the things you just unpacked.
   - The audience's before state: they've seen the items but think of them as display objects. The message reframes the items as tools for daily deployment.
   - Speed: this message needs to land within the first 20 seconds of reading (inside cover + page 1).

3. **MESSAGE: "This is Brad's publication — and it's funnier than you expected"**
   - Brad's voice establishes the tone. Mode 1 (competent institutional humor) carries the bulk. Mode 2 (delusional personal branding) appears in the intro and escalates subtly — a "From the Founder" intro, trademark symbols on activities, Brad's self-awards scattered through margins.
   - The comedy must reward re-reading. First pass: laugh at the activities. Second pass: catch Brad's marginalia, fine-print jokes, and self-promotional insertions.

4. **ACTION: Reader imagines deploying a kit item at work — and maybe actually does it**
   - Each activity page should end with the reader thinking "I could actually do that" — or at least "I know someone who would."
   - The fill-in page (name, date, days survived) is the conversion event: once they write in it, it's theirs.
   - The bingo card is the shareability payload: the page that gets photographed, pinned up, or texted.

## MESSAGES & CONSTRAINTS

**Specific messages to convey (prioritized):**
1. **"Here's what you do with all of this."** The manual is a deployment guide, not a recap. Each item gets activities the recipient can imagine doing.
2. **"This world is real and you're in it now."** The institutional format (Brad's Elevate Co. publication) extends the office-as-prison metaphor into interactive territory — check boxes, incident reports, survival records.

5. **"Brad made this."** Subtle but present — Brad's Elevate Co. branding, his trademark tics, his credential drops in margins — establishes this as his "flagship thought-leadership publication." The publication itself is a joke about Brad's self-image.

- Format/size: 16 pages, ~5.5" x 4.25" quarter-letter landscape, saddle-stitched
- Ink: Likely full color (pending confirmation). Photography and illustration available if confirmed.
- Paper: 80-100gsm uncoated interior. Matte. Analog feel.
- Brand: Brad Sterling / Elevate Co. sole voice. No OG character. No handwritten elements.
- Non-negotiables:
  - Must feel different from Doc Set 1. Note: with both doc sets potentially in full color, the differentiation strategy shifts from "color vs. no color" to format (landscape vs. portrait), purpose (activity guide vs. brand book), and editorial voice. The contrast must come from what the piece IS, not just how it's printed.
  - Must include at least one shareable/photographable page (bingo card or equivalent)
  - Must include fill-in interactive elements (checkboxes, name field, survival record)
  - Activities must map to actual kit items — not generic office humor
  - Brad's voice rules apply: format stays buttoned-up, content gets unleashed. No winking. No breaking the fourth wall.

# Recommended Approach: Office Survival Manual (Doc Set 2)

## Strategic Direction

**Recommended overall approach:**
Design the manual as Brad Sterling's attempt at a "workplace wellness activity guide" — a corporate publication that reads like something an HR department would issue if the HR department were run by a man in a purple satin shirt who has never actually worked in an office. The format is institutional: structured pages, consistent layout grids, numbered activities, official-looking checkboxes, section headers in uppercase sans-serif. The content within those formats is where Brad's genuine talent for office humor shines (Mode 1), and his self-promotional instincts occasionally hijack the margins (Mode 2).

The key design insight is that this manual should feel like a deliberately different artifact from Doc Set 1. Even if both are full color, the differentiation comes from purpose, format, and editorial voice — not print specs. Doc Set 1 is a portrait-format brand book (Brad's polished marketing piece). The manual is a landscape-format activity guide (Brad's "thought-leadership content piece" — and he believes it's equally premium). Think employee handbook vs. annual report. Think field manual vs. coffee table book. The contrast between the two doc sets is itself a joke: Brad produced both, but one is his sales pitch and the other is his content play.

**Rationale:**
- **Given the goal:** The manual needs to bridge from "admire the kit" to "use the kit." An institutional activity-guide format (numbered activities, checkboxes, field notes) naturally frames the items as tools to deploy, not objects to display. The format does the work.
- **Given the audience's before state:** The recipient has spent 5-15 minutes in Brad's world. They know the voice. They're warmed up. The manual can go deeper into the comedy without re-establishing context. This means page 1 can hit the ground running.
- **Given the context:** The manual competes against the hidden mug compartment for attention. It must earn its read fast. A strong, visually distinct cover + an immediately funny opening spread are non-negotiable. The landscape format helps — it's physically different from everything else in the kit, signaling "this is a different kind of thing."

**Design principles for this project:**

- **Institutional format, not editorial.** This is an employee handbook, not a magazine. Grid-based layouts, consistent structure, bureaucratic typography. The format credibility is what makes the content funny.
- **Institutional aesthetic regardless of color capability.** Even in full color, the design should evoke government pamphlets, employee handbooks, and training manuals — not magazines or brand books. Use color to reinforce the institutional feel (warning labels, classification stamps, form field highlights) rather than for polish. If it reverts to 2-color, this principle becomes even stronger.
- **Contrast with Doc Set 1 through format and purpose.** Portrait vs. landscape. Brand book vs. activity guide. Polished sales piece vs. utilitarian content piece. The differentiation must hold regardless of color capability. If a design choice makes this feel more like Doc Set 1, it's wrong.
- **Each spread is a self-contained comedy beat.** A person sharing one page on their phone should get the joke without having read any other page.
- **Rhythm over density.** Not every line needs to be the funniest line. Build pages with setup-escalation-payoff-breathing room. A well-placed blank space after a punchline is funnier than another joke.
- **Write-in elements are the conversion event.** Once the recipient puts pen to paper — their name, their hire date, their "days without incident" count — the manual stops being a gift and starts being theirs.

## Action Plan

**Phase 1: Content Architecture**
- Rewrite the activity-guide-recs-v2 content for Brad's voice — this is the critical-path prerequisite (the activities are strong; the framing needs to shift from OG's "field notes" to Brad's "Workplace Elevation Protocols"). No design work should begin until this is at least drafted.
- Explore title options (Volume numbering is flexible — designer has freedom to propose what fits best, including the numbering logic as a Brad character beat)
- Explore accent color direction (test gold vs. orange — gold reads premium/Elevate Co. brand, orange reads prison/institutional and matches kit-wide choices. Present both for decision.)
- Draft the page map with content assigned to each spread
- Define Brad's Mode 1 / Mode 2 distribution across pages
- Write the bingo card content (the most shareable page — worth getting right early)
- Deliverable: Annotated page map with content outline, voice assignments, and interactive element placement

**Phase 2: Design System (Manual-Specific)**
- Define the 2-color palette (black + accent) with specific usage rules
- Establish the typography hierarchy for this format — likely a subset of Doc Set 1's type system but applied to a utilitarian/institutional context rather than a brand-book context
- Design the page grid: margins, columns, consistent element positions (section header zone, activity zone, checkbox zone, margin-note zone for Brad's Mode 2 interjections)
- Create the repeating element library: checkbox style, section header treatment, "classification" stamps, warning labels, margin notes, Brad's trademark symbols
- Design the cover — this is the highest-stakes single surface
- Deliverable: Design system document with specimen pages showing typography, color usage, and element library

**Phase 3: Spread Design**
- Design each spread as a self-contained unit, working through the page map sequentially
- Cover and inside-cover/page-1 spread first (highest priority — earns the open)
- Bingo card page second (highest shareability — test photographability early)
- Fill-in / survival record page third (conversion event — must feel inviting)
- Remaining item activity spreads in order
- Back cover last (closing beat — Brad's sign-off)
- Deliverable: All 8 spreads (16 pages) designed, content integrated, ready for review

**Phase 4: Production Prep**
- Confirm all dimensions with print production
- Set up saddle-stitch imposition
- Confirm gutter margins and safe zones
- Generate print-ready files (2-color separation)
- Order proof
- Deliverable: Print-ready files + physical proof

**Key milestones/checkpoints:**
- Content architecture approved before any design work begins
- Cover design reviewed as standalone deliverable before remaining pages
- Bingo card reviewed for photographability (take a photo of the proof on a phone screen — does the joke read?)
- Physical proof reviewed for paper feel, fold, ink coverage, and readability at this size

## Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|------------|--------|------------|
| Manual feels like filler, not content | Medium | High | Strong cover design + immediately funny first spread. If the open is earned, the rest follows. Test with a cold reader: would you keep reading? |
| Brad's voice inconsistency across 16 pages | Medium | High | Write a voice guide addendum specific to the manual — Mode 1 dominance, Mode 2 only in defined positions. Review all copy against brad-copy-voice-guide.md before design. |
| Full color undermines institutional aesthetic | Low | Medium | Even with full color available, the design should reference institutional documents (OSHA manuals, employee handbooks, military field guides). Color should reinforce the bureaucratic feel, not fight it. Avoid the temptation to make it "pretty" — utilitarian is the style. |
| Activities feel redundant with item box descriptions | Medium | Medium | Item boxes describe what the item IS (Brad's retail marketing). Manual describes what to DO with it. Different frame entirely. Review for overlap and eliminate any. |
| Interactive elements feel like homework | Low | Medium | Keep fill-ins playful and low-stakes. "Days without incident" is funnier than "Rate your workplace satisfaction." The bingo card is opt-in fun, not mandatory. |
| Manual delays the mug reveal too long | Low | High | Design the manual to be browsable in 60-90 seconds — the recipient can always return to it later. It shouldn't feel like a gate blocking the finale. |
| Content from activity-guide-recs-v2 doesn't translate to Brad's voice | Medium | Medium | The activity IDEAS are voice-neutral (they work for any narrator). The framing and tone need full rewrite. Budget time for this — it's not a find-and-replace. |
| Photographed pages are unreadable on phone screens | Medium | Medium | Test typography at phone-photo resolution. Minimum 10pt for any text that needs to read in a photo. High contrast (black on cream, accent color for emphasis). |
