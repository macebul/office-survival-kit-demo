## ARTIFACT 1: DESIGN BRIEF

---

### 1. Goal

#### 1.1 The Task (What's Being Made)

- A tuck-end retail box for a 15-card deck of "workplace offense" complaint cards
- Brad Sterling's Elevate Co. branding — marketed as **"Interpersonal Feedback Delivery System"**
- Full-color print on all 6 exterior faces + tuck flap
- ~4" x 2.5" x 0.75" (working estimate, accommodating 15 business-card-format cards)
- Visual direction already established: black background, red/cream typography, amber accent cards, enforcement/bureaucratic QC aesthetic
- Production dielines and templates already exist (SVG/PDF)

#### 1.2 The Goal (What It Must Achieve)

- **Immediate goal:** Make the recipient laugh when they pick up this box in Tray 2 — the dark, aggressive, "official documentation" appearance should be funny in context alongside the gentle Cell Talk box and elegant Sweet Nothings box
- **Authority goal:** The box should look like it belongs in a government filing cabinet. The comedy is that Brad has created a formal accountability system for things like "leaving 0:03 on the microwave." The more serious the box looks, the funnier the petty offenses become.
- **Standalone goal:** Work as a funny, self-contained object even if separated from the kit — someone seeing this box on a desk should want to pick it up
- **Brand goal:** Look like a real, mass-produced compliance product from a slightly unhinged corporate enforcement division
- **Deployment goal:** Prime the recipient for how to USE the cards — the box copy should teach them that these are complaint forms you file against coworkers for specific offenses

#### 1.3 The Goal Chain

```
Box noticed in Tray 2 — stands out as the "dark/serious one" among three very different boxes (ATTENTION)
  -> Recipient picks it up, reads "Interpersonal Feedback Delivery System" and "Structured Accountability Cards" (FIRST IMPRESSION: "this is official")
    -> Reads further — realizes these are complaint cards for things like "replying 'sounds good' to an email that required a decision" (THE TURN)
      -> Opens box, reads a card front (the CHARGE) then flips to back (Brad's take) (THE PAYOFF)
        -> Immediately starts thinking about which coworker gets which card (ACTION)
```

#### 1.4 What "Success" Looks Like

- The box creates an **authority gap** — looks like an enforcement tool for serious workplace violations, contains complaint cards about leaving time on the microwave
- The recipient understands the card mechanic (CHARGE on front / Brad's commentary on back) from the box packaging before opening
- The QC badge and "AUTHORIZED FOR INTERDEPARTMENTAL USE" seal get a reaction — they signal that Brad has put this through some kind of official approval process
- When seen alongside Cell Talk (zen) and Sweet Nothings (elegant), the three boxes read as the same product line without looking remotely similar — like Brad created a different "division" for each deck
- The box is kept on a desk after the cards are used. It's an object people comment on.
- The recipient immediately identifies at least 3 specific coworkers for specific charges

### 2. Audience

#### 2.1 The Buyer (Gift Giver)

- **Who:** Someone buying a gag gift for a coworker, friend, or family member who works in an office
- **Relationship to recipient:** Close enough to know the recipient will find workplace humor funny, not offensive
- **Purchase channel:** Amazon (FBA)
- **What they're buying:** A laugh. Social currency. "I saw this and thought of you" energy.
- **The buyer never sees this box directly** — they see the outer Amazon box, the briefcase exterior, and possibly product listing photos. The Rap Sheets box is a discovery for the recipient.

#### 2.2 The Recipient (User)

- **Who:** An office worker currently "doing time"
- **Humor profile:** Appreciates dry workplace observation humor. The person who mentally catalogs every microwave offense, every "let's circle back," every tentative calendar response — and has never been given a formal way to document these crimes. Until now.
- **Before state:** Opening Tray 2 after already discovering Tray 1 items. They've seen the Cubicle Shank (tactical pen), Lifer Ink (tattoos), and Turf Tags (stickers). They're deep in the world, fully entertained, expecting more.
- **After state:** Armed with 15 complaint forms they will actually deploy. They already have a Kevin in mind.
- **Key insight:** Every card in this deck describes something the recipient has actually experienced. The humor isn't hypothetical — it's recognition. "Scheduling a meeting to discuss the results of the previous meeting" isn't a joke. It happened yesterday.

#### 2.3 Buyer vs. User Gap

- The buyer never interacts with this box directly
- All box design decisions serve the recipient's in-hand discovery experience
- The box needs to photograph well for social sharing (the recipient shows friends what they got)
- The dark, aggressive visual direction creates high contrast against the other boxes in photos — it's visually striking

### 3. Context

#### 3.1 Physical Context

- **Location:** Tray 2 of briefcase, wide back recess
- **Neighbors:** Cell Talk and Sweet Nothings — three identical-dimension tuck-end boxes side by side. Confiscated Stapler empty recess (chalk outline) also in Tray 2.
- **Discovery moment:** Tray 2 is revealed when Tray 1 is lifted. Recipient sees all three card boxes and the empty recess simultaneously.
- **Visual contrast in tray:** Three boxes with radically different visual identities sitting side by side — sage green/white (Cell Talk), black/red/amber (Rap Sheets), cream/blush pink (Sweet Nothings). This variety is intentional and correct.
- **Viewing conditions:** In-hand, close-up reading distance. Indoor lighting. The red text on black background needs to be fully legible at this scale.
- **Physical interaction:** Picked up, turned over, all faces read (the Stickers and Tats boxes prove that recipients examine every face). Opened, cards pulled out, individual cards flipped front-to-back.

#### 3.2 Experience Context (Where It Falls in the Reveal Sequence)

1. Outer Amazon box (Brad's marketing — full-color, over-the-top)
2. Briefcase exterior (matte black, duct tape handle)
3. Open briefcase -> Doc Set 1
4. Lift Doc Set 1 -> **Tray 1** (Cubicle Shank, Lifer Ink, Turf Tags)
5. Lift Tray 1 -> **Tray 2 — THIS BOX** + Cell Talk + Sweet Nothings + Confiscated Stapler recess
6. Lift Tray 2 -> Doc Set 2 on compartment cover
7. Lift compartment cover -> Hidden mug compartment (Mug Shot)

- By Tray 2, the recipient has experienced 4 layers and understands the world
- They've already seen box designs with strong personality (the bold yellow Stickers box, the refined cream Tats box, the premium Pen box)
- Comedy expectations are established — no setup needed, just payoff
- The three card boxes are the "middle act" — substantial discovery before the finale

#### 3.3 Brand Context

- **The World:** The office is a prison. Played 100% straight. "Rap sheet" = your documented history of offenses. Here, repurposed as complaint forms you file about coworkers.
- **Character:** Brad Sterling (B.S.) — sole voice on every surface
- **Brad's Mode 1 (Product Copy):** Competent, dry, institutional. This box leans HEAVILY into Mode 1 — the enforcement aesthetic is Brad at his most corporate and official. He takes this accountability system completely seriously.
- **Brad's Mode 2 (Personal Brand):** Delusional, aspirational. Will appear on the back face (Brad headshot + title + quote) — consistent with how he appears on other box backs (Tats, Stickers).
- **Comedy Rule (LOCKED):** "Format stays buttoned-up, content gets unleashed." The box looks like it dispenses official government forms. The "offenses" are about microwaving fish and saying "let's circle back."
- **Tone:** Sardonic, not cynical. Warm. The humor is recognition — everyone knows a "tentative on the calendar invite" person.
- **Brad's Division for this product:** Not yet named. Other boxes use "Territorial Compliance Division" (Stickers) and "Dermal Achievement Division" (Tats). Rap Sheets needs its own.

#### 3.4 What the Cards Actually Are (Critical for Box Copy)

The box copy must accurately represent the card mechanic. Here's what's inside:

- **15 cards**, business-card format (3.5" x 2")
- **Front (CHARGE):** A specific workplace offense — stated as a formal complaint. "Replying 'sounds good' to an email that required a decision." / "Leaving 0:03 on the microwave instead of clearing it." / "Bringing a guitar to the office party."
- **Back (BRAD):** Brad's commentary on the offense — sometimes judging, sometimes respecting the move, sometimes accidentally confessing to doing the same thing. The commentary escalates and lands a punchline.
- **Examples the box could reference or preview:**
  - CHARGE: "Scheduling a meeting to discuss the results of the previous meeting." -> BRAD: "A meeting about a meeting is just a meeting admitting it failed."
  - CHARGE: "Saying 'just to play devil's advocate' before ruining everything." -> BRAD: "The devil has a full legal team. He doesn't need you."
  - CHARGE: "Leaving 0:03 on the microwave instead of clearing it." -> BRAD: "Three seconds. You couldn't commit to three more seconds."
- **Why they work:** Every charge is immediately recognizable. Every recipient has a specific coworker in mind. Brad's commentary adds the second laugh — he's either outraged at the offense or secretly admires it.
- **Deployment:** Hand a card to the offending coworker. Leave one on their desk. Tape it to the microwave. Slide it into their mailbox. The formal bureaucratic format makes it funnier — you're not complaining, you're *filing*.

### 5. The Design Problem (Attention > Message > Action)

#### 5.1 Attention Problem — LARGELY SOLVED

The existing visual direction handles this well:
- The black/red palette naturally stands out against Cell Talk's sage and Sweet Nothings' cream
- The enforcement aesthetic is visually aggressive at small scale — bold type, high contrast
- The box reads as "the dark/serious one" of the trio — recipients will notice it

**Remaining attention concern:** Red text on black requires careful contrast management at actual print size. The QC badge and authorization seal are small details — they need to be crisp and readable.

#### 5.2 Message Problem

- **Primary message:** "These are formal complaint cards for documenting your coworkers' workplace offenses"
- The hero image previews this with the "OFFICE INFRACTION NOTICE" card showing a specific charge — this is the right approach
- "Interpersonal Feedback Delivery System" and "Structured Accountability Cards" land the premise without explaining the joke — the corporate euphemism IS the humor
- The box must convey the CHARGE/VERDICT mechanic somewhere — likely on the back face or a side panel
- The severity classification system (e.g., "HOSTILE") is an opportunity for additional humor on the box

#### 5.3 Action Problem

- The box must make recipients want to open it and go through the cards
- The sample card visible in the hero (a specific relatable charge) creates recognition — "this has happened to me"
- After reading a few cards, recipients should immediately think of specific coworkers for specific charges
- The formal complaint format invites deployment — these aren't cards you keep, they're cards you *file*

### Strategic Direction

**"The Internal Affairs Bureau."** Rap Sheets is the enforcement arm of Brad's Elevate Co. empire — the division that documents, classifies, and prosecutes workplace offenses. Where Cell Talk heals (zen affirmations) and Sweet Nothings politely destroys (elegant insults), Rap Sheets officially *charges* people.

The visual direction is already established and it's correct: this box looks like it belongs in an evidence locker, not a gift set. That severity is the comedy engine — the more it looks like an official compliance product, the funnier "Leaving 0:03 on the microwave" becomes as a prosecutable offense.

### Personality Within the Set

| Deck | Personality | Visual Tone | Emotional Register | Brad's Role |

| Sweet Nothings | The diplomat | Elegant, refined, luxurious | "Hope this finds you well" | Sophisticated stationery curator |

Rap Sheets should be the most aggressive-looking of the three — the one that feels like it came from internal affairs. This contrast makes the set funnier as a whole. Three completely different moods, one delusional founder behind all of them.

### Risks & Mitigation

| Risk | Mitigation |
|------|------------|
| Red text on black illegible at print size (4" x 2.5") | Test contrast at 100% scale. Ensure sufficient weight in typography. Use cream/white for body text, reserve red for headers/accents. |
| Box too dark — loses detail in low light and in product photos | The amber accent cards in the hero help. Ensure the back face and side panels have enough contrast. May need red or amber accent lines/borders. |
| "Enforcement" aesthetic reads as actually hostile or off-putting | The CONTENT is the relief valve. The moment someone reads a specific charge ("bringing a guitar to the office party"), the tone shifts from threatening to absurd. Box must preview at least one charge. |
| Brad's back-face appearance may clash with dark aesthetic | Brad appeared on the cream/gold Tats box as "Distinguished Honorees" and on the yellow Stickers box as "Territorial Assessment Report." The dark background may actually work better for Brad's headshot — high contrast, dramatic. Use a formal/serious Brad photo to match the enforcement tone. |
| Division name choice is wrong | Test "Interpersonal Accountability Division" against the alternatives. It should feel as natural as "Territorial Compliance Division" and "Dermal Achievement Division" — bureaucratic, euphemistic, and slightly too long. |

### What's NOT Needed

- A new visual direction — the hero image has it right
- A shared grid system across the three card boxes — they're intentionally distinct
- Extensive copy explaining the premise — the recipient is already 4 layers deep by this point
- Any reference to the OG character system (removed April 2026)
- Softening the enforcement tone — the severity is what makes the petty offenses funny
