## GOAL
*What needs to be different in the world because of this design.*

**The Task vs. The Goal:**
- **Task (what's being made):** Production-ready artwork for all exterior surfaces of a tuck-end box (~5" x 3.5" x 0.5") that holds a set of 12 office-themed territory-marking stickers
- **Goal (what changes in reality):** The recipient picks up this box, reads it, and laughs before they even open it. The box reframes a sheet of stickers as territorial contraband — something deployed, not decorated with. By the time they peel the first sticker, they're already scanning the office for a surface to claim.

**Before State -> After State:**
- **Before:** The recipient has just lifted Doc Set 1 and is seeing Tray 1 for the first time. Item boxes sit in die-cut recesses. The sticker box is an unknown. Stickers as a gift component risk reading as filler, childish, or low-value — the throwaway item to pad the count. The recipient has no reason to be excited about stickers yet.
- **After:** The recipient has picked up the yellow box, read "TURF TAGS" and "Workspace Personalization Labels," understood the joke (office stickers = territory marking), and is already imagining which sticker goes on which surface at their desk. The stickers aren't filler — they're the most deployable item in the kit. The box made that case before the stickers were even visible.

**Downstream Goal:**
- Make the recipient see stickers as territory-marking tools, not decorative filler. The box must upgrade the perceived value and comedic purpose of a sticker sheet from "nice extra" to "the thing I'll actually use first."

**Upstream Goal Chain:**
- Recipient reads box and laughs -> Opens box -> Examines stickers -> Mentally places stickers on desk surfaces -> Deploys sticker on monitor/mug/chair within 24 hours -> Coworker notices -> "Where did you get that?" conversation -> Gift-giver looks great -> Stickers appear in Amazon review photos and social media -> Product earns word-of-mouth

**What Success Looks Like:**
- The recipient picks up the Turf Tags box and reads it instead of immediately opening it
- They laugh at the dog-peeing-on-office-chair illustration before they even read the copy
- The "PEEL → STICK → HOLD COURT → ACCEPT PARADE → EXIT" instruction panel gets read aloud to whoever is nearby
- They open the box and immediately start mentally placing stickers on surfaces
- A sticker ends up on a monitor, water bottle, or desk within 24 hours
- Someone at work asks "what is that?" about a deployed sticker
- Stickers appear in Amazon review photos — the most photographable, shareable item in the kit
- The box itself is kept or displayed, not discarded

**What Failure Looks Like:**
- The recipient glances at the box, opens it, sees stickers, and says "oh, stickers" with flat energy
- The box reads as generic novelty packaging — nothing distinguishes it from any other sticker product
- The territorial concept doesn't land — the stickers feel decorative rather than subversive
- The box feels childish or low-effort compared to the Cubicle Shank box or the briefcase exterior
- Brad's voice is absent or inconsistent
- The stickers stay in the box and never get deployed

**Risk of Misalignment:**
- Over-designing the box to look "premium" or "edgy" when stickers are inherently casual and fun. The Turf Tags box should be slightly more accessible and playful than the Cubicle Shank box — stickers are a lighter, more shareable item. The yellow color and dog illustration in the current draft hit the right register.
- Treating the box as interchangeable with Lifer Ink. They share a recess and a size, but stickers mark spaces (outward, public, assertive) while tattoos mark bodies (personal, earned, inward). The design must make this distinction clear at a glance.

## AUDIENCE
*The specific humans whose state this design is trying to change.*

**Buyer vs. User:**
- **Are they the same person?** No — this is a gift product
- **The Buyer:** Already purchased. They never see the item boxes before buying. The buyer's needs were served by the Amazon listing and outer box.
- **The User:** The office worker receiving the kit. They've just opened the briefcase, absorbed the exterior, and lifted Doc Set 1. They're seeing Tray 1 for the first time — items nestled in die-cut recesses, Pelican-case style.
- **Decision Influencer:** None at this stage. Purchase is complete. This box serves the unboxing experience and post-unboxing deployment.
- **Optimizing for:** The recipient (user). Secondary optimization: the box should be photogenic enough that deployed stickers and the box itself appear in reviews and social media.

**Primary Audience: "The Lifer"**
- **Who they are:** An office worker who has been in the corporate world long enough to have a desk, a routine, and opinions about shared spaces. They know which mug is theirs. They know which chair is theirs. They have quietly territorial instincts about their corner of the open floor plan — and they know it's slightly ridiculous, but they can't help it.
- **Before State:**
  - *What they know:* They're inside the kit world. They've seen the briefcase exterior, read Brad's labels, and absorbed the office-as-prison premise. They're warmed up and looking for more.
  - *What they believe/assume:* Stickers in a gag gift are filler — the throwaway item to pad the count. They expect stickers to be generic, mildly funny, and ultimately forgettable. The sticker box is the item they're least excited about.
  - *What they're trying to do:* Quickly assessing what's in Tray 1. They're picking up items, reading boxes, and deciding what to examine more closely. The box has 3-5 seconds to earn a closer look before they move on to the next item.
- **The Gap:** Medium-high. Stickers have a perception problem in the gag gift context — they read as cheap, young, and filler. The box must close the gap between "oh, stickers" and "oh, THESE stickers." The territorial framing elevates stickers from decoration to statement.
- **What would make them say "this is for me":** The specific, accurate recognition of office territorial behavior. The person who angles their monitor to claim desk space. The person who leaves their jacket on their chair so nobody takes the spot. The box should make them feel seen in their quiet territorial habits.
- **What would make them say "this isn't for me":** Childish sticker packaging (bright primary colors, cartoon style, "fun!" energy). Overly aggressive territory-marking language that reads as genuinely hostile rather than sardonic. Generic office humor with no specific observations.
- **Hidden Agenda:** They want to mark their space at work but can't do it seriously. The stickers give them permission to do it as a joke — which is the only acceptable way to be territorial in an open floor plan. The comedy is the permission slip.
- **Skepticism / Resistance:** "Stickers? Really?" The biggest barrier is the item category itself. Every gag gift with stickers has trained recipients to expect the weakest item. The box design must work harder than any other item box to overcome this bias.

**Secondary Audience: Coworkers who see deployed stickers**
- They encounter the stickers out of context — on a monitor, a filing cabinet, a desk partition. The box establishes the brand vocabulary that the stickers carry into the world. The box's tone should prime the recipient to deploy stickers confidently, not sheepishly.

**Key Psychological Insight:**
- Stickers are the most interactive and shareable item in the entire kit. Every other item stays in or near the briefcase — the stickers physically leave and enter the workplace. This makes them the kit's most powerful word-of-mouth vehicle. But that only works if the recipient feels confident deploying them. The box's job is to frame sticker deployment as an act of quiet rebellion, sanctioned by Brad Sterling and Elevate Co. — not as a childish impulse. The box is the permission structure.

## CONTEXT
*The specific circumstances under which the audience will encounter this design.*

**Physical / Digital Environment:**
- **Where:** At home or in a break room during the unboxing. Later: at a desk when the recipient deploys stickers. The box may live on a desk or shelf afterward.
- **Medium/Device:** Physical printed box, ~5" x 3.5" x 0.5". Held in one hand. Tuck-end opening. Full color exterior on paperboard.
- **Environmental conditions:** Indoor, well-lit. During unboxing, the briefcase is open and Tray 1 is visible. The recipient is picking up items from die-cut recesses — a tactile, exploratory moment. Likely someone watching (gift-giving context).

**Sought vs. Served:**
- **Sought — within a sought experience.** The recipient is actively exploring Tray 1. They're picking up items. The box is sought in the sense that they chose to pick it up — but the concept is served. They don't know what "Turf Tags" means until they read the box.
- **Implication:** The box name and front face must communicate the concept quickly. "Turf Tags" alone is not self-explanatory — the dog illustration and "MARK YOUR TERRITORY" tagline close that gap in the current draft.

**Attention Available:**
- **Time window:** Short initial scan (3-5 seconds while picking it up from the tray), then extended if hooked (15-30 seconds of reading). The box competes with the other items in the same tray (Lifer Ink, Cubicle Shank) and the overall reveal moment.
- **Competing stimuli:** Other item boxes, the Pelican-case reveal moment, and the recipient's general excitement about seeing Tray 1 for the first time. Each box gets a fraction of the attention budget.
- **Decision mode:** Exploratory. No purchase decision. "Which item do I look at next?" is the only decision happening. The box needs to earn a second look in a crowded field.

**Emotional / Mental State:**
- Delight, curiosity, slight overwhelm. Tray 1 is the first layer of items — the recipient is seeing the kit's contents for the first time. The box that communicates fastest and funniest wins the most attention.

**Social Setting:**
- Likely with others present. The sticker concept is inherently social — "look at these, where should I put this one?" Stickers invite conversation and group participation. The box should feel like something you'd hold up and show someone, not something you'd read quietly.

**Technical / Physical Constraints:**
- **Box type:** Tuck-end (top tuck flap)
- **Dimensions:** ~5" x 3.5" x 0.5" (working estimate — confirm with production)
- **Print:** Full color exterior, all surfaces
- **Material:** Paperboard, weight TBD — must feel substantial for its size
- **Finish:** Consistent with other item boxes in the kit
- **Contents:** Sticker strip(s) — flat, thin. The box is mostly air. The 0.5" depth is for die-cut recess fit, not sticker thickness.
- **Recess fit:** Must fit precisely in Tray 1's die-cut recess
- **Viewing distance:** Initially from above (looking into the open tray, ~18-24"), then in hand (~12-18")

**Context Shifts:**
- **Primary:** In-tray discovery — box sitting in the die-cut recess, viewed from above
- **Secondary:** In-hand examination — picked up, turned over, read closely
- **Tertiary:** Desk display — the box may live on a desk or shelf after stickers are deployed
- **Quaternary:** Amazon review photos — the box may appear in customer photos alongside deployed stickers
- **Most constrained:** In-tray from above. Only the top face is visible. Product name, visual identity, and enough character to distinguish from Lifer Ink must read from ~18-24" above.

## EXISTING DESIGN: SURFACE-BY-SURFACE DOCUMENTATION

### Color & Visual Identity
The box uses a bold **yellow** background with **black** typography and illustrations throughout. This is a significant departure from the matte black briefcase and the darker tones of other kit elements — and it works. The yellow reads as "caution tape" / "warning sign" / "construction zone" — all territory-marking vocabulary. It also ensures the box is immediately visually distinct from Lifer Ink in the shared tray.

### Front Face (the face visible when sitting in the tray and when held upright)
**Current design:**
- **Header:** "ELEVATE CO. / Territorial Compliance Division"
- **Product name:** "TURF TAGS™" — large, dominant, bold sans-serif
- **Illustration:** Black silhouette of a dog lifting its leg on an office chair — the single strongest visual on the box. Immediate, funny, needs zero explanation.
- **Tagline (top right):** "MARK YOUR TERRITORY."
- **Subheader:** "Workspace Personalization Labels" (Brad's corporate rebrand)
- **Subtitle:** "Because Asking Nicely Didn't Work." (italic)
- **Red horizontal divider line** separating the product identity from the fine print zone
- **Six stars** (rating element)
- **Fine print block:**
  - "CONTAINS: 12 ADHESIVE PROPERTY MARKERS"
  - "FOR SURFACE APPLICATION ONLY*"
  - "DEPLOYMENT NOTICE: Application of these markers constitutes a legally binding claim.†"
  - "EC-WPL-001" (product code)
- **Footnotes (right side):**
  - "* Surfaces only. We've had this conversation."
  - "† It does not. Act like it does."
  - "This product has been used as evidence in 3 workplace disputes and 1 divorce."

**Evaluation:** This face is strong. The hierarchy is correct — "TURF TAGS" dominates, "Workspace Personalization Labels" plays the Brad layer underneath. The dog illustration is the single best visual decision on the box — it communicates the concept instantly, is genuinely funny, and works at every viewing distance. The fine print escalation (from "12 adhesive property markers" to "3 workplace disputes and 1 divorce") follows Brad's Format/Content Escalation pattern. The red line creates a clear zone separation. "Territorial Compliance Division" is a nice Brad-ism — a department that doesn't exist, presented as if it always has.

## NAMING HIERARCHY ANALYSIS

The box currently handles the dual naming system well, but this is the most important design decision, so it deserves explicit documentation.

### Current Hierarchy (Front Face)
1. **"TURF TAGS™"** — largest text, dominant position, the name you read first
2. **"Workspace Personalization Labels"** — smaller, subordinate, Brad's corporate rebrand
3. **"Because Asking Nicely Didn't Work."** — italic subtitle, comedic kicker

### Why This Hierarchy Is Correct

The **prison-world term** ("Turf Tags") should dominate the item box because:
- The recipient is inside the kit world at this point. They've been inducted by the briefcase exterior and Doc Set 1. The prison vocabulary is the native language of this product.
- "Turf Tags" is punchy, immediate, and communicates the concept in two words. It earns the pickup at tray-viewing distance.
- "Workspace Personalization Labels" is the Brad layer — the joke is that he's repackaged something primal as a corporate solution. If Brad's name dominates, you lose the punch and get the joke last instead of first.

The **corporate term** ("Workspace Personalization Labels") should play the subordinate role because:
- It's the second beat of a two-beat joke. First you laugh at the concept (Turf Tags), then you laugh at Brad for sanitizing it.
- If it were dominant, the box would read as boring first and funny second. You'd lose people at the 3-5 second attention window.
- The corporate term works harder as a contrast. It's funnier BECAUSE it sits below the prison term — the gap between the two is visible in the visual hierarchy itself.

### Where the Corporate Term Could Lead
On the **outer Amazon box**, where Brad is fully in character as a retail marketer, "Workspace Personalization Labels™" could lead. That's Brad's storefront. But inside the kit, the prison name should dominate.

### Recommendation
**Keep the current hierarchy.** It's working. "TURF TAGS" is the name. "Workspace Personalization Labels" is the Brad layer. This matches how Cubicle Shank / Executive Tactical Stylus works throughout the kit.

## Strategic Direction

**Recommended approach:** Treat the v01 draft as 90% there. Resolve the refinement opportunities above, lock the sticker count, finalize sticker selections, and move to production prep. The box does not need a rethink — it needs polish and verification.

**Design principles (carried forward from v01):**
- **Yellow = territorial.** The color is the brand of this specific item box. Don't dilute it.
- **The dog is the hero.** The illustration carries the front face. Protect its visual prominence.
- **Every surface has one job.** Front = identity. Back = Brad's assessment. Top = instructions. Sides = one-liner jokes. Bottom = thesis. Tuck = deployment order. No surface is wasted.
- **Sticker-appropriate energy.** Slightly more accessible than the Cubicle Shank box. The yellow, the dog, and the instruction panel all skew playful — this is correct for stickers.
