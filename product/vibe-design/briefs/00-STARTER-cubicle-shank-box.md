<goal>
Create comprehensive Design Briefs that give designers crystal-clear direction before they begin work. Through a thorough but efficient interview process, uncover everything the designer needs to know — organized around three foundational principles:

1. **Goal** — What needs to be different in the world because of this design? What is the downstream goal, and what upstream goals does it serve?
2. **Audience** — Who are the specific humans whose state this design is trying to change? What is their before state? Are the buyer and user the same person?
3. **Context** — Under what circumstances will the audience encounter this design? Is this sought or served? How much attention is realistically available?

The final Design Brief should:
- Translate vague requests into precise understanding
- Define the real goal (the change in the world) — not just the task (the deliverable)
- Map the upstream goal chain so the designer understands where this fits
- Build a rich picture of the audience's before state, psychology, and hidden motivations
- Identify whether the buyer and user are the same person, and who to optimize for
- Define the context with enough specificity to make real design decisions
- Identify the causal chain of problems (what must work first) using Attention > Message > Action
- Flag open questions and assumptions that need validation
- Give the designer confidence to execute without second-guessing

After the Design Brief, generate a separate Recommended Approach artifact with strategic direction and a detailed action plan.
</goal>

<interview_me_first>
Before creating the Design Brief, interview the designer to understand the project thoroughly:

**Your Approach:**
- Ask simple, easy-to-answer questions (yes/no or 1-2 word answers preferred when possible)
- Present questions in numbered lists (one question per number) and restart numbering each time at 1
- When appropriate, apply letters to possible responses (A, B, C, etc.) but make it clear the user can answer with their own text if they prefer
- Try to make your questions VERY simple to answer. Better to ask more simple questions than fewer complex ones
- Ask 1-5 questions at a time as appropriate
- Continue until you have complete understanding of the project
- Be adaptive—let the conversation flow naturally based on responses
- Skip questions that have already been answered or are obviously not relevant
</interview_me_first>

## Phase 1: Project Basics
- What type of design project is this?
- What's being requested in simple terms?
- Who requested this?
- Is this a new project or a revision/update?

## Phase 2: GOAL
- Task vs. goal distinction
- Before state → After state
- Downstream goal
- Upstream goal chain (complex projects)
- Success and failure criteria

## Phase 3: AUDIENCE
- Buyer vs. User split
- Primary audience persona
- Before State (what they know, believe, are trying to do)
- The Gap (small nudge or big lift?)
- Psychological depth
- Key psychological insight

## Phase 4: CONTEXT
- Physical/digital environment
- Sought vs. Served
- Attention available
- Emotional/mental state
- Technical/physical constraints

## Phase 5: Problem Identification & Prioritization
- All problems listed
- Attention > Message > Action causal chain

## Phase 6: Messages & Constraints
- Prioritized messages
- Budget, timeline, format, brand/legal constraints

- Gaps to resolve
- Assumptions to validate

<key_reminders>
- Goal ≠ Task. Map the goal chain. The audience arrives with a before state. The gap is the design problem.
- Buyer and user are often different people. Context determines what's possible. Sought vs. Served changes everything.
- Find the causal chain. Flag what you don't know. Scale depth to complexity.
- Two artifacts, always. Bullet points, not paragraphs.
</key_reminders>

## MY PROJECT

I need a Design Brief for the **Cubicle Shank item box** — the individual packaging for the tactical pen inside the Office Survival Kit gag gift.

### What This Surface Is

A small tray-and-sleeve (matchbox style) box that holds the tactical pen. It sits in Tray 1 of the briefcase, in the narrow front recess (centered). This is the longest box in the kit and the only one that uses the matchbox construction.

### Physical Specs

- **Box type:** Tray-and-sleeve (matchbox style) — NOT tuck-end
- **Box dimensions:** ~6.5" x 1.75" x 1.0" (WORKING ESTIMATE)
- **Location:** Tray 1, narrow recess (front, centered)
- **Print:** Full color exterior, Brad's corporate branding

### Item Inside

- **Item name:** Cubicle Shank
- **Brad's rebrand:** Executive Tactical Stylus™
- **What it is:** A tactical pen
- **Item description:** Your pen. Your reliable tool. The one thing between you and total surrender to the system.

### Brand Context

**The World:** The office is a prison — played straight. "Shank" = your pen, your reliable tool. Affectionate tone.

**Character:** Brad Sterling (B.S.) — sole voice. Two modes: (1) competent product copy in straight-faced formats, (2) delusional personal branding. This box carries Brad's branding.

**Comedy rule:** Format stays buttoned-up, content gets unleashed.

**Tone:** Sardonic, not cynical. Warm under the dark humor.

### The Item Box's Role in the Experience

- Discovered when Tray 1 is revealed (after lifting Doc Set 1)
- Part of the Pelican-case reveal moment — items sitting in die-cut recesses
- Must work as a standalone funny object (someone might pick it up and read it)
- Must communicate what's inside while maintaining the prison-office world
- The matchbox slide mechanism is itself a moment — pulling the inner tray out

### Type of Project

- A. Packaging

### Complexity

- B. Moderate (small surface area, established brand voice, clear constraints)
