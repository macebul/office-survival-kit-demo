## GOAL
*What needs to be different in the world because of this design.*

**The Task vs. The Goal:**
- **Task (what's being made):** Production-ready artwork for all six exterior surfaces of a matte black clamshell briefcase, plus a removable belly band
- **Goal (what changes in reality):** The recipient picks up the briefcase, starts reading, and doesn't stop. Every surface rewards attention. By the time they open the lid, they're already laughing and showing it to someone else.

**Before State → After State:**
- **Before:** The recipient has just pulled a matte black box out of the Amazon mailer. They know it's a gag gift (the outer box told them). They're curious but haven't committed emotionally yet. The briefcase is a black box — literally. It could be cheap. It could be forgettable. They don't yet know how deep this goes.
- **After:** The recipient is turning the briefcase over, reading every label, showing individual elements to the person next to them. They're saying "oh my god, look at this one." The briefcase has earned its spot — it feels like a real object with a real history, not a novelty wrapper. They believe the inside will be worth the attention.

**Downstream Goal:**
- Make the recipient read every surface. The briefcase exterior must reward close inspection on every face — not just the lid. Each label, stamp, and sticker should feel like a discovery, not decoration.

**Upstream Goal Chain:**
- Recipient reads every surface → Recipient is fully bought into the world before opening → Opening the lid feels like an event, not a chore → Every interior layer lands harder because the briefcase set the tone → Recipient shares the experience (photos, showing coworkers) → Gift-giver looks great → Product earns reviews, repeat purchases, and word-of-mouth

**What Success Looks Like:**
- The recipient flips the briefcase over to look at the bottom
- They read aloud from the Nutrition Facts to whoever is nearby
- They notice the PLU sticker and either laugh or look confused (both are wins)
- They pause on the MONDAY edge and react
- The briefcase gets photographed before it's even opened
- In reviews, people describe specific exterior elements by name

**What Failure Looks Like:**
- The recipient glances at the lid and immediately opens it — the exterior was wallpaper, not content
- Labels look like clip art or generic novelty packaging
- The comedy reads as mean-spirited, cynical, or try-hard rather than sardonic and warm
- The institutional/scrapbook aesthetic looks messy or cheap rather than intentional and handmade
- Elements compete with each other instead of creating a reading order
- Text is too small to read at arm's length or too dense to invite engagement

**Risk of Misalignment:**
- Over-designing individual labels could undermine the "assembled under difficult conditions" feel. Each element should look good, but the *collection* should look accumulated, not art-directed. If every sticker is perfectly placed and perfectly aged, the handmade fiction breaks.

## AUDIENCE
*The specific humans whose state this design is trying to change.*

**Buyer vs. User:**
- **Are they the same person?** No — this is a gift product
- **The Buyer:** A coworker, friend, or family member shopping on Amazon for a funny/thoughtful gift. They've already purchased by the time the briefcase is seen — the outer Amazon box sold them. They care about the recipient's reaction.
- **The User:** The office worker receiving the gift. They are "doing time" in a corporate environment. They didn't choose this product — it arrived.
- **Decision Influencer:** Amazon reviews and listing photos. The buyer saw the briefcase exterior in listing images before purchasing. The exterior *already sold the product once* — now it needs to deliver on that promise in person.
- **Optimizing for:** The recipient (user). The buyer already converted. The briefcase exterior now serves the in-person reveal experience. Secondary optimization: the briefcase should photograph well, because the recipient sharing photos drives future sales.

**Primary Audience: "The Lifer"**
- **Who they are:** An office worker — any age, any industry, any level — who has been in the corporate world long enough to have opinions about meetings, printers, reply-all, and Kevin. They work in an environment with cubicles (or open plan), fluorescent lighting, shared kitchens, and HR departments. They're not miserable — they're adapted. They cope through humor.
- **Before State:**
  - *What they know:* They know this is a gag gift. The Amazon box already established the tone. They're expecting funny, but they don't know how funny or how deep it goes.
  - *What they believe/assume:* Most novelty gifts are surface-level — one joke repeated on every surface, cheap execution, disposable. They expect to get the gist in 5 seconds and move on. They assume the outside is a wrapper, not content.
  - *What they're trying to do:* They're opening a gift, probably with someone watching. They want to enjoy the moment and react authentically. They want to feel seen — like whoever gave them this actually understands their daily experience.
- **The Gap:** Medium. They're primed to laugh (the Amazon box did its job), but they expect the briefcase to be the packaging, not the show. The gap is between "this is a funny box" and "this is a world I'm stepping into." The briefcase needs to upgrade their expectation from novelty to experience.
- **What would make them say "this is for me":** Specific, accurate office observations. Not generic "I hate Mondays" humor — the kind of details that make them say "how do they know about the printer." Real formats (nutrition labels, asset tags, QC stickers) used with real institutional precision. The feeling that this was made by someone who has also done time.
- **What would make them say "this isn't for me":** Crude humor. Mean-spirited jokes about specific people or demographics. Sloppy execution that reads as lazy rather than intentional. Anything that feels mass-produced or template-driven. Over-explained jokes.
- **Hidden Agenda:** They want to feel validated. The office is absurd, they know it's absurd, and nobody ever says it out loud in a format this polished. They want permission to laugh at something they endure every day — and they want that laughter to come from recognition, not mockery. They also want to show this to their coworkers, which means it needs to be funny enough to share but safe enough to have on a desk.
- **Skepticism / Resistance:** "Is this just another mug that says 'I survived another meeting that could have been an email'?" They've seen lazy office humor. The briefcase needs to signal within the first 3 seconds that this is different.

**Secondary Audience: The Gift-Giver (watching the reveal)**
- They're watching the recipient open the gift. Their emotional state is invested — they chose this. They want to see the reaction. They need the briefcase to generate a visible, audible reaction. If the recipient reads something and laughs, the gift-giver wins. If the recipient politely says "oh, that's funny" and sets it down, the gift-giver loses. The gift-giver is also the person most likely to leave an Amazon review.

**Key Psychological Insight:**
- The recipient doesn't expect the packaging to be the product. Every novelty gift they've received trained them to look inside immediately. The briefcase exterior's job is to interrupt that habit — to be so dense with discoverable detail that they slow down, flip it over, and read. The comedy must be layered: something lands on the first glance, more lands on closer inspection, and the deepest jokes reward the person who reads every line of the Nutrition Facts. This is how you turn a gag gift into an experience.

## CONTEXT
*The specific circumstances under which the audience will encounter this design.*

**Physical / Digital Environment:**
- **Where:** A desk, kitchen table, or break room. Possibly at a birthday/holiday gathering with coworkers present. Well-lit indoor space.
- **Medium/Device:** Physical object — a 14" x 11.5" x 5.0" matte black paperboard briefcase, held in hands or sitting on a surface at arm's length (~18-24" viewing distance)
- **Environmental conditions:** Likely social — someone is watching. Could be a 1-on-1 gift exchange or a group setting (office party, holiday gathering). Moderate noise. The recipient has enough space and light to read small text if they choose to look closely.

**Sought vs. Served:**
- **Hybrid — but closer to Sought.** The recipient knows a gift is coming. They pulled the briefcase out of the Amazon box. They're looking at it on purpose. But individual elements on secondary surfaces (bottom, edges) are served — the recipient must be compelled to flip the case over or look at the sides. The lid and front face are sought. Everything else must earn its attention.
- **Implication:** The lid/front can assume some goodwill and curiosity. But the back, bottom, and edges need elements that are visible enough to invite discovery. The MONDAY ransom note on the left edge and the travel sticker cluster on the right edge both serve this function — they're visually distinct enough to catch peripheral attention.

**Attention Available:**
- **Time window:** Extended — but in bursts. The recipient has the briefcase in hand and no competing task. But the social pressure of a gift-opening means they'll initially scan quickly (5-10 seconds on the lid/front), then deepen if hooked. Total exterior engagement could be 1-5 minutes if the design earns it. Many labels will be re-read later in private.
- **Competing stimuli:** The social setting (people watching), the urge to open it and see what's inside, and potentially other gifts. The inside of the box is the briefcase exterior's biggest competitor — the design must delay the urge to open.
- **Decision mode:** Experiential / discovery. No purchase decision happening. The recipient is in receive-and-enjoy mode.

**Emotional / Mental State:**
- Anticipation, mild excitement, social awareness (people are watching). Slight vulnerability — receiving a gift is a performance. They want to react well. They're primed to laugh but also self-conscious. If the humor is too edgy or too niche, they might not react in front of others.

**Social Setting:**
- Almost always with others present. This changes behavior significantly: reactions will be somewhat performative. The recipient may read things aloud. Labels that work as read-aloud moments ("FRAGILE (EMOTIONALLY)") have disproportionate impact. Dense text blocks (Nutrition Facts) may be read silently first, then shared selectively.

**Technical / Physical Constraints:**
- **Material:** 24pt-32pt rigid paperboard, matte black exterior, solid board construction (NOT corrugated)
- **Print:** Full exterior coverage. Applied elements get subtle satin finish. No glossy surfaces.
- **Color palette:** Matte black (box), aged cream/kraft (labels), red (rubber stamps), gray (duct tape handle), light gray/silver (printed clasps). No additional colors on the box itself. Stickers/labels may use their own colors (travel stickers, QC sticker white).
- **Viewing distance:** Arm's length for reading (~18-24"). Labels must be legible at this distance. The Nutrition Facts label will be read at ~12-14" (held closer).
- **Die-cut:** Handle is a die-cut arch with duct tape wrap. This is a physical 3D element, not printed.
- **Magnets:** 4 neodymium disc magnets along the front edge (invisible from exterior). They affect the front face layout — clasp graphics must align with magnet positions.
- **Closure:** 4-magnet system + belly band (belly band is removed on first open and not reattached)
- **Edge visibility:** Top edge of the box shows raw board layers where lid meets body — intentional and on-brand

**Context Shifts:**
- **Primary context:** In-person gift reveal (social, first impression, performative)
- **Secondary context:** Desk display afterward. The briefcase lives on a shelf or desk. Labels are re-read by coworkers who come by. New jokes are discovered over time. This is the long-tail value — the briefcase becomes a conversation piece.
- **Tertiary context:** Amazon listing photos. The briefcase exterior IS the product in listing images. The design must photograph well and communicate quality + humor at thumbnail scale. The lid and front face are the most photographed surfaces.
- **Most constrained context:** Amazon listing thumbnail (~300x300px). At that scale, only the lid's main label, the CLEARED FOR OFFICE USE stamp, and the overall briefcase silhouette are legible. These elements must carry the product identity at thumbnail resolution.

## PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION & PRIORITIZATION

**All problems this design must solve:**
- Communicate "this is premium, not cheap" through material feel and design precision
- Communicate "this is funny" through copy and comedic design choices
- Establish the office-as-prison world without ever explaining it
- Create a reading order across 22+ elements on 6 surfaces so nothing is missed
- Make every surface worth discovering (prevent the "I only looked at the top" problem)
- Balance institutional precision (Brad's product copy) with handmade/accumulated feel (the collection of labels, stamps, stickers)
- Keep text legible at arm's length across varying label sizes
- Fit 5+ elements on the front face within a 5.0" height constraint
- Make the Nutrition Facts label work on a 5.0"-tall back face
- Photograph well for Amazon listing images at multiple scales
- Remove all OG character elements — the exterior is pure Brad/Elevate Co.
- Integrate belly band as a cohesive first-touch element despite TBD copy status
- Make the handle feel intentionally homemade, not accidentally cheap
- Create an aesthetic that's "somewhere between serious and scrappy" — intentional and considered, but with improvised character

**Causal Chain (Attention > Message > Action):**

1. **ATTENTION: The briefcase must look like it's worth examining** *(If we fail here, nothing else matters)*
   - The recipient just pulled this out of the Amazon box. In the first 2 seconds, the overall object needs to register as "interesting" not "cheap." The matte black finish, the aged cream label, the red stamp, and the duct tape handle create a visual contrast that signals intentionality. If it looks like a printed novelty box, they'll open it immediately and skip the exterior entirely.
   - Competing with: the urge to open it and the expectation that packaging is disposable

2. **MESSAGE (Primary): This is a world, not a product**
   - The lid communicates the product identity (OFFICE SURVIVAL KIT) and the world it lives in (institutional labels, evidence tags, CLEARED FOR OFFICE USE). These must register in the first 5-10 seconds. If the recipient reads the main label and the evidence tag, they're in the world. The vocabulary (case number, seized, chain of custody) does the worldbuilding without explanation.
   - The audience's before state: they expect a one-joke novelty product. The message must upgrade that expectation immediately.

3. **MESSAGE (Secondary): Every surface has something for you**
   - The side edges (MONDAY, travel stickers), back face (Nutrition Facts), and bottom (NOTICE, PLU sticker) must signal their presence. This isn't about reading everything at once — it's about the recipient noticing enough visual activity on non-primary surfaces to flip the case over. The MONDAY ransom note and the travel sticker colors are visible even from peripheral angles. The back face's dense label cluster is visible when the case is picked up.

4. **MESSAGE (Tertiary): The details reward close reading**
   - The Nutrition Facts, evidence tag, IT Asset Tag, and Inspected By sticker all have layered comedy — the first line is plausible, each subsequent line escalates. This is the depth that turns a gag gift into a keeper. If the design doesn't invite close reading (type too small, too dense, insufficient white space), the best copy in the product goes unread.

5. **ACTION: Share it — read it aloud, show it to someone, photograph it**
   - The desired action isn't "buy" (already happened) or "open" (that's inevitable). The desired action is *share*. The recipient reads something aloud. Shows a specific label to the person next to them. Takes a photo before opening. This is the action that generates reviews, social posts, and word-of-mouth. The design must create "shareable moments" — individual elements that work as standalone jokes when read aloud or photographed.

# Recommended Approach: Briefcase Exterior

## Strategic Direction

**Recommended overall approach:**
The briefcase exterior is a dense, layered reading experience disguised as packaging. The design approach should treat each surface as an editorial layout — every element positioned with intentional hierarchy, reading order, and breathing room. The overall aesthetic is "institutional scrapbook" — individual elements are precise and format-correct (FDA labels, evidence tags, QC stickers), but the collection looks accumulated over time, as if this briefcase has passed through several departments and picked up stamps and stickers along the way.

The design should work at three zoom levels: (1) across-the-room silhouette — matte black briefcase with a cream label and duct tape handle, reads as "intentional and slightly absurd"; (2) arm's length — main label text, red stamp, and element distribution across surfaces are visible, inviting closer inspection; (3) reading distance — every line of copy, every sub-field on every label, rewards attention.

**Rationale:**
- **Given the goal:** The briefcase must delay opening and reward surface examination. This means each surface needs a clear anchor element (the thing you see first) and discoverable depth (the things you find when you look closer). The design can't be uniform — it needs visual peaks and valleys.
- **Given the audience's before state:** They expect a novelty wrapper. The design must signal "this is content" within 2 seconds. The main label's aged kraft paper, torn edges, and stencil typography — combined with the red stamp and evidence tag — create enough visual density to interrupt the "just open it" reflex.
- **Given the context:** Social gift-opening with a secondary life as a desk display. The design needs "read-aloud moments" (FRAGILE EMOTIONALLY, VOID REGARDLESS) that are visible at arm's length, and "deep-read moments" (Nutrition Facts, Evidence Tag chain of custody) that reward private close inspection later.

**Design principles for this project:**
- **Institutional precision, accumulated disorder.** Each element is internally well-designed. The collection looks organically placed. Stickers are slightly angled. Stamps overlap edges. Labels aren't perfectly aligned to each other. But nothing looks sloppy — it looks like a briefcase that has been through processing.
- **Three zoom levels.** Silhouette (across the room), scan (arm's length), read (in hand). Every surface should offer something at each level.
- **Comedy through format fidelity.** The labels must look like real labels. The Nutrition Facts must look like real FDA formatting. The evidence tag must look like a real chain-of-custody document. The comedy comes from the content filling those formats, not from the formats being silly.
- **Surface-level autonomy.** Each surface should work as a standalone composition. Someone who only sees the back face should still get a complete, funny experience. Surfaces shouldn't depend on each other for jokes to land.
- **Restraint on aging/distressing.** The briefcase is "slightly worn," not post-apocalyptic. Aging should be subtle — muted colors, slight texture, worn label edges — not heavy grunge or splatter. The box should look like it's been in an office for a year, not recovered from a disaster.
- **Photography-first for primary surfaces.** The lid and front face will be the most-photographed angles. These compositions must work as standalone images at multiple resolutions, including Amazon thumbnail (~300px).

## Action Plan

**Phase 1: Layout & Composition**
- Create 6 artboards at production dimensions (see Exterior Spec v3, Section 12)
- Place all 19+ elements on their assigned surfaces using the Exterior Spec as a position guide
- Resolve Fit Flag issues: front face element density, Nutrition Facts height on back face, MONDAY clearance on left edge
- Establish the reading order hierarchy on each surface (anchor element → secondary → discoverable details)
- Define the "imperfection grid" — how much offset, angle, and overlap for stamps/stickers
- Deliverable: Flat layout proof (all 6 surfaces, elements positioned, no final typography or textures yet)

**Phase 2: Typography & Label Design**
- Design each label/element as a self-contained unit using the copy from Exterior Copy v5
- Select or create fonts: stencil/military for main label, monospace/typewriter for evidence tag and asset tag, clean sans-serif for QC sticker, FDA-standard format for Nutrition Facts, ransom note mixed type for MONDAY
- Size all text for legibility at intended viewing distance (arm's length for most, reading distance for dense labels)
- Design the Nutrition Facts label in authentic FDA format — confirm it fits the 5.0" back face
- Design the evidence tag with chain-of-custody layout
- Design ransom note MONDAY with individual letter treatments
- Design travel sticker cluster with distinct colors per sticker
- Deliverable: Individual element artwork (each label/stamp/sticker as a separate asset, production-ready)

**Phase 3: Surface & Material Treatment**
- Develop the matte black base texture (subtle, not flat — slight surface variation)
- Develop the aged cream/kraft paper texture for labels (yellowed, slightly stained, worn edges)
- Develop the torn-edge treatment for the main label
- Develop red rubber stamp distress texture (imperfect ink coverage, faded areas)
- Develop satin finish differentiation — applied elements should visually separate from the matte surface
- Design the duct tape handle wrapping pattern and gray tape texture
- Design the printed clasp graphics (light gray/silver on black)
- Deliverable: Full textured compositions (all 6 surfaces with final materials applied)

**Phase 4: Belly Band**
- Confirm belly band copy (currently TBD/under review)
- Determine wrap orientation (vertical vs. horizontal) with production
- Design belly band as a separate component — both outer surfaces
- Ensure belly band design works with and reveals the briefcase exterior when removed
- Deliverable: Belly band artwork (separate from main briefcase artboards)

**Phase 5: Production Prep & Review**
- Create die-cut template for handle cutout
- Mark magnet positions on front face (for manufacturer reference, not printed)
- Create print-ready files with bleed, crop marks, and fold lines
- Generate a photography mockup (composite rendering showing all surfaces) for internal review
- Conduct a "read-through" test: can someone read every element at arm's length? Is anything too small?
- Review all copy against Exterior Copy v5 for accuracy (character-by-character proof)
- Deliverable: Production-ready file package + photography mockup

**Key milestones/checkpoints:**
- Layout proof approval (before investing in detailed typography and textures)
- Nutrition Facts fit confirmation (the single highest-risk layout element)
- Front face density confirmation (5 elements + handle + clasps on 5.0" face)
- Belly band copy sign-off (blocks Phase 4)
- Physical proof from manufacturer (first real-world validation)

## Risks & Mitigations

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|------|-----------|--------|------------|
| Nutrition Facts label doesn't fit 5.0" back face in authentic FDA format | Medium | High | Design the label first; if it doesn't fit vertically, arrange supporting labels (Ingredients, Best By, etc.) beside it rather than below. Horizontal cluster layout is already noted as acceptable in Exterior Spec. |
| Front face too crowded with 5 elements + handle + clasps | Medium | High | Spread small labels (IT Asset Tag, Inspected By, FRAGILE) into corners using full 14" width. Test at production scale before finalizing. Fit Flag already raised. |
| "Aged/worn" finish looks cheap rather than intentional | Medium | High | Restraint is key. Use subtle texture and muted tones, not heavy grunge. The matte black base should be clean — aging applies only to applied elements (labels, stamps). Get a physical proof early. |
| Belly band copy not finalized in time | High | Medium | Design the briefcase exterior as a complete experience without the belly band. The belly band adds value but shouldn't be a dependency for the exterior design to work. |
| Duct tape handle looks flimsy or fake in production | Medium | Medium | Specify tape texture and wrap pattern clearly in the production brief. Request a handle sample from the manufacturer before full production. |
| Print registration can't hold the "stamp overlapping label edge" effect precisely | Low | Medium | Design the overlap with enough tolerance that ±2mm registration drift still looks intentional. The stamp is supposed to look hastily applied — slight misalignment is on-brand. |
| Amazon thumbnail legibility — too much detail, nothing reads at 300px | Low | High | The lid's main label ("OFFICE SURVIVAL KIT") and the red stamp are the only elements that must read at thumbnail. Design these at high contrast. Everything else is bonus detail. |
| Text too small to read at arm's length on dense labels | Medium | High | Set a minimum type size floor (8pt absolute minimum, 10pt preferred for body text on labels). Test by printing at 100% scale and reading at 24". |
| Exterior Spec v3 and Copy v5 have OG element discrepancy | Low | Low | Flag for cleanup. The brief assumes no OG elements. Remove Elements 22-23 from the next Exterior Spec revision. |
