# Comedy Writing Rules — Office Survival Kit

> These rules apply to ALL product copy in this project. Use the `comedy-writing` skill for the full reference. These rules are the enforcement layer.
> **Last Updated:** July 22, 2026

## Mandatory: Invoke the `comedy-writing` skill before writing ANY product copy

If the task involves writing, editing, or reviewing text that will appear on a physical product surface (packaging, cards, labels, booklets, trays, boxes), the `comedy-writing` skill MUST be loaded first.

---

## Hard Rules (Never Break These)

### World Integrity
1. **Never acknowledge the office/prison comparison as a metaphor.** The office IS a prison. Nobody thinks this is clever. Nobody winks. The moment anyone says "it's like a prison" the world breaks.
2. **Use the prison/office vocabulary as the default language.** Stepping outside it should feel like stepping out of character.
3. **Tone is sardonic, not cynical.** Warm underneath the dark humor. This is a gift for a coworker you like.

### Brad Sterling Voice
4. **Product Copy Brad is competent.** His institutional-format humor (FDA labels, QC stickers, asset tags) should be genuinely funny. Don't sabotage his product copy to make him look dumb.
5. **Personal Brand Brad is sincere.** Zero self-awareness. Never winks. "Premium" is his comma. He trademarks everything.
6. **The gap between the two Brads is the joke.** Same person, competent product copy AND delusional self-promotion. The reader sees the contradiction. Brad never does.
7. **Brad has never done office time.** He's adjacent to this world, fluent in its language but not a veteran. He's the guy who opens a military surplus store without having served.

### Subtext
8. **Every joke needs a truth underneath it.** Before writing, name the specific office observation being commented on. If you can't articulate the subtext, the joke has no foundation.
9. **Subtext is never stated.** The moment you spell out what the joke means, you've killed it. The reader discovers the hidden message — that's what makes it land.
10. **Target the system, not the reader.** Comfort the afflicted, afflict the comfortable. If the reader could feel attacked rather than seen, the target is wrong.

### Comedy Mechanics
11. **Every piece of copy needs at least 2 of 6 humor elements:** Clever, Bizarre, Cruel, Naughty, Recognizable, Cute. "Recognizable" should almost always be one of them.
12. **Escalation pattern:** Line 1 plausible, Line 2 slight edge, Line 3 clearly a joke, Line 4 full absurd. **Format never cracks.**
13. **Specificity over puns.** Humor comes from specific details and observations, not wordplay.

### Kill On Sight
14. **No puns.** Brad is dry.
15. **No exclamation points in product copy.** Keep it institutional and flat.
16. **No winking at the reader.** No "get it?" No nudge-nudge. No "if you know what we mean." No "technically."
17. **No actual workplace advice.** This is comedy, not a self-help product.
18. **No making Brad self-aware.** He never realizes the contradiction.
19. **No generic insults.** Every insult needs absurd specificity. "You're annoying" is nothing. "You have the energy of a wasp trapped in a car that everyone's pretending isn't there" is copy.
20. **No buttons after the punchline.** No "Act accordingly." No "You're welcome." No "Ask Kevin." The joke ends ON the implication, not commenting on it.
21. **No stated violence.** "Use on Kevin" and "deploy against coworkers" are saying the violent thing. Violence lives in double meanings and implication — the reader infers it.
22. **No leading with violence.** Lead with product, let violence show up uninvited. The copy describes product performance; the reader hears something else.
23. **No leaving the office/prison world.** Geneva Convention, federal watchlist, CDC, military references — too far outside the universe.

## The Voice

### What the voice IS
A warm, cheerful, slightly conspiratorial friend who is genuinely excited to sell you something ridiculous. The brand KNOWS what it's selling and is DELIGHTED about it.

**Tone markers:** "Look," "Seriously," "Trust us," "We're not going to ask," "We believe in you," "Which is to say."

### What the voice is NOT
- NOT dry, clinical, or neutral
- NOT professional or corporate-sounding
- NOT a one-liner comedian rattling off disconnected jokes
- NOT confused about its own product
- NOT instructing the reader to be threatening

### The Peppiness Requirement
We have repeatedly fallen into writing copy that is too professional and too serious. The copy needs enthusiasm, warmth, personality, levity, and a "we're going to make your life so great" vibe.

**TOO NEUTRAL:** "Won't bleed through paper. Can't speak for anything else."
**CORRECT:** "Other pens leak in your pocket. This one doesn't leak anywhere. Not in your pocket. Not in your hand. Not in Kevin. Completely leak-proof design."

The correct version is chatty, building, listing things off like someone excitedly showing you features.

## The 7 Mechanical Rules

1. **Joke lands on the LAST word or LAST phrase.** Nothing after the punchline. If the line was funnier three words earlier, cut those three words.
2. **Setup is a REAL product copy format.** Feature bullet, spec, disclaimer, care instruction, FAQ, contents list. The reader's brain recognizes the format BEFORE the joke hits.
3. **Punchline word must be CONCRETE and UNCOMFORTABLE.** Not "concerns" — instead: specific nouns, specific actions, specific situations.
4. **Talk TO the buyer.** "Your ___." "You'll ___." Make the reader complicit.
5. **Be CHEERFUL about something you shouldn't be cheerful about.** The brand is your fun, slightly irresponsible friend.
6. **Never instruct the reader to be threatening.** Describe the product in a way that makes the threat OBVIOUS without directing it.
7. **Rule of three when using lists.** Two normal items, third breaks the pattern. The third must be a REAL THING, not a concept. Three of the same category isn't funny. Two normal followed by one crazy IS funny.

**Additional:**
- Strongest lines are 5-13 words
- Ratio: ~60-70% normal product copy, 30-40% absurd
- The second sentence makes the first worse

## Craft Standards

### Sentences
- Simple. Like a witty email to a friend.
- Prune words that don't serve the humor.
- Short beats long.

### Imagery
- Write visually. The reader should see a scene.
- Only include details that make the picture funnier.
- Leave room for the reader's imagination.

### Word Choice
- Use funny words: familiar but rarely used, often with hard consonants.
- Use pop culture references the target audience (25-45 office workers) recognizes.
- Use animal analogies when stuck.

### Exaggeration
- Find the worst case, multiply by ten.
- The exaggeration must have near-logic underneath it.
- If the reader can't imagine it almost working, the exaggeration is random, not funny.

### Density
- Every surface should reward re-reading.
- Surface scan = one laugh. Close read = escalating laughs.
- Hidden details, Easter eggs, and callbacks are features.

### Callbacks
- End pieces by referencing something that already got a laugh.
- Callbacks create a sense of craftsmanship and closure.

## Lessons Learned (Hard-Earned — Do Not Repeat These Mistakes)

### What doesn't work (rejected consistently)
1. **Stated violence** — "Use on Kevin," "stabbing," "deploy against coworkers." Saying the violent thing instead of letting the reader infer it.
2. **Winking/nudging** — "You know why," "if you know what we mean," "technically," "we mean writing clarity." Acknowledging its own joke.
3. **Buttons after the joke** — "Act accordingly," "you're welcome," "ask Kevin," "the autopsy confirmed it." Adding a reaction AFTER the violent implication weakens it.
4. **Office observational humor** — "Meetings suck," "survived another Monday." Knowing nod, not a laugh.
5. **Euphemism** — "Left a permanent mark," "got the point," "signed someone." Too clever, requires decoding.
6. **Non-sequitur absurdity** — Crow gifts, British accents, bizarre life improvements. Random is not funny.
7. **Lines where violence is the SUBJECT** — "Blood? Sure. Screaming? Occasionally." Leading with violence and pivoting to product. Approved lines do the OPPOSITE.
8. **Repeating the same structural trick** — "X-resistant. Y-resistant. VIOLENT-WORD-resistant." The violent third item needs to be a real thing with a double meaning, not a violent word jammed onto the pattern.

### Lessons from iterative development
9. **Observations are not jokes.** "Kids are more competent than adults" is an observation everyone's already had. It's not a reversal, not a surprise, not funny. A reversal would be: the age restriction exists because of child labor laws, not safety.
10. **Extensions are not escalation.** Adding more people who saw the video (HR, Legal, corporate) is extension — it makes it longer. Adding what happened BECAUSE of the video ("So did the jury") is escalation — it makes it worse. One extends, the other escalates. Only escalation is funny.
11. **Ellipsis can replace a punchline.** "Through a light jacket..." is funnier than "We stopped testing after the jacket." The reader's imagination fills in something worse than any written ending.
12. **Start from authentic packaging language.** QTY lines should look like QTY lines. Warning labels should look like warning labels. The format must be recognizable BEFORE the joke hits. Cross-category weapon lists might be funny concepts but don't read like real packaging.
13. **Avoid overusing qualifiers.** "Actually" works once. At three uses across a section, it's a crutch. Watch for repeated dry qualifier patterns.
14. **Pharma ad language is universally recognizable.** "Talk to your doctor" beats "Ask your surgeon" — recognizability > precision. Use formats everyone has seen 1000 times.
15. **Chain puns benefit from story order.** Pen → con → shank → stab follows narrative causality. Random order loses the momentum.
16. **Badges need visual specificity.** "Janitor" isn't just a word — it's a retro-illustrated character with a mop and a thumbs up. Illustrations are part of the comedy design.
17. **Second lines can diminish the first.** Sometimes the best revision is deleting the follow-up.
18. **Stop at the punchline.** If the line was funnier three words earlier, cut those three words.
19. **Don't repeat the same joke structure across iterations.** When every variant is the same idea in 10 costumes, move to a different angle entirely.
20. **Don't push rejected directions.** When a line or direction is explicitly rejected, stop generating variants of it.
21. **"Clever" is not "funny."** Descriptive statements and observations without setup/reversal/subversion are clever but don't land. Every line needs a comedy mechanic — reversal, escalation, misdirect, or pattern-break.

## Design Principles (Established Across Sessions)

1. **Content-first, dimensions later.** Write the best material for each surface's intent. The box adapts to the content, not the other way around.
2. **Every piece of text earns a laugh.** If a word isn't funny or setting up something funny, it's wasted real estate.
3. **Reversals.** Lines should set up one expectation and deliver something else.
4. **Short beats long.** Especially in chart cells and small copy blocks. 5 words max per chart cell, ideally 3 or fewer.
5. **Specificity over concepts.** Concrete nouns, specific details, visual images.
6. **The professional packaging design IS the comedy technique.** The box looks real. The copy is where the humor lives.
7. **Stop at the punchline.** If the line was funnier three words earlier, cut those three words.
8. **Physical product facts are non-negotiable.** No cap = no cap jokes. Blister card = no side panels.
9. **Badges need layered comedy.** Single-mechanic labels don't clear the bar. The badge, its format, and its sub-text should create multiple simultaneous comedy layers.
10. **Second lines can diminish the first.** Sometimes the best revision is deleting the follow-up.

## Review Checklist (Run Before Marking Copy Complete)

### Subtext
- [ ] Can you name the specific office truth this piece comments on?
- [ ] Is the subtext felt but never stated?
- [ ] Does the joke target the system/institution, not the reader?
- [ ] Strip away the mechanics — does a meaningful observation remain?

### Mechanics & Voice
- [ ] At least 2 humor elements present
- [ ] "Recognizable" is one of them (or strong rationale for omitting)
- [ ] Correct Brad mode (Product Copy vs. Personal Brand)
- [ ] Escalation pattern followed (plausible > edge > joke > absurd)
- [ ] Format stays institutional while content goes off the rails
- [ ] Prison/office vocabulary used naturally
- [ ] No metaphor acknowledgment
- [ ] No puns, exclamation points, or winking
- [ ] Sentences are pruned and simple
- [ ] Visually written — reader can see a scene
- [ ] Exaggeration has near-logic
- [ ] Sardonic and warm, not mean or cynical
- [ ] Would an office worker show this to a coworker?

### The 7 Mechanical Rules
- [ ] Joke lands on the LAST word or phrase — nothing after the punchline
- [ ] Setup uses a REAL product copy format the reader recognizes
- [ ] Punchline word is concrete and uncomfortable, not abstract
- [ ] Copy talks TO the buyer — "your," "you'll"
- [ ] Cheerful about something alarming
- [ ] Never instructs the reader to threaten — product describes itself
- [ ] Rule of three: two normal, third breaks the pattern with a REAL THING

### Kill-On-Sight Check
- [ ] No stated violence (violence lives in double meaning or implication)
- [ ] No buttons or reactions after the punchline
- [ ] No winking, nudging, or "get it?" energy
- [ ] No leading with violence and pivoting to product
- [ ] No office observational humor ("meetings suck")
- [ ] No leaving the office/prison world
- [ ] Copy never knows it's funny
