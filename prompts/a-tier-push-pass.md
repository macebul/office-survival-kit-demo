## What Makes an A-Tier Card

From analyzing the strongest cards across all three decks, A-tier cards share these traits:

### Cell Talk A-Tier Pattern
- **Front:** A sincere, recognizable affirmation (the kind you see on Instagram, therapy worksheets, motivational posters)
- **Back:** A twisted reframe that the READER almost agrees with before catching the absurdity. Brad's voice is the delivery vehicle, not the subject. The reader is the main character. Brad is the devil on their shoulder.
- **The best backs are universal rationalizations anyone has quietly made to themselves** — delivered in Brad's voice but landing in the reader's lap
- Brad thinks he's being inspirational the entire time. Every sentence could be on a motivational poster if you weren't paying attention
- The comedy is the gap between what Brad thinks he's saying and what he's actually confessing

**A-tier examples:**
- "How far I've come is measured by how quiet it's gotten behind me. That's not loneliness. That's a lead." — Reader has felt alone and wanted to call it winning
- "Effort is a myth invented by people who didn't think to put their name on something first. Vision is effortless. Execution is just paperwork." — Reader has dismissed hard work this way in their head
- "You don't wait. You take. Then you deserve it. That's how deserving works. I didn't make the rules. I made the packaging." — Addressed to the reader, sounds like a pep talk, Brad slips in at the end
- "Comparison is the thief of joy. Unless you come out ahead. Then it's called benchmarking and it feels incredible." — Universal self-deception everyone has felt

**What A-tier is NOT:**
- Brad talking exclusively about Brad (his keynotes, his trademarking, his credentials)
- Brad monologuing about his personal brand without connecting to the affirmation
- A standalone Brad character sketch that doesn't pull the reader in

### Rap Sheets A-Tier Pattern
- **Front:** A universal office misdemeanor everyone has witnessed or committed
- **Back:** Brad escalates, prosecutes, or (in some cases) endorses the offense — but always with a specific image or metaphor that elevates beyond simple commentary
- The charge should be something EVERYONE has seen — the more universal, the stronger
- Brad's response should have misdirection, subversion, or an unexpected leap

**A-tier examples:**
- "Nobody asked. The playlist was fine. You carried a whole instrument into a building with Wi-Fi and decided what we needed was you. Live. Acoustic. In sandals." — Specific, visual, escalating
- "A 'friendly reminder' is a threat in a sundress. Nothing that starts with 'friendly' has ever ended well. 'Friendly fire' didn't." — Sharp metaphor + unexpected callback
- "'Brief' was a lie and we both knew it the moment you opened a second tab on the shared screen. Eleven minutes. People have been paroled faster." — Specific detail + world-rules vocabulary
- "Three seconds. You couldn't commit to three more seconds. You walked away like a man who has never finished anything. I see you." — Escalation from trivial to existential

### Sweet Nothings A-Tier Pattern
- **Front:** A genuinely nice office compliment (3-7 words)
- **Back:** A hyper-specific, absurd insult built around an everyday image so bizarre nobody can take it personally
- The CRAFT of the insult is funnier than the insult itself
- NOT in Brad's voice — anonymous ambient cruelty
- The best ones use images everyone has experienced (shopping cart wheels, Legos, IKEA furniture, Roombas)
- Four cards now use non-simile formats (escalating list, subverted math, escalating qualification, shrinking escalation) — these break the pattern and re-engage the reader

**A-tier examples:**
- "Working with you is like assembling IKEA furniture with no instructions and extra parts. I don't know what you're for, but throwing you away feels risky." — Specific, absurd, the closer is devastating
- "Talking to you is like stepping on a Lego in the dark — I wasn't prepared, it's never pleasant, and somehow it's always worse than I remember." — Universal experience, perfectly structured
- "Getting feedback from you is like getting directions from a dog. You seem very sure. You are pointing at a squirrel." — Simple, visual, stupid, perfect
- "Things that are appreciated: your honesty. Things that are not: the timing, the volume, the eye contact, or the pointing." — List format, escalates into absurd specificity

## Cards to Evaluate

Read every card in `docs/editorial-pass-results.md`. For each card, assess:
1. Does the back reward flipping the card over with a genuine comedic payoff?
2. Would someone read this to a coworker? Would the coworker want to see the card?
3. Is this funnier the second time, or is it spent after one read?
4. Would someone photograph this card and send it to a group chat?
5. Does the card leave space for the reader to complete the thought?

Tier every card: **A** (photograph-worthy), **B** (solid laugh), or **C** (functional but forgettable).

Then, for every B-tier and C-tier card:
- Diagnose WHY it's weaker (flat closer? generic image? no subversion? too Brad-focused? reader can't see themselves?)
- Propose 2-3 replacement options
- Each option must be under 30 words on the back

## Critical Rules for Writing

1. **Cell Talk backs must be reader-facing.** The reader should almost agree with the twisted reframe. Brad's voice is tone, not subject. The reader is the main character.
2. **Every card needs misdirection, subversion, or an unexpected leap.** Observations aren't jokes. The reader should think they know where it's going, then get caught off guard.
3. **Specificity creates absurdity.** "You're annoying" isn't funny. "You have the energy of a leaf blower at 7 AM, full throttle, no leaves, just standing in the driveway blowing nothing at everybody" is funny. The specific image does the work.
4. **The craft of the joke must be visible.** The reader should appreciate HOW the joke is constructed, not just WHAT it says. This is what makes cards re-readable and photo-worthy.
5. **30-word maximum on all card backs.** Non-negotiable production constraint.
6. **Trust the reader.** Don't narrate punchlines. Don't explain jokes. Leave space for the reader to complete the thought.
7. **No borrowed jokes.** Every comparison, metaphor, and image must feel original. If you've seen it on Twitter, it doesn't belong here.

## Quality Bar

The benchmark is the OG Office Survival Manual. Key samples:
- "A cubicle is 6x8. Death row is 6x9. Enjoy the upgrade."
- "Flush before you cry. It helps cover the sound."
- "A mug has lived in the sink since Q3. No one claims it. No one moves it. At this point, it's load bearing."
- "Kim quit in The Lot. Didn't go inside. Sent the email from her car. We watched from the window. Some say she's still out there. Living."

The manual's signature move: **leaving space for the reader to complete the thought.** Every A-tier card should do this.
