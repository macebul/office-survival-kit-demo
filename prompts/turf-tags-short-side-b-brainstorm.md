# Short Side B — Brainstorm

## Comedy Mechanisms from Approved Copy (Cubicle Shank)

1. **Double meaning / play on words:** "The pen that ends sentences. May also extend yours." — "sentences" means both grammar and prison time. "Extend" means lengthen AND prison sentence extension. Every word carries two meanings.

2. **Format that says one thing, content that says another:** "GOOD SHANKING SEAL OF APPROVAL" — Good Housekeeping format, but for stabbing. The institutional format validates something insane.

3. **Reversal / subverted expectation:** "Does it come in red?" "Eventually." — You expect a color option. You get a body count. One word does all the work.

4. **Embedded words / structural wordplay:** "We put the pen in penitentiary. The con in conference room. The shank in Shawshank. And the stab in stability." — Hidden words inside bigger words, revealed through emphasis.

5. **Escalation that breaks the frame:** PEEL → STICK → HOLD COURT → ACCEPT PARADE → EXIT — Starts real, ends absurd, format never cracks.

6. **Casual understatement of something dark:** "PREVIOUS OWNER." next to a skull with a pen in it. "RESTRUCTURING." next to a cat destroying everything.

## What's on Short Side A for Comparison

"SHARING IS ~~CARING~~ OVER."

Mechanism: Takes a known cultural phrase and defaces it. Visual edit (strikethrough) IS the joke. 4 visible words. Maximum economy.

## Short Side B Requirements
- 3.5" x 0.5" — tiny surface, very few words
- Must be a standalone joke, independent from other surfaces
- Can NOT repeat: legal/inspection callbacks, phrase defacement (used on Side A)
- SHOULD use: play on words, reversals, multiple layers, or subverted expectations
- Should feel like a discovery when you rotate the box

## Brainstorm — 15 Options

### Play on Words / Double Meanings

**1. "APPLIED SCIENCE."**
- First read: a field of study (applied science vs. theoretical science)
- Second read: the science of applying stickers — literally applied, to a surface
- Third read: Brad thinks sticker deployment IS a science. He's not joking.
- Short (2 words). Institutional. Multiple layers.

**2. "STICK WITH IT."**
- Wait — this is a pun. Kill it.

**3. "ADHESIVE REASONING."**
- First read: sounds like "adhesive" is just describing the product (sticky reasoning?)
- Second read: "adhesive reasoning" = reasoning that sticks, logic you can't argue with
- Third read: Brad's entire territorial philosophy — his logic is adhesive. It sticks to things. Like his stickers.
- Institutional. Sounds like a department name or a legal term.

**4. "PERMANENT POSITION."**
- First read: a permanent position = a full-time job (office)
- Second read: a permanent position = these stickers don't come off (product)
- Third read: a permanent position = a body that isn't moving (dark/prison)
- Two words. Three readings. Clean.

**5. "OCCUPANCY: 1."**
- First read: fire code / building code sign format — "maximum occupancy"
- Second read: this space is occupied by one person. You.
- The number "1" is the territorial declaration. Nobody else fits.
- Format parody (building code) with territorial content.

### Reversals / Subverted Expectations

**6. "REMOVAL IS VOLUNTARY. THE STICKER'S, NOT YOURS."**
- Setup: "removal is voluntary" sounds like a standard adhesive disclaimer
- Turn: you realize the sticker is voluntary. Your removal from the area is NOT.
- Might be too long for the surface.

**7. "UNSTICK AT YOUR OWN RISK."**
- Sounds like a standard product warning about removing stickers
- Second read: "unstick" = remove the sticker, but "at your own risk" implies physical danger from the person who placed it
- Short. Warning format.

**8. "CONTENTS HAVE SHIFTED."**
- First read: airline luggage warning — "contents may have shifted during transit"
- Second read: the contents (your stuff) have shifted because someone moved them and now there will be consequences
- Third read: the "contents" are the person reading this. They've shifted into someone else's territory. They should shift back.
- Institutional format (airline safety language).

**9. "DO NOT REMOVE UNDER PENALTY."**
- First read: standard product tag warning (like mattress tags)
- Second read: "under penalty" — penalty of what? Left open. The reader fills it in.
- Third read: mattress tag law = the most absurd law everyone knows. Applying that legal weight to a sticker is the format/content gap.

### From the Sticker Designs (building on locked material)

**10. "ALL PETS ARE FRIENDLY UNTIL PROVOKED."**
- Callbacks "PET FRIENDLY." from Sticker 2 (bear claws)
- Reframes the sticker's cheerful claim into a conditional threat
- Might be too long.

**11. "RESTRUCTURING IN PROGRESS."**
- Callbacks "RESTRUCTURING." from Sticker 5 (cat pushing things off desk)
- Adds "in progress" — it's happening right now. This is active. Not a warning, a status update.
- Still might be too close to the sticker content.

### Institutional Format Parodies

**12. "PROPERTY OF: SEE STICKER."**
- Format: "Property of:" label with a blank
- Content: the answer to "whose is this?" is "read the sticker I already put on it"
- Circular logic — the proof of ownership is the sticker, and the sticker's authority comes from... having been stuck there.
- Short. Institutional.

**13. "VOID IF REMOVED."**
- First read: standard product warranty language — the warranty is void if the sticker/seal is removed
- Second read: what's void? The warranty? Or something worse? Your lease on this cubicle? Your safety?
- Two words. Threatening through ambiguity.
- Sounds like a legal statement. Feels like a threat.

### Wild Cards

**14. "NOT A SUGGESTION."**
- Three words. Reframes every sticker on the sheet as a directive, not a decoration.
- Works as a thesis statement for the product — these aren't cute. They're policy.
- Simple but might be too thin.

**15. "SURFACE TENSION."**
- First read: a physics term (the property that allows water to resist external force)
- Second read: surface = the surfaces these stickers go on; tension = the office tension they create
- Third read: the tension is ON the surface — the sticker is the source of conflict
- Two words. Scientific term repurposed. Multiple layers.
- This one might be too cerebral.
