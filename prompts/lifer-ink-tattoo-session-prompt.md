## The Product

**Lifer Ink** = temporary tattoos marketed as earned commendations for surviving office life.

- **Real brand:** Witty Yeti (consumer-facing)
- **In-world brand:** Elevate Co. (on all packaging)
- **Founder character:** Brad Sterling — genuinely believes temporary face tattoos are professional commendations. He is NOT in on the joke.
- **Core concept:** Prison tattoo culture translated to the office. Each tattoo is EARNED for surviving specific office scenarios. The comedy lives in the gap between Brad's institutional seriousness and the absurdity of what he's presenting.
- **Physical format:** 6 individual temporary tattoos + an Achievement Card ("Commendation Requirements") inside a tuck-end box

**The Big Idea:** Lifer Ink isn't temporary tattoos. It's a commendation system for office survival. The achievement card turns "should I apply this?" into "which one did I earn?"

## Comedy Principles — THE MOST IMPORTANT PART

**Every line of copy must use real comedy mechanics.** This is the single biggest failure mode in previous sessions — writing lines that describe things or make observations without any actual joke technique. Here's what MUST be present:

### What Works (from locked/approved lines across the project)

1. **Reversals / Subversion** — Reader expects one thing, gets another. Their brain does a flip.
   - "IF SWALLOWED, CONTACT YOUR SUPERVISOR. NOT BECAUSE IT'S DANGEROUS. BECAUSE WE HAVE QUESTIONS." — You expect "because it's dangerous." You get "because we have questions." Surprise.
   - "AGES 16+. Child labor laws. You can't shank someone if you can't legally be in the building." — The age limit isn't about SAFETY, it's about workplace ACCESS.

2. **Puns / Wordplay / Multiple Simultaneous Meanings** — Words that work two or more ways at once. The reader discovers layers on re-reading.
   - "The pen that ends sentences. May also extend yours." — "Ends sentences" = grammar / prison / killing. "Extend yours" = prison sentence / lifespan.
   - "Bureau of Permanent Records" — government records / school threat / irony (tattoos are temporary)

3. **Escalation** — Each beat makes it worse, but the FORMAT stays steady.
   - "Through the sentence. Through the paragraph. Through a light jacket..." — material escalation, ellipsis lets imagination do the work
   - "Patent pending. Trial also pending. Jury still out. Literally." — each sentence recontextualizes the previous one

4. **Specificity** — Concrete nouns, specific details, visual images. Not abstract concepts.
   - "Kevin's says LIFE instead of LIFER" — you SEE the misspelled tattoo
   - "Not while it's still in Kevin" — you SEE the pen embedded in Kevin
   - "She's been there 11 years" — specific number, specific person

5. **Flow / Rhythm** — Lines should sound good read aloud. Short beats. Parallel structure.
   - "Fear, tears, then beers" — four words, rhyming, three-stage narrative
   - "Smooth ink. Smooth barrel. Smooth criminal." — parallel structure, pattern break on third

6. **Playful / Warm / Conspiratorial Tone** — A friend showing you something ridiculous, not a cynical observer. Excited, not dark.
   - The darkness is in the PRODUCT/SITUATION, not the TONE
   - "We're genuinely flattered" energy — cheerful about absurd things

### What Does NOT Work (failure modes from previous sessions)

- **Observations** — describing a situation without a joke. "Karen has been here three years" is not a joke.
- **Institutional straight-man copy** — playing it so straight there's no comedy layer at all.
- **Dark consequence humor** — threatening, intimidating, cynical. The tone should be warm even when the content is dark.
- **Walls of text** — keep it SHORT. The Cubicle Shank uses charts, bullets, discrete blocks. Not paragraphs.
- **Explaining the joke** — if you have to explain why something is funny, it isn't.
- **Generic insults** — every insult needs absurd specificity.
- **Describing what the product does** — "these are temporary tattoos that commemorate office achievements" is a product description, not comedy.

### The Kill List (never do these)

- No puns in general copy (puns ARE allowed in taglines and wordplay-specific slots)
- No exclamation points in product copy
- No winking at the reader ("get it?")
- No actual workplace advice
- No making Brad self-aware
- No acknowledging the office/prison comparison as a metaphor — the office IS a prison

### Humor Elements Checklist

Every piece of copy needs at least 2 of these 6 elements, and "Recognizable" should almost always be one:

1. **Clever** — wordplay, structure, hidden layers
2. **Bizarre** — unexpected, surreal, "wait what?"
3. **Cruel** — pointed, specific, earned meanness (not generic)
4. **Naughty** — edgy, pushing boundaries, "should they be saying this?"
5. **Recognizable** — "oh my god that's MY office" — the audience sees themselves
6. **Cute** — endearing, warm, makes you like the brand

## Voice Standard

**The Witty Yeti (WY) voice** is a warm, cheerful, conspiratorial friend who is excited to show you something ridiculous. Think of someone who found the most absurd product and can't wait to tell you about it.

The best locked example of this voice on Lifer Ink is the application instructions (APP-3v2):

> 1. CUT around design. *(Custom modification? We support it.)*
> 2. REMOVE clear film. *(Two layers. You'll remove the wrong one first.)*
> 3. PLACE face-down on skin. *(Counterintuitive. Like most good decisions.)*
> 4. APPLY wet cloth, 30 seconds. *(The longest 30 seconds that doesn't involve a microwave.)*
> 5. PEEL backing slowly. *(Panic is normal. Peeling too fast is why Kevin's says "LIFE" instead of "LIFER.")*
> 6. ALLOW to dry, 60 seconds. *(You will touch it. We've never seen anyone not touch it.)*
> 7. DISPLAY. *(Optional. But you didn't go through all that for nothing.)*

Notice: talks TO the buyer, cheerful about mishaps, specific concrete images, warm and conspiratorial, jokes land LAST in each line.

## Quality Bar

Before presenting any copy, ask yourself:

1. Is there an actual comedy MECHANIC here (reversal, pun, escalation, misdirect, pattern-break)? Or am I just describing something?
2. Would this make an office worker laugh and show it to a coworker?
3. Does this have layers — does a second reading reveal something the first reading missed?
4. Is the tone warm and playful, or cold and institutional?
5. Does it flow well read aloud?
6. Is it SHORT enough? Could it be shorter and still land?

If you can't identify the specific comedy mechanic in a line, the line doesn't have one. Rewrite it.
