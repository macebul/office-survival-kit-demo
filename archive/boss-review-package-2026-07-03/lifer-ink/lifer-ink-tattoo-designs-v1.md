# Lifer Ink — Tattoo Designs v1

**Last Updated:** July 3, 2026
**Status:** Locked — Ready for visual design development
**Purpose:** Final tattoo concepts for the Lifer Ink temporary tattoo set. Each tattoo includes image description, achievement copy, visual direction, comedy layers, and application notes.
**Tattoo Count:** 6 individual temporary tattoos. Each tattoo is printed on a rectangular backing sheet, but only the design itself transfers to skin (die-cut to the image shape — no blank rectangle of ink).
**Also Includes:** 1 Achievement Card ("Commendation Requirements") inside the tuck-end box
**Sizes:** 3-6cm range. Simple images that read at conversational distance on skin.

---

## Design Principles (from sessions)

1. **"Laugh, not nod" is the filter.** A concept that gets recognition ("oh that's clever") fails. It must trigger an actual laugh at conversational distance on someone's skin. Subversion and absurdity are the primary humor engines.
2. **Image-first comedy.** The visual must be funny before any copy is read. If the concept needs explanation to land, it doesn't work as a tattoo. Concepts that describe a mood ("DAYS WITHOUT INCIDENT: 0") get a nod, not a laugh.
3. **Physical constraints are absolute.** At 3-6cm, anything requiring legible text + multiple visual elements + recognition of a specific reference has too many failure points. The strongest tattoos are simple images that read at a glance.
4. **Prison/office dual-language is the achievement copy engine.** Every achievement line uses words that simultaneously mean something in prison AND something in the office. The reader's brain does the flip without being told.
5. **One surface, one job.** Each tattoo gets one line on the Achievement Card. No follow-up copy. No taglines. No captions. The format is: image + one-line commendation. Period.
6. **Ship strong, not full.** 6 strong designs > 12 with filler. Every concept earned its slot.

---

## Comedy Rules (from .claude/rules/comedy-writing.md)

- No puns. Brad is dry.
- No exclamation points in product copy.
- No winking at the reader.
- At least 2 of 6 humor elements per piece: Clever, Bizarre, Cruel, Naughty, Recognizable, Cute.
- Specificity over generality.
- Sardonic and warm, not mean or cynical.
- The office IS a prison. Never acknowledge the comparison as a metaphor.

### TATTOO 1 — TEARDROP

**Image:** A single classic teardrop tattoo. Solid black. The kind you'd see in a mugshot or a prison movie — positioned to sit just below the outer corner of the eye. Clean, simple, unmistakable. Zero ambiguity about what this references.

**Achievement Line:** One for every name they — or you — crossed off the list.

**Format:** Small (~3cm). Solid black fill. This is the smallest tattoo in the set — it should feel like the real thing at a quick glance.

**Comedy Layers:**
- **Prison behavior:** The teardrop is the most iconic prison tattoo in popular culture. It means you killed someone. Everyone knows this. That's the entire setup — the symbol carries generations of cinematic and cultural weight before the wearer says a word.
- **Office behavior:** Applying a teardrop tattoo in an office context implies you've been TAKING PEOPLE OUT. Not layoffs, not reorganization — the hitman reading. "Busy quarter" = corporate productivity language carrying a body count.
- **The voluntary application:** In prison, you earn a teardrop. In the office, you're putting one on yourself. With a wet paper towel. At your desk. The gap between the gravity of the symbol and the silliness of the act is where the laugh lives.
- **Achievement line engine:** "Crossed off the list" = layoff list / hit list. "They — or you" implicates the wearer. The em dash creates a pause that lets the second reading land.
- **Conversation starter:** Someone sees a teardrop under a coworker's eye at 2pm on a Tuesday. They already know what it means. The question becomes "which one did you earn it for?" — which is the entire product concept working as designed.

**Humor Elements:** Recognizable, Naughty, Cruel, Bizarre (4/6)

**Application:** Below the outer corner of either eye. This is the only tattoo in the set with a specific, iconic placement. Applying it anywhere else would miss the reference.

**Visual Direction:** Should look as authentic as possible. No cartoonish outline, no decorative fills. A real teardrop tattoo is a simple solid shape — the comedy is in a temporary version appearing on an office worker's face, not in the design itself being funny.

**Backup Achievement Line:** "One for every name they, or you, crossed off the list." (if em dashes are rejected at print)

**Status:** Locked.

### TATTOO 2 — LIFER

**Image:** The word "LIFER" in bold block capital letters. Prison-style tattoo typography — heavy, blocky, slightly rough. The letterforms should reference the hand-done, imperfect look of actual prison tattoos rather than clean digital type. Think of the word scratched or inked onto skin with whatever was available — not typeset by a graphic designer.

**Achievement Line:** For employees whose sentence has no end date.

**Format:** Small (3-4cm wide). Black block letters. Horizontal strip — the word reads left to right in a single line.

**Comedy Layers:**
- **Prison behavior:** Block-letter word tattoos are one of the most recognizable prison tattoo tropes. Single words inked across the body (LOVE/HATE, THUG LIFE) are visual shorthand for "this person has done time." The format alone does 80% of the comedy work.
- **Office behavior:** "Lifer" in corporate slang means someone who's been at the company forever and will never leave. It's said with a mix of pity, admiration, and dread — "she's a lifer." Inking it on your body turns a passive observation into a declaration of identity.
- **The dual meaning of "sentence":** The achievement line's "sentence" means prison sentence AND employment tenure simultaneously. "No end date" is how both prison lifers and career employees describe their situation. The word does double duty without either reading canceling the other.
- **Product name crossover:** The tattoo literally IS the product name. Wearing "LIFER" is wearing the brand. It's the most direct bridge between the product concept and the physical item.
- **The casual reveal:** The word appears when a sleeve rides up, a collar shifts, or someone rolls up their sleeves at their desk. Every mundane moment becomes a potential reveal of your status. The comedy is ambient — it just keeps happening throughout the day.

**Humor Elements:** Recognizable, Clever, Naughty, Bizarre (4/6)

**Application:** Inner forearm, upper arm, or across the chest. Anywhere the word reads horizontally and can be casually revealed or deliberately shown.

**Visual Direction:** Imperfect, hand-done-looking letterforms. Not clean typography — these should look like someone did them with a needle and ink in a cell. The roughness IS the authenticity. A polished font would kill the reference.

**Status:** Locked.

### TATTOO 3 — PAPER AIRPLANE

**Image:** A paper airplane in flight with the words "FLIGHT RISK" stamped across or just below it in small, institutional block type — like a classification stamp on a personnel file. Simple, clean, immediately recognizable folded-paper shape with a slight motion trail or dotted flight path behind it. The airplane should look like it's going UP and AWAY — the trajectory matters. It's escaping, not landing.

**Achievement Line:** For anyone who's updated their credentials while still on the inside.

**Format:** Small-medium (4-5cm). Black line art. The flight path/motion trail can be lighter or dotted to create depth.

**Comedy Layers:**
- **Prison behavior:** "Flight risk" is official booking language — inmates classified as flight risks get higher security, ankle monitors, restricted yard time. It's stamped on files and flagged in systems. The paper airplane is the escape vehicle. Together they read as: classified, tagged, and already in the air.
- **Office behavior:** Managers literally use "flight risk" about employees they suspect are job-hunting. It's said in hushed tones during talent reviews. The paper airplane is the universal gesture of boredom, disengagement, and "I have mentally checked out of this meeting." Combining the two says: "I am actively planning my escape and I'm advertising it on my skin, in your building."
- **Resume/credentials layer:** "Updated their credentials while still on the inside" — in prison, this means building your reputation while incarcerated. In the office, it means updating your LinkedIn/resume while still employed. The act of preparing to leave while pretending to stay. Everyone who's job-searched at work feels this one.
- **The confession:** Without the text, the paper airplane is cute. With "FLIGHT RISK," the wearer is volunteering a classification that's supposed to be kept quiet. It's a confession disguised as a warning label. The transgression is the joke — you're not supposed to TELL people you're leaving.
- **The trajectory:** The airplane going UP is important. It's aspirational. It's not crashing, it's launching. The comedy is warm — this person hasn't given up, they're preparing. The tattoo is a badge of hope, not defeat.
- **Visual simplicity:** A paper airplane is 6-8 lines plus two small words. It reads instantly at any size. The stamp-style text adds institutional weight without cluttering the image.

**Humor Elements:** Recognizable, Clever, Cute, Naughty, Cruel (5/6)

**Application:** Forearm, wrist, or shoulder — anywhere it looks like it's taking off from the body. The placement should suggest launch, not landing.

**Visual Direction:** Clean line art. The paper airplane should look folded from actual paper — visible fold lines, slightly asymmetric (real paper airplanes are never perfect). The flight trail (dotted line or light motion marks) should suggest trajectory without cluttering the image. "FLIGHT RISK" should appear in small, condensed, uppercase institutional type — stamped below or across the airplane at a slight angle, like a classification stamp on a manila folder. Not hand-drawn, not decorative. It should look like someone in processing put it there.

**Status:** Locked.

### TATTOO 4 — PEN THROUGH TALLIES

**Image:** A ballpoint pen stabbed through a set of tally marks (five lines, the fifth crossing through the previous four in the classic "counting days" pattern). The pen is embedded at an angle, piercing the tallies like it was driven through them. This is the Cubicle Shank crossover — the pen should be recognizable as a standard office ballpoint, not a fancy pen, not a pencil.

**Achievement Line:** For keeping score with something sharper than necessary.

**Format:** Medium (4-5cm). Black line art with potential red accent for pen barrel detail. The pen and tallies should read as a single composed image, not two separate elements overlapping.

**Comedy Layers:**
- **Prison behavior:** Tally marks scratched into a wall = counting days served. The most iconic visual of incarceration after bars on a window. Every prison movie has a shot of tally marks. The pen through them adds violence — someone didn't just count the days, they attacked the record.
- **Office behavior:** Tallies in the office = counting meetings survived, deadlines endured, Mondays processed. The pen through them says "I kept track AND I had a weapon the whole time." The mundane office pen becomes simultaneously a writing instrument, a counting tool, and a threat.
- **Cubicle Shank crossover:** The pen IS the Cubicle Shank — Item #1 in the Office Survival Kit. Someone who owns the kit recognizes this pen. It's an Easter egg that rewards ownership of the full product line without requiring it. If you don't own the Shank, it's a pen through tallies. If you do own it, it's THE pen through tallies.
- **Achievement line engine:** "Keeping score" = tracking grievances / prison grudge culture / office politics. "Sharper than necessary" = the pen's point / the person's attitude / the Cubicle Shank's hidden purpose. Three readings from two words.
- **The angle:** The pen is stabbed through at an angle, not laid across. It was driven in with force. This wasn't counting — it was punctuation.

**Humor Elements:** Recognizable, Clever, Cruel, Naughty (4/6)

**Application:** Forearm or upper arm. The tallies should read horizontally (like they would on a wall), not vertically.

**Visual Direction:** The tallies should look hand-scratched, not typeset — uneven spacing, slightly different heights, the way someone actually scratches them into a surface. The pen should look like a standard Bic-style ballpoint — cheap, ubiquitous, the kind that lives in every desk drawer. The stabbing angle (approximately 30-45 degrees) should make it look embedded, not resting.

**Status:** Locked.

### TATTOO 5 — CHALK OUTLINE (SANDWICH)

**Image:** A crime-scene chalk outline of a sandwich. The classic police body-outline treatment — white dashed or solid outline on what would be skin, suggesting the object is GONE and only its trace remains. The sandwich shape should be specific enough to be instantly recognizable: two triangular halves (the universal "cut diagonally" sandwich shape), with enough detail to suggest fillings (a lettuce leaf edge, a tomato slice curve) without being cluttered.

**Achievement Line:** Issued upon the confirmed loss of a labeled food item.

**Format:** Medium (5-6cm). White or light gray outline (to read as "chalk" against skin). This is the only tattoo in the set that uses white/light ink instead of black — the chalk outline reference demands it.

**Comedy Layers:**
- **Crime scene behavior:** The chalk outline means someone died here. It's the universal visual shorthand for "a body was found." The format is instantly recognized from every detective show ever made. The comedy is in applying that gravity to a sandwich.
- **Office behavior:** Fridge theft is the single most universal office grievance. Everyone has had food stolen. Everyone has opinions about it. This is arguably the most "recognizable" tattoo in the set — the audience identification is immediate and visceral. The sandwich didn't just disappear. It was murdered. There's an outline. There's an investigation.
- **The specificity of the sandwich:** It's not a generic "food item." It's a sandwich, cut diagonally, with visible fillings. Someone made this sandwich. They cut it the way they like it. They labeled it. And now it's a crime scene. The specificity of the sandwich shape is what makes the chalk outline absurd instead of abstract.
- **Achievement line engine:** "Confirmed loss" = police report language / HR incident report language. "Labeled food item" = everyone who has ever written their name on a container in the office fridge. The label didn't save it. The label just made the loss more personal.
- **The evidence reading:** Chalk outlines are drawn AROUND the body, then the body is removed. The sandwich is gone. The outline is what's left. The tattoo wearer is walking around with the evidence of a crime that was never solved. It's a cold case they carry on their skin.

**Humor Elements:** Recognizable, Bizarre, Clever, Cute (4/6)

**Application:** Forearm, shoulder, or anywhere with enough flat space for the sandwich shape to read clearly. The outline should sit flat against the skin like it was drawn on a surface (because chalk outlines ARE drawn on surfaces).

**Visual Direction:** The outline should look like actual chalk — slightly uneven, not perfectly smooth. White or very light gray ink. The sandwich shape should be the classic diagonal-cut triangle pair with just enough interior detail (a wavy lettuce edge, a round tomato slice) to confirm it's a sandwich and not an abstract shape. Consider a small "EVIDENCE" or case number tag nearby for additional forensic flavor, but only if it doesn't clutter the image at 5-6cm.

**Status:** Locked.

### TATTOO 6 — CELL BLOCK (SPREADSHEET)

**Image:** A stick figure trapped inside a spreadsheet cell. The figure is gripping the cell borders (the lines between columns/rows) like prison bars, pressing against them, clearly wanting out. Column headers across the top — "A | B | C | D" in the standard spreadsheet letter format — immediately identify the grid as a spreadsheet rather than a generic table or cage. Landscape orientation — wider than it is tall, because spreadsheets are landscape documents.

**Achievement Line:** For doing hard time in someone else's cell.

**Format:** Medium-large (landscape orientation, wider than tall). Black line art. Column headers (A, B, C, D) across the top row. Alternating row shading optional — the column headers are the primary "this is a spreadsheet" signal. This is the largest tattoo in the set and the only one in landscape orientation — it needs horizontal space to sell the spreadsheet grid.

**Comedy Layers:**
- **Prison behavior:** A figure gripping bars is THE image of incarceration. Hands wrapped around vertical bars, face pressed between them, desperate to get out. It's the most universally understood visual of imprisonment.
- **Office behavior:** The "bars" are spreadsheet cell borders. The "cell" is a spreadsheet cell. The figure isn't in prison — they're in Excel. But the posture, the desperation, the grip on the bars — it's identical. The office worker trapped in a spreadsheet they didn't create, maintaining formulas they don't understand, is doing time in someone else's cell.
- **The dual "cell" meaning:** This is the tattoo that makes the entire product concept click. A prison cell and a spreadsheet cell are the same word because, for the person trapped in either one, they're the same experience. The tattoo doesn't explain this — it just IS both things at once. The viewer's brain does the translation.
- **Column headers:** The "A | B | C | D" across the top is the instant spreadsheet identifier. Every office worker has stared at those letters. The column headers do the same job as "FLIGHT RISK" on the paper airplane — they stamp an institutional classification onto the image that tells the viewer exactly what system trapped this person. Without them, the grid could be anything. With them, it can only be a spreadsheet.
- **Achievement line engine:** "Hard time" = prison sentence / difficult work. "Someone else's cell" = inherited a cell in prison / inherited someone else's spreadsheet (and now you're responsible for formulas you didn't write and can't break). Both readings are simultaneously active.
- **The stick figure's posture:** The figure should look the way everyone feels at 3pm on a Wednesday when they're four hours into a spreadsheet that was supposed to take twenty minutes. Not angry — trapped. There's a difference. Trapped is funnier because it's more accurate.

**Humor Elements:** Recognizable, Clever, Bizarre, Cute (4/6)

**Application:** Forearm (landscape orientation — the wider surface accommodates the horizontal format), or upper arm/shoulder. The spreadsheet grid should be oriented so the rows run horizontally when the arm hangs naturally.

**Visual Direction:** The grid lines should be clean and straight — this is a digital document, not a hand-drawn table. Column headers (A, B, C, D) across the top in small, clean type — the same letters every office worker sees when they open a spreadsheet. The stick figure, by contrast, should be slightly rough and human — the organic trapped inside the geometric. Alternating row shading is a nice-to-have if the designer can make it transfer cleanly, but the column headers carry the "this is a spreadsheet" identification — don't rely on subtle gray fills for legibility. The figure's hands gripping the cell borders should be the focal point — that's where the prison/spreadsheet overlap is most visually obvious.

**Note on sticker vs. tattoo:** This concept was considered for the Turf Tags sticker sheet but belongs as a tattoo. The tattoo format works because: (1) tattoos in this product are "commendations earned for surviving office life" — being trapped in a spreadsheet is exactly the kind of ordeal that earns a commendation, (2) the achievement card line ("For doing hard time in someone else's cell") only works in the prison/office dual-language system that the tattoos use, and (3) a sticker version would need to function as a territory marker when deployed on a surface, but a trapped stick figure doesn't claim territory — it documents suffering. That's what the tattoos do.

**Status:** Locked.

## Achievement Card Format

Each tattoo gets one entry on the Achievement Card:
- Small image of the tattoo design (left)
- One-line commendation (right)
- No follow-up copy, no taglines, no captions

The card header references the commendation system framing. The lines are NOT explanations of the tattoos — they're the "what you earn it for" criteria. The reader looks at their tattoo options, reads the achievement requirements, and decides which one they've qualified for.

### What Makes the Achievement Lines Work

Every line uses the same engine: **prison language that simultaneously reads as office language.** Neither reading cancels the other. Both are active at once.

| Word/Phrase | Prison Reading | Office Reading |
|---|---|---|
| "Crossed off the list" | Hit list | Layoff list |
| "Sentence" | Prison sentence | Employment tenure |
| "Credentials" | Prison reputation | Resume / LinkedIn |
| "Keeping score" | Tracking grudges | Performance metrics |
| "Sharper than necessary" | The shank | The attitude |
| "Hard time" | Time served | Difficult work |
| "Cell" | Prison cell | Spreadsheet cell |
| "Flight risk" | Escape-prone inmate classification | Employee likely to quit |
| "Labeled" | Marked evidence | Named food container |
| "Confirmed loss" | Body confirmed | Food confirmed stolen |

## Decisions Log

| Decision | Resolution | Rationale |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| Tattoo count | 6 (not 8-12) | 6 strong designs > 12 with filler. 10+ batches of replacement concepts generated and rejected. Shipping strong beats shipping full. |
| Copy format | Image + one achievement line | Follow-up lines and short taglines both attempted and rejected. Every addition diluted the achievement lines. |
| Visual style | Simple, reads at a glance | Physical constraints at 3-6cm kill complex concepts. Simple images that read at conversational distance on skin. |
| Humor filter | Laugh, not nod | "Clever" concepts get recognition but not laughs. Subversion and absurdity are the humor engines that work on skin. |
| TATT-6 sticker vs. tattoo | Tattoo | Documents suffering (tattoo function), doesn't claim territory (sticker function). Achievement line only works in the prison/office dual-language system. |
| White ink for TATT-5 | Yes | Chalk outline reference demands light-on-dark. Only tattoo in the set that breaks the black ink convention — the format reference justifies it. |
| TATT-2 placement | Forearm/chest (not knuckles) | 3-4cm is too small for knuckle spanning. Block letters work anywhere — the prison tattoo typography carries the reference without requiring knuckle placement. |
| TATT-3 "FLIGHT RISK" text | Added | Paper airplane alone scored 3/6 humor elements (Recognizable, Clever, Cute) — warm but not funny enough. "FLIGHT RISK" adds Naughty and Cruel (5/6). Institutional stamp text turns a cute image into a confession. Both prison (booking classification) and office (talent review language) readings are active. |
| TATT-6 column headers | Added (A, B, C, D) | Alternating row shading may not transfer cleanly on temp tattoos — subtle gray fills are the hardest detail to reproduce. Column headers are a higher-contrast, more reliable "this is a spreadsheet" identifier. Row shading now optional nice-to-have. |
| Achievement line em dashes | Keep (with backup) | "They — or you —" reads better with em dashes. Comma version available if print production rejects them. |
