# Office Survival Kit — Copy Voice Guide (For All Sessions)

## THE WORLD

**The office is a prison. This is not a metaphor.**

Nobody in this world thinks it's clever to compare the office to prison. There's nothing to compare — the office just IS this way. You serve your sentence. You do your time. The language of incarceration IS the language of corporate life. It's the water everyone swims in.

The moment anyone acknowledges the comparison AS a comparison, the world breaks. It's only funny because everyone treats it as completely normal.

### Shared Vocabulary (use naturally, never explain)

| Term | Meaning | Tone |
|------|---------|------|
| Doing time / serving your sentence | Working at a company | Matter-of-fact |
| Your cell | Your cubicle or desk | Normal |
| The yard | Break room, common areas | Normal |
| Mess hall | Break room / kitchen | Normal |
| Solitary | Noise-canceling headphones, bathroom hiding | Normal |
| Contraband | Anything that makes the day bearable | Normal |
| Lifers | Long-tenured employees | Respectful |
| Release day | Last day, retirement, quitting | Aspirational |
| The warden | Upper management, HR, the boss | Normal |
| Intake / processing | Onboarding | Normal |
| Commissary | Vending machines, supply closet | Normal |
| Shank / shiv | Your pen, your one reliable tool | Affectionate |

| Rap sheet | Performance review history | Dreaded |

This vocabulary isn't reserved for "funny parts" — it's the DEFAULT language for everything. Stepping outside it should feel like stepping out of character.

## THE TWO BRADS

Brad Sterling operates in two modes. The comedy comes from the fact that both exist on the same product, written by the same person, with zero self-awareness about the gap.

### Mode 1: Product Copy Brad
**When Brad writes about the office, the product, the jokes.**

This Brad is competent. He knows his audience. He can write a sardonic Nutrition Facts label, a deadpan IT Asset Tag, a perfectly absurd Care & Storage warning. The humor comes from the format (official labels, FDA layouts, QC stickers) played totally straight while the content escalates into absurdity. The format never breaks.

**Voice:** Dry, institutional, straight-faced. Clinical detachment.

**Escalation structure:**
1. First line reads as completely plausible
2. Second line has a slight edge
3. Third line is clearly a joke but format stays serious
4. Final line is full absurd but still formatted perfectly

**Word choice:**
- Institutional: "deployed," "authorized," "issued," "designated"
- Bureaucratic precision: case numbers, dates, serial numbers
- Office pain points named with clinical detachment
- "Kevin" as a recurring proper noun treated as a known hazard

**Examples:**
- "PROPERTY OF: NOBODY ANYMORE"
- "Serving Size: 1 Monday / Servings Per Container: Too Many"
- "DO NOT REMOVE UNDER PENALTY OF MONDAY"
- "Not a significant source of work-life balance, upward mobility, or the feeling that any of this matters."
- "If symptoms persist, update your LinkedIn."

**What to avoid:**
- Puns (too cute — Brad's product copy is dry, not clever)
- Exclamation points (the format is always flat)
- Breaking the format (the form never acknowledges it's a joke)
- Winking at the reader (the straight face IS the technique)

### Mode 2: Personal Brand Brad
**When Brad writes about Brad.**

All self-awareness evaporates. He becomes a hustle-culture LinkedIn post given physical form. Completely, unshakably sincere. He never winks. He thinks this is what leadership looks like.

**Voice:** Aspirational, self-mythologizing, motivational-speaker cadence. Vocabulary borrowed from startup culture, TED Talks, and lifestyle brand copy.

**Sentence structures Brad loves:**
- Origin myth: "When [Brad] discovered [thing], he didn't see [obvious interpretation]. He saw [grandiose reinterpretation]."
- Pivot-as-epiphany: "That's when everything changed."
- False-humble brag: "Brad is an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself."
- Premium stacking: Three adjectives minimum. "Premium workplace tools, premium packaging, and a premium price point."
- Credential drops: Awards that sound almost real. Organizations that might exist. Titles definitely self-given.

**Word choice:**
- "Vision," "disrupt," "elevate," "curate," "experience"
- "Professional Edition" (Brad thinks adding this makes it luxury)
- His own name in third person

**What to avoid:**
- Making Brad aware of the joke (he never is)
- Having Brad be mean or cynical (he's earnest — his product copy is sarcastic, but HIS BRAND is dead serious)
- Going too far into parody (he should feel like a real person you've met)

## THE GAP IS THE JOKE

The funniest thing about the product is that both Brads are on the same packaging. Competent product humor AND unhinged self-promotion, same person, zero contradiction in his mind.

**Don't sabotage the product copy to make Brad look dumber.** The BETTER the product copy is, the MORE absurd his founder bio becomes by contrast. If everything were bad, Brad would just be incompetent. Good at the job + terrible at self-awareness = funnier character.

---

## THE COMEDY RULE (LOCKED)

**"Format stays buttoned-up. Content gets unleashed."**

Take a real format (nutrition label, asset tag, QC sticker, care instructions, evidence tag, prison form, official notice) and fill it with escalating office absurdity. The format never breaks. The content gets progressively more unhinged.

## DOC SET 1 / BOOKLET VOICE

The booklet ("From the Desk of Brad Sterling") is Brad narrating the reader's office life.

**Key principles:**
- Each page is about the READER'S reality. Brad's character gets established through how he sees their world.
- 10-25 words of copy per page, woven between images. Images carry half the comedy.
- Three-act structure: Assessment (your world) -> Diagnosis (it gets worse) -> Intervention (Brad has the answer)

**Sample copy from the booklet:**
- "Day one. They photographed you. Catalogued you. Assigned you a number and a seat."
- "Then they showed you your cell. A cubicle is 6x8. Death row is 6x9. Enjoy the upgrade."
- "I left a pen in Conference Room B. By Thursday it had a name, a reputation, and three people afraid of it."
- "'Sounds good' means no. 'Let's circle back' means never. 'Per my last email' means I've already told someone who can end you."

## OVERALL TONE

**Sardonic, not cynical.** The people in this world haven't given up — they've adapted. Nobody is nihilistic. Nobody thinks it's hopeless. The attitude is: "this is absurd, we all know it's absurd, and here's how we get through it together."

That warmth underneath the dark humor is what makes it a gift someone gives to a coworker they actually like. It's not mean. It's validating.

## RECURRING CHARACTERS

| Character | Role | Density Target |
|-----------|------|----------------|
| **Kevin** | The coworker. Everyone has one. Office villain. | 4-6 across full kit |
| **Gary** | The quality inspector. Dutiful, slightly haunted. | 2-3 across full kit |
| **Diane** | HR. Her presence is the warning. | 1-2 across full kit |

Don't force character appearances. Let them show up where the joke earns it.

---

## WORDS/PHRASES TO AVOID

- Anything that sounds like actual workplace advice
- Puns (too cute)
- Exclamation points (keep it dry)
- Winking at the reader
- Breaking the world rules — nobody acknowledges the office/prison comparison as a comparison
- "Like a prison" or "it's basically prison" — NO. It IS prison. No similes.

---

## DENSITY IS A FEATURE

The reader who scans should laugh. The reader who reads closely should laugh harder. Every line earns its space. If a line doesn't add a joke, a character beat, or a world detail, cut it.

---

## THE COPY/IMAGE RELATIONSHIP

Copy and images are not separate — they're one integrated scene. The copy should NOT repeat what the image shows. The image provides the visual punchline or the setup; the copy provides the other half. They complement, not duplicate.
