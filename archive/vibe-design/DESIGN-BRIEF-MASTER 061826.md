## GOAL

*What needs to be different in the world because of this design.*

**The Task vs. The Goal:**
- **Task (what's being made):** Print-ready packaging design for a briefcase box, 7 blister-packed items (6 blister packs + 1 windowed box), 3 card deck boxes, and the briefcase exterior/interior
- **Goal (what changes in reality):** A person picks up any single piece of this kit and -- before reading a word -- believes it is a real, high-quality product. Then they start reading and realize the copy is hilarious. The packaging IS the straight man. The format never cracks.

**Before State > After State:**
- **Before:** The recipient receives a briefcase-shaped box. They don't know what's inside. They see professional-looking packaging and assume it's a real product.
- **After:** They've opened every item, read every surface, laughed at escalating layers of comedy, and want to show individual pieces to coworkers. Items end up displayed on desks, passed around offices, and photographed for social media.

**Downstream Goal:**
- The packaging must sell the illusion of authenticity on first glance, then reward closer reading with escalating humor. Every surface is a comedy delivery mechanism disguised as legitimate retail packaging.

**Upstream Goal Chain:**
- Packaging looks authentic > recipient picks it up and reads it > comedy lands > they show it to coworkers > word of mouth + social sharing > more sales

**What Success Looks Like:**
- Someone sees a blister pack and genuinely mistakes it for a real product for 2-3 seconds
- Every surface rewards re-reading -- first scan gets one laugh, close reading gets escalating laughs
- The kit feels premium and intentional, not cheap or thrown together
- A recipient pulls out individual items to show coworkers throughout the week
- The packaging is photographed and shared on social media because it looks real enough to confuse people

**What Failure Looks Like:**
- Packaging looks like a novelty/gag gift at first glance (cheap, loud, trying too hard)
- Comedy is visible before authenticity is established (the joke is on the surface instead of in the details)
- Items look like they belong to different products (no visual cohesion across the system)
- Copy is hard to read because design competes with it instead of serving it
- The designer treats this as a joke product and designs accordingly -- this must be designed as if it were a REAL product line

## AUDIENCE

*The specific humans whose state this design is trying to change.*

**Buyer vs. User:**
- **Are they the same person?** Usually no
- **The Buyer:** Office worker (25-45) buying a gift for a coworker, friend, or white elephant exchange. Encounters the product on Amazon or social media. Needs to believe this is worth $40-60 before purchasing. Makes the decision based on listing images and reviews.
- **The User/Recipient:** The coworker or friend who receives the kit. Experiences the unboxing and every packaging surface. This is who the packaging design is primarily for.
- **Optimizing for:** The recipient's experience. The buyer is sold through listing photography and reviews. The packaging design serves the person holding it in their hands.

**Primary Audience: "Office Lifer Lisa"**
- **Who they are:** 28-40, works in a corporate or semi-corporate office environment, has strong opinions about coworkers, finds dark humor cathartic, appreciates craft and attention to detail
- **Before State:**
  - *What they know:* They received a gift. The briefcase looks premium. They're expecting something funny based on the giver's personality.
  - *What they believe/assume:* Most "funny office gifts" are one-note mugs or cheap novelties that get thrown in a drawer
  - *What they're trying to do:* Open a gift, probably in front of coworkers or at a party
- **The Gap:** Small nudge on "this is funny" -- they're expecting humor. Big lift on "this is QUALITY" -- they're expecting cheap novelty. The packaging must immediately signal that this is not a $15 gag gift.
- **What would make them say "this is for me":** Recognizable office frustrations described with absurd specificity. Packaging that feels like someone actually cared. Details that reward attention.
- **What would make them say "this isn't for me":** Cheap-looking packaging. Obvious jokes. Bathroom humor. Mean-spirited tone.
- **Hidden Agenda:** They want to display this at their desk because it signals they have good taste in humor AND that someone thought enough of them to buy a quality gift
- **Skepticism / Resistance:** "Is this actually going to be funny or is it going to be one of those things that's mildly amusing for 30 seconds?"

**Secondary Audience: Bystander Coworkers**
- The people who see the items on the recipient's desk or get handed an item to read. They encounter individual pieces out of context. Each item must be independently funny without requiring knowledge of the full kit.

**Key Psychological Insight:**
- The recipient's delight scales with the gap between expectation and reality. They expect cheap novelty. When the packaging signals premium/authentic, that gap creates the setup. The comedy in the copy delivers the payoff. The wider that gap, the funnier it is. **The designer's job is to maximize that gap by making the packaging as authentically premium as possible.**

## CONTEXT

*The specific circumstances under which the audience will encounter this design.*

**Physical / Digital Environment:**
- **Where:** Primarily at a desk, in a break room, or at an office party. Sometimes at home.
- **Medium/Device:** Physical product held in hands, at arm's length reading distance (12-18 inches)
- **Environmental conditions:** Office lighting (fluorescent or LED), moderate background noise, possibly an audience of curious coworkers watching

**Sought vs. Served:**
- **Sought** -- the recipient is actively opening a gift and examining each item. They WANT to engage.
- **Implication:** Attention is given, not earned. The design does not need to shout for attention. It needs to reward the attention it already has. This means: prioritize readability, information density, and comedic pacing over eye-catching graphics or bold visual hooks.

**Attention Available:**
- **Time window:** Extended. Gift recipients examine items thoroughly. Each blister pack will get 30-60 seconds of front examination, then a flip to read the back. High-engagement items (cards, booklet) get minutes.
- **Competing stimuli:** Other items in the kit. The recipient will examine one item, then move to the next. Each item must stand on its own AND build on the cumulative experience.
- **Decision mode:** Exploratory / entertainment. They are not deciding whether to buy. They are discovering and being entertained.

**Emotional / Mental State:**
- Excited, curious, socially engaged (others may be watching). Primed to laugh. Willing to read closely if the first scan rewards them.

**Social Setting:**
- Often opened in front of others. This means the packaging needs to be visually impressive from a distance (premium feel, cohesive system) AND textually rewarding up close (comedy in the details). The recipient will read things aloud to the group.

**Technical / Physical Constraints:**
- All dimensions are defined in the **Interior Layout Spec** (`product/interior-layout-spec.md`). Key numbers:
  - Briefcase: 14.0" × 11.5" × 5.0" exterior (13.5" × 11.0" × 4.5" interior)
  - Blister pack cards: 13.25" × 10.75" L-shaped (with 4.75" × 3.75" notch), 18pt paperboard, same die cut for all 7 cards
  - Card deck boxes: 3.7" × 2.7" × 0.7" exterior (all 3 identical dimensions)
  - Mug box: 4.0" × 5.0" × 4.0" with die-cut window
- Both sides of every blister card are full comedy surfaces (~249 sq in per card, ~1,743 sq in total across 7 cards)
- All print files need CMYK, 300 DPI minimum, proper bleed (typically 0.125" / 3mm)
- Body copy must be legible at the actual print size -- no smaller than 7pt for fine print, 9pt for body copy

**Context Shifts:**
- Items will be removed from the briefcase and live independently on desks, shelves, and in drawers. Each blister pack and card box must work as a standalone object, not just as part of the kit.

## PROBLEM IDENTIFICATION & PRIORITIZATION

**All problems this design must solve:**
- Establish visual authenticity -- packaging must look like real retail products
- Create a unified system across 7 diverse items that still feels cohesive
- Serve the comedy by making copy maximally readable and well-paced
- Signal premium quality to justify the price point
- Give each item its own identity/color system while maintaining brand cohesion
- Support the "layer cake" comedy structure (first glance > closer look > deep read)
- Work as standalone items when removed from the briefcase
- Be producible as print-ready files with correct specs

**Causal Chain (Attention > Message > Action):**

1. **AUTHENTICITY: "This looks like a real product"** *(If we fail here, nothing else matters)*
   - The comedy depends entirely on the packaging looking legitimate. If it looks like a gag gift, the joke is dead on arrival. There is no gap between expectation and reality, and therefore no surprise.
   - This means: real typography, real layout conventions, real color usage, real print quality. Study actual retail packaging in each item's category.

2. **MESSAGE: "Wait... what does this actually say?"**
   - Once the recipient picks up an item and starts reading, the copy needs to land. The design must make copy easy to find, easy to read, and paced correctly. Headlines pull them in. Body copy escalates. Fine print delivers the deepest jokes.
   - This means: clear typographic hierarchy, generous reading space, no design elements that compete with or obscure the copy.

3. **MESSAGE: "This whole thing is a system"**
   - As the recipient moves through multiple items, they should feel the cohesion -- same universe, same brand voice, same quality level. Recurring design elements, consistent typography, and the fictional division system create this.
   - This means: shared type system, shared layout principles, shared finishing details (logo placement, barcode treatment, fine print style).

4. **ACTION: "I need to show this to someone"**
   - The recipient should want to hand individual items to coworkers, display them, photograph them. Each item must be visually interesting enough to photograph and textually dense enough to reward sharing.
   - This means: items must look good in photos (clean design, strong branding), and the comedy must be discoverable in layers so there's always something new to find.

## DESIGN DIRECTION

### The Core Principle

**The packaging is the straight man. The copy is the comedian.**

Design every surface as if this were a legitimate product. Use real retail packaging conventions. Study how actual products in each category are packaged and follow those conventions faithfully. The comedy comes exclusively from the copy content -- never from the visual design "being funny."

### What This Means in Practice
- No wacky fonts, no comic sans, no "funny" typography
- No cartoon illustrations or exaggerated graphics
- No bright/loud color schemes that signal "novelty gift"
- No visual winking at the reader
- YES to clean, professional typography
- YES to authentic-feeling product photography and icons
- YES to institutional/corporate visual language
- YES to details that real products have (barcodes, regulatory marks, weight/count info, lot numbers)

### Color System

**Designer proposes the color palette.** The product owner will review and approve.

**Direction / Feel:**
- The overall feel should be premium, institutional, and high-quality
- Think: something that evokes both corporate authority and a slight prison/institutional edge
- The designer has full creative freedom on the specific colors -- propose what looks best

**Brand Level (Briefcase + Unifying Elements):**
- The briefcase and any shared branding elements need a cohesive palette
- Dark tones (black, charcoal, dark gray) are likely the foundation, but that's a starting point, not a constraint
- Designer should present 2-3 accent color options on the briefcase exterior for product owner review before proceeding to items

**Item Level (Each Blister Pack / Box):**
- Each item should have its own color palette that makes sense for what that item is
- The item palettes should feel like they belong to the same product family when seen side by side
- Some direction on per-item feel (may evolve with copy):
  - Sweet Nothings (passive-aggressive valentines): Romantic palette -- pinks, reds, whites
  - Cell Talk (affirmation cards): Calming/zen palette -- greens, earth tones
  - Rap Sheets (complaint cards): Institutional/bureaucratic feel
  - Cubicle Shank (tactical pen): Tactical/hardware feel
  - Lifer Ink (temporary tattoos): Tattoo parlor aesthetic
  - Turf Tags (vinyl stickers): TBD based on copy direction
  - Mug Shot (enamel mug): TBD based on copy direction

### Typography System

**Recommendation: Shared type system, flexible application.**

Use 2-3 fonts across the entire product line:

1. **Primary heading font:** Something with institutional/corporate authority. Used for item names, division names, and key headlines across all items. This is the typographic thread that ties everything together.
   - Direction: Could be a clean geometric sans-serif (like Gotham, Proxima Nova) or something with more institutional weight (like a condensed grotesque). Should feel like it belongs on government forms or corporate signage.

2. **Body copy font:** A highly legible workhorse for all body text, descriptions, and the comedy copy. This font carries the bulk of the humor -- it must be invisible as a typeface and perfect as a reading experience.
   - Direction: Clean, neutral, excellent at small sizes. Think packaging body copy.

3. **Optional accent font (use sparingly):** For specific elements like "fine print," regulatory-style text, barcode labels, or institutional markings. Monospaced or condensed.

**How this stays cohesive but varied:** The same fonts appear on every item. What changes per item is the color palette, the proportions (headline size vs. body size), and the imagery. The Valentine cards use the same heading font as the tactical pen -- but in pink on white instead of gold on black. This creates a family feeling without making everything look identical.

**Key typography rules:**
- Body copy minimum 9pt at print size (7pt for intentional "fine print" comedy)
- Headlines should have strong scale contrast with body (not 18pt vs 16pt -- more like 24pt vs 10pt)
- Left-aligned body copy (do not center paragraphs)
- Generous line spacing (1.4-1.5x) for body text -- the copy is dense and needs to breathe

### Imagery Direction

A combination of:
- **AI-generated product photography:** For hero shots, product-in-use imagery, or lifestyle context on blister cards and boxes. Will be provided or generated to spec.
- **Icons and illustrations:** For logos (division logos, Elevate Co. marks), regulatory-style marks, and functional elements. Should feel like real corporate iconography -- clean, professional, not cartoony.
- **Tables, charts, comparison grids:** Some items may include parody comparison tables, spec sheets, or feature grids. These should look like real product specification layouts.

### Institutional Details (Easter Eggs and Authenticity Markers)

Real products have these. So should ours:
- Barcodes (can be fictional but should look real)
- Lot numbers / batch codes
- Weight or count information
- "Made in [location]" text
- Regulatory-style marks and symbols
- Fictional patent numbers, trademark symbols
- QC inspection marks (Gary appears here -- see recurring characters below)
- Fine print disclaimers (these are comedy gold -- design should make them findable but not prominent)

## THE "LAYER CAKE" STRUCTURE

The comedy is designed to work in layers. The designer must support this with typographic hierarchy:

**Layer 1 -- First Glance (2-3 seconds):**
- Item name, hero image, division name
- Impression: "This looks like a real product"
- Design job: Establish authenticity and premium feel

**Layer 2 -- Casual Read (10-20 seconds):**
- Tagline, primary description, key features
- Impression: "Wait, this is actually funny"
- Design job: Make these elements easy to find and read. Clear hierarchy pulls the eye from Layer 1 to Layer 2.

**Layer 3 -- Close Read (30-60 seconds):**
- Body copy, feature lists, comparison tables, secondary text
- Impression: "This is really well written, there's so much here"
- Design job: Dense but readable. Reward the reader who commits to reading everything.

**Layer 4 -- Deep Dive (fine print, margins, hidden elements):**
- Disclaimers, fine print, lot numbers, regulatory text, Easter eggs
- Impression: "Oh my god, even the fine print is funny"
- Design job: Present as real fine print / regulatory text. Don't call attention to it. Let the reader discover it.

**In scope:**

- Dieline creation (dielines/templates are provided)

The designer receives finished dimensions, finished copy, and finished dielines. They make it look good.
