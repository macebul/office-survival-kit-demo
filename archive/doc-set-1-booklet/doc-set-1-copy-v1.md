# Doc Set 1 — "From the Desk of Brad Sterling"

**Last Updated:** April 6, 2026
**Status:** Draft v5 — Narrative arc structure. Reader's world through Brad's lens.
**Voice:** 100% Brad Sterling / Elevate Co.
**Format:** 12-page saddle-stitched booklet, ~5.5" x 7" portrait, full color, glossy
**Visual style:** Same generated photorealistic/styled imagery as the outer box packaging. Brad appears on every page in his full visual DNA (purple satin, gold chain, aviators, smile). Each page is a different Brad scenario.

> **Design philosophy:** Each page is 2-3 image vignettes with 10-25 words of copy woven between them. The images carry half the comedy. The copy and visuals cannot be separated — they're one integrated scene. Reference the OG Manual's first 30 pages for how text and illustration flow together on a page.

> **Narrative arc:** The booklet walks the reader through their office life — from arrival to breaking point — narrated by Brad, a man who has never worked in an office but has studied them from a co-working space and has opinions about all of it. The reader keeps flipping because each page is about THEIR reality. Brad's outsider perspective is the comedy. His character gets established through how he sees your world, not through pages about himself.

> **Three-act structure with integrated phase labels:**
> - **PHASE 1: ASSESSMENT** (Pages 2-4) — Brad observing your world. "Your life, as Brad sees it."
> - **PHASE 2: DIAGNOSIS** (Pages 5-7) — The daily grind. Brad identifies the problem. (It's you.)
> - **PHASE 3: INTERVENTION** (Pages 8-11) — Brad arrives with the solution. The profiteer reveal.
>
> Phase labels appear as small integrated design elements on the first page of each act — not standalone dividers. Brad calls watching your life "assessment," your daily suffering "diagnosis," and his product "intervention." The clinical corporate framing of your misery into Brad's sales funnel is itself a joke.

> **Key reference:** See `outer-box-image-prompts-v1.md` for Brad's visual DNA spec and the established image generation approach. See `goals-metrics v01.md` for product goals — every page serves at least one goal.

```
ACT 1: ASSESSMENT         ACT 2: DIAGNOSIS         ACT 3: INTERVENTION
(Your world, as Brad      (It gets worse.           (Brad has the answer.
 sees it)                  Brad identifies            The profiteer reveal.)

Page 3: Your Desk          Page 6: Your Boss         Page 9: The Solution
Page 4: The Break Room     Page 7: The Grind         Page 10: The Portrait
                                                     Page 11: The Mission
```

**The reader's emotional journey:**
1. "Ha, that's my life" → recognition, connection (Act 1)
2. "This is getting worse and this guy has no idea" → escalation, entertainment (Act 2)
3. "And THIS guy thinks he can help me?" → comedic tension, the turn (Act 3)
4. Puts booklet down → "What did he actually make?" → opens the kit

**Goal alignment:**
- Goal 2 (Delight): Every page is a recognized office moment made funny by Brad's lens
- Goal 4 (Shareability): Individual pages work as standalone images; the narrative makes people describe the whole booklet
- Goal 5 (Engagement): Story structure keeps you flipping; details reward re-reading
- Unboxing completion (90%+): The narrative builds to "I have the solution" — priming the reader to explore the kit

## PAGE 2 — "Arrival" | PHASE 1: ASSESSMENT

**The hook. First content page. Must land a laugh in 2-3 seconds.**

---

**VIGNETTE 1 (top half):**
The reader's Day 1 — a new employee being processed. Badge photo with no smile allowed. Lanyard being placed around neck like a collar. Handbook being handed over (thick, chained, or absurdly heavy). HR person with a clipboard, clinical efficiency. The employee looks shellshocked. The scene reads as both office onboarding AND prison intake simultaneously — without either being labeled as such.

**Copy (over or near vignette 1):**

> Day one.
>
> They photographed you.
>
> Catalogued you.
>
> Assigned you a number and a seat.

---

**VIGNETTE 2 (bottom half):**
Brad in his co-working space or observing from outside — clipboard in hand, binoculars around his neck, studying the intake process with the same clinical detachment as the HR person above. The visual PARALLEL is the joke: Brad and HR are doing the same thing (studying/processing people), just in different settings. Brad's expression isn't concern — it's professional interest. He's collecting data.

**Copy (over or near vignette 2):**

> I do the same thing.
>
> I just call it market research.

---

**Full copy read-through:** "Day one. They photographed you. Catalogued you. Assigned you a number and a seat. I do the same thing. I just call it market research."

**Comedy engine:** The first half uses world-rules vocabulary that works on two levels — it describes office onboarding AND prison intake. "Photographed. Catalogued. Assigned a number." The reader recognizes their Day 1 without the page having to say "remember your first day?"

Then "I do the same thing" is the surprise. You expect Brad to be DIFFERENT from the corporate machine. He's not. He does exactly what HR does — studies, categorizes, processes people. "I just call it market research" is Brad's entire business model in one sentence: he doesn't create anything new, he renames things.

The image sells it before the punchline lands: Vignette 1 shows corporate processing. Vignette 2 shows Brad doing the same thing from his co-working space. The visual parallel means the reader SEES "I do the same thing" before they READ it.

**Deepest layer:** Brad is accidentally revealing that he's not an alternative to the corporate system. He IS the corporate system with better packaging. Which is literally what he did to the product — same kit, new branding, higher price.

**Goal alignment:** Goal 2 (Delight) — immediate recognition + surprise punchline. Goal 5 (Engagement) — the parallel rewards a second look. Establishes the office-as-prison vocabulary on the first content page, priming the reader for the world-rules throughout the kit.

## PAGE 3 — "Your Desk" | PHASE 1: ASSESSMENT

---

**VIGNETTE 1 (top half or two-thirds):**
A cubicle. The universal workspace. Shot from above or at eye level — the walls, the monitor, the chair, the personal items that accumulate over years. It should feel immediately recognizable to anyone who's worked in an office. The space is small. The walls are thin. The lighting is fluorescent. Maybe a tape measure or dimension markers are visible — "6x8" — as if someone (Brad) has measured it.

**Copy (top of page, leading into vignette 1):**

> Then they showed you your cell.

**Copy (over or near vignette 1):**

> A cubicle is 6x8.
>
> Death row is 6x9.

---

**VIGNETTE 2 (bottom portion):**
The "upgrade" — the reader AT their desk, settled in, surrounded by the evidence of time served. Or: Brad presenting the cubicle like a real estate agent showing a property, gesturing at the 6x8 space with open palms as if it's a feature, not a sentence.

**Copy (beneath, landing as the punchline):**

> Enjoy the upgrade.

---

**Full copy read-through:** "Then they showed you your cell. A cubicle is 6x8. Death row is 6x9. Enjoy the upgrade."

**Comedy engine:** Dark math, delivered deadpan. The reader does the calculation: their workspace is SMALLER than death row. "Enjoy the upgrade" reframes this as a promotion — you didn't get downgraded from death row, you got UPGRADED to a cubicle. Three words. The brevity is what makes it land. This is Mode 1 Brad — genuinely good sardonic product copy delivered in a straight-faced institutional voice. It sounds like something printed on a welcome card.

The image sells the scale: the cubicle's dimensions visible, maybe with Brad's measurement annotations or real estate-style callouts ("OPEN CONCEPT" on a 6x8 box, "NATURAL LIGHT ADJACENT" on a cubicle near a window someone else has). The visual details reward a second look.

**Goal alignment:** Goal 2 (Delight) — immediate LOL, universally relatable. Goal 4 (Shareability) — this is a "photograph and text to someone" page. Goal 5 (Engagement) — visual details in the cubicle reward re-reading.

## PAGE 5 — "The Instincts" | PHASE 1: ASSESSMENT

**The reversal on page 4. The reader thought "been here longer than the carpet smell" was about people. It wasn't. It was about the survival behaviors baked into office culture. The instincts are older than the carpet. They were here before the company name on the door. This reframe leads directly into the Experiment spread on pages 6-7 — Brad saw these instincts and decided to test one.**

---

**VIGNETTE (full page):**
A visual showing survival instincts in action — the behaviors Brad observed. A desk with items arranged defensively. A chair angled to watch the door. Someone's lunch labeled aggressively. Headphones on before the person has sat down. A mug positioned like a territorial marker. These are the instincts — ancient, hardwired, unspoken. The image shows what Brad means without the copy having to explain it.

**Copy:**

> Not the people.
>
> The instincts.

---

**Comedy engine:** The reversal recontextualizes page 4. "Been here longer than the carpet smell" → the reader assumed it meant employees → page 5 reveals it meant the survival BEHAVIORS. The instincts to hoard, defend, claim, hide — they predate the carpet, the furniture, the company itself. Two words ("The instincts.") do all the work. The image fills in what Brad observed. This leads directly into the Experiment spread: Brad saw these instincts and thought "what happens if I test one?"

## PAGES 6-7 — "Conference Room B" | PHASE 2: DIAGNOSIS (SPREAD)

**Phase 2 begins. The phase label "DIAGNOSIS" appears as a small integrated design element.**

**Brad runs a field test. He plants a pen in a meeting room. The office turns it into a weapon. Brad discovers his product. Now a full spread — room to breathe across two pages.**

---

**VIGNETTE 1 (top):**
Brad outside a glass conference room, placing a pen on the table through a cracked-open door. He's being stealthy — aviators on, looking both ways, placing it carefully like he's planting evidence. Or: the pen sitting alone on an empty conference room table, spotlit, like bait in a trap. Brad watching from the hallway.

**Copy (top):**

> I left a pen in Conference Room B.

---

**VIGNETTE 2 (middle):**
The aftermath — the pen's journey. An office worker clutching it protectively. Or multiple people eyeing it. Or someone who has clearly NAMED it — maybe a label on it, or it's been modified, or it's being concealed in a desk drawer like contraband. The pen has gone from office supply to survival tool. The transformation happened without Brad's involvement.

**Copy (middle):**

> By Thursday it had a name,
> a reputation,
> and three people afraid of it.

---

**VIGNETTE 3 (bottom):**
Brad observing the result — clipboard in hand, taking notes, looking pleased. Or: the pen, now labeled "CUBICLE SHANK" by its owner, displayed like a trophy or hidden like contraband. Brad's expression: this is the data he was looking for.

**Copy (bottom, the punchline):**

> I didn't leave a pen.
> I left a Cubicle Shank.
>
> I just didn't know it yet.

---

**Full copy read-through:** "I left a pen in Conference Room B. By Thursday it had a name, a reputation, and three people afraid of it. I didn't leave a pen. I left a Cubicle Shank. I just didn't know it yet."

**Comedy engine:** Brad runs a clinical trial on office workers — plants a pen and observes. The office immediately weaponizes it. The reversal: Brad thought he was testing whether people need writing tools. The office taught him they need WEAPONS. He didn't invent the Cubicle Shank — the office did. He just watched.

This page does triple duty:
1. It's funny on its own (a pen developing a reputation and a body count in one week)
2. It connects Brad's observation to the actual kit — he's not just watching, he's TESTING, and the results are building toward the product
3. It primes the reader for the Cubicle Shank they'll find in Tray 1 — when they open that box, this page echoes

**Goal alignment:** Goal 2 (Delight) — escalation joke with a strong punchline. Goal 5 (Engagement) — the Cubicle Shank callback rewards the reader who remembers this page when they open the pen box.

## PAGES 10-11 — "The Ink" | PHASE 2: DIAGNOSIS (SPREAD)

**The identity page. Connects to Lifer Ink (tattoos). The contrast between empty status signals and actual earned marks.**

**VIGNETTE 1 (top):**
Office status signals — someone in a sharp suit, nice watch, fresh haircut. Looking polished. Looking like they have it together. The surface-level armor people wear. Presented cleanly, maybe as a fashion/style image.

**VIGNETTE 2 (bottom):**
The contrast — what those surface signals DON'T show. The things you actually survived. Could show a chalk outline of a sandwich (the lunch that didn't make it) or a paper airplane (flight risk). Or the images could be tattoo-style flash art of these moments — like a tattoo parlor wall but for office survival.

>
> Where's the mark
> for the lunch that didn't make it?
>
> For being a flight risk?
>

---

**Full copy read-through:** "New suit. New haircut. Nice watch. None of it proves anything. Where's the mark for the lunch that didn't make it? For being a flight risk? You earned your ink. You just haven't worn it yet."

**Comedy engine:** The first three beats (suit, haircut, watch) set up a familiar status game. "None of it proves anything" is the dismissal. Then the page names REAL things the reader survived — a stolen lunch described as a crime scene, being a flight risk (dual meaning: paper airplane + HR term for someone about to quit). "You earned your ink" reframes temporary tattoos as EARNED marks, not novelty items. "You just haven't worn it yet" points directly to the Lifer Ink box in Tray 1 without naming the product.

**Goal alignment:** Goal 2 (Delight) — the dismissal of status signals is relatable; the specific examples are funny. Goal 5 (Engagement) — "the lunch that didn't make it" and "flight risk" echo when the reader finds the actual tattoo designs later.

**Comedy engine:** The emotional low point of the narrative. The reader feels seen. Then they notice Brad's reaction — and the gap between their pain and his excitement is the darkest, funniest beat in the booklet. The profiteer seeing your worst moment and thinking "now they're ready."

## PAGES 14-15 — "The Reveal" | FINAL SPREAD

**The closing spread. Brad's face + his closing quote + the kit visible. The tonal flip — from dark comedy to hype. The reader puts this down and opens the briefcase.**

---

**PAGE 10 (left — Brad's Portrait):**
Full Brad Sterling portrait — purple satin shirt, oversized gold chain, aviators, megawatt smile. Game show host energy. He's holding or standing next to the OPEN briefcase, contents visible, presenting it like a prize. This is the first time the reader sees Brad's face after 8 pages of his voice. He looks exactly like they imagined. He looks worse. And he's holding the thing they're about to open.

PHOTO COURTESY OF ELEVATE CO. in tiny text.

The image does the product integration — the kit is VISIBLE. Brad is presenting it. No copy needed on this page to sell the product. The image says "here are the tools."

---

**PAGE 11 (right — The Quote):**
Big typography, lots of white space. The quote lands with weight because the page is mostly empty. This is Brad's thesis — the tonal flip from "work is a prison" to "but you can win."

**Copy:**

> Everybody's trying to survive.
>
> That's the problem.
>
> Nobody's trying to win.

---

**Comedy engine:** The entire booklet has been dark comedy about office survival — the cell, the block, the loadout, arming up, earning ink. The word "survive" has been the throughline. On the last page, Brad flips it. Surviving is thinking small. The kit isn't about surviving. It's about WINNING. "Nobody's trying to win" is peak Brad — hustle-culture, delusional, motivational-speaker energy — AND it's accidentally motivating. The reader laughs because it's so Brad. And then they look at the kit on page 10 and think: "OK, let's see what this guy put together."

The subversion is the TONAL SHIFT itself. After 8 pages of dark humor, the sudden pivot to positive, aspirational Brad is the surprise. The reader didn't expect to feel hyped by the guy in the purple satin shirt. But they do. And that's the comedy AND the product integration in one beat.

**Goal alignment:** Goal 2 (Delight) — the tonal flip is a genuine surprise. Goal 4 (Shareability) — "Nobody's trying to win" is quotable, photographable. Unboxing completion (90%+) — the reader sees the kit in Brad's hands and wants to open theirs.
