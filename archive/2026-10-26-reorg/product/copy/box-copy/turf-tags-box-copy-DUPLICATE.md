# Turf Tags — Complete Box Copy

**Last Updated:** June 5, 2026
**Status:** Draft v1 — All surfaces drafted, pending stakeholder review
**Purpose:** Every text element on the Turf Tags item box, organized by surface.
**Voice:** Brad Sterling / Elevate Co. — Mode 1 (institutional, territorial compliance). Played 100% straight.
**Brand:** Elevate Co. — Bureau of Territorial Affairs
**Product:** Turf Tags™ — Workspace Personalization Labels™
**Division:** Bureau of Territorial Affairs

> **The Rule:** The format is the straight man. The content is the comedian. This box looks like a territorial compliance product issued by a corporate property management division. Vinyl stickers are written about with the gravity of international border law. The more seriously the box takes workspace sovereignty, the funnier it becomes when you realize it's about putting a sticker on your stapler.

> **Naming Hierarchy (DRAFT):** "TURF TAGS™" dominant. "Workspace Personalization Labels™" subordinate. Matches Lifer Ink / Cell Talk pattern.

## SURFACE 1 — FRONT FACE (5.0" x 3.5")

*The face visible when the box sits in Tray 1. First thing the recipient reads. Bold yellow background. This surface establishes the territorial concept in one glance — the dog illustration does half the work before the copy even registers.*

---

### TT-1: Brand Header
**Treatment:** Small, spaced uppercase sans-serif at top left. Institutional. Yellow background with black text throughout.

**Placement:** Top left, above everything

> **ELEVATE CO.**
> Bureau of Territorial Affairs

**Design note:** "Bureau of Territorial Affairs" is Brad's fake compliance division for this product line. It sounds exactly like a government agency — the kind of department that would issue permits for fence placement or adjudicate property line disputes between neighbors. Brad created a bureau for stickers. He filed no paperwork to establish it. He is the sole employee.

**Status:** Draft.

---

### TT-2: Product Name (Hero Copy Element)
**Treatment:** Large, dominant, bold sans-serif. Black on yellow. This is the first text element the recipient actually reads after the dog illustration grabs their attention. It should feel like a product designation stamped on industrial equipment, not a sticker brand.

**Placement:** Upper portion, spanning most of the width. Dominant scale.

> **TURF TAGS™**

**Design note:** "Turf Tags" works on two levels: (1) turf = your claimed territory in the office, tags = the markers you place on it, and (2) turf tags echo graffiti tags — marking territory the way someone would tag a wall, except the wall is your monitor and the spray paint is a vinyl sticker. Brad chose the name because he thinks it sounds like a product a territorial consultant would sell. He's right.

**Status:** Draft.

---

### TT-3: Hero Illustration
**Treatment:** Black silhouette of a dog lifting its leg on an office chair. Clean, bold, reads instantly at any distance. The single strongest visual on the box — it communicates the entire concept without a word of copy. Centered or slightly offset for composition.

**Placement:** Center of the front face, prominent. The visual anchor of the entire box.

**Design note:** The dog-peeing-on-the-chair illustration is doing more work than any line of copy on this surface. Territory marking is primal. Dogs do it. Humans want to but can't. Brad made a product that lets them. The silhouette style keeps it tasteful and graphically bold — it reads as an icon, not a cartoon. At tray-viewing distance (18-24" above), this is the first thing the recipient's eye locks onto. The yellow background makes the black silhouette pop even harder.

**Status:** Draft.

---

### TT-4: Tagline
**Treatment:** Bold uppercase, positioned near the hero illustration. A command, not a suggestion. Reads as a slogan you'd see on a territorial awareness campaign poster.

**Placement:** Adjacent to or above the dog illustration — wherever it reads as the call to action

> **MARK YOUR TERRITORY.**

**Design note:** Three words. A command. Works literally (mark your territory with these stickers) and as the entire thesis of the product. The period makes it a statement, not an invitation. Brad didn't ask if you want to mark your territory. He's telling you to. The fact that this instruction appears next to a dog urinating on furniture is not something Brad considers ironic. He considers it on-brand.

**Status:** Draft.

---

### TT-5: Corporate Rebrand Subtitle
**Treatment:** Smaller, lighter weight. Subordinate to "TURF TAGS™" but clearly present. This is Brad's formal product classification — the sanitized corporate name that would appear in a procurement catalog.

**Placement:** Below "TURF TAGS™", above or below the tagline

> Workspace Personalization Labels™

**Design note:** "Workspace Personalization Labels" is Brad's corporate translation of "territory-marking stickers." It's what he'd put on an invoice. It's what he'd say in a meeting with HR. The trademark symbol means Brad trademarked this phrase. Of course he did. The gap between "TURF TAGS" and "Workspace Personalization Labels" is visible in the hierarchy itself — primal concept on top, corporate sanitization underneath. Same product. Two languages.

**Status:** Draft.

---

### TT-6: Subtitle / Kicker
**Treatment:** Italic or lighter weight, set below the corporate subtitle. Reads as the tagline's punchline — the reason these stickers exist.

**Placement:** Below "Workspace Personalization Labels™"

> *Because Asking Nicely Didn't Work.*

**Design note:** This line explains why the product exists — in Brad's worldview, there was a diplomatic phase where people politely requested that coworkers respect their space. That phase failed. Turf Tags are the escalation. Stickers are what happens after words stop working. Brad sees this as a natural progression in conflict resolution. He's not wrong.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-7: Red Divider Line
**Treatment:** A horizontal red line separating the product identity zone (above) from the compliance/fine-print zone (below). Creates a clear visual break.

**Placement:** Spanning the width of the front face, roughly two-thirds of the way down.

**Design note:** The red line functions like the divider on official documents between the public-facing header and the regulatory fine print. Everything above it is marketing. Everything below it is "compliance." Brad thinks this makes the box look official. He's right — it does — and the fine print below it is where the escalation happens.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-8: Product Contents Line
**Treatment:** Small, clean, factual. All caps. Reads as a regulatory contents declaration.

**Placement:** Below the red divider line, left-aligned

> CONTAINS: 12 ADHESIVE PROPERTY MARKERS

**Design note:** "Adhesive property markers" is Brad's regulatory term for stickers. Not stickers. Not decals. Property markers. Because in Brad's world, applying a sticker to your monitor is a property claim, and property claims require official markers. The count (12) must match the actual sticker count in the box — if the count changes, this line updates. Brad would never misrepresent inventory on a compliance document.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-9: Application Notice
**Treatment:** Small text, regulatory format. The asterisk signals a footnote — which is where the escalation lives.

**Placement:** Below contents line

> FOR SURFACE APPLICATION ONLY*

**Design note:** This reads as a standard application instruction — like "for external use only" on a bottle. The asterisk promises a footnote that will clarify. The footnote (TT-11) delivers.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-10: Deployment Notice
**Treatment:** Small text, legal-notice format. The dagger (†) signals a second footnote.

**Placement:** Below application notice

> DEPLOYMENT NOTICE: Application of these markers constitutes a legally binding claim.†

**Design note:** "Deployment" — not "application" or "use." Brad deploys stickers. The deployment notice reads exactly like the fine print on a property deed or lease agreement. The dagger footnote (TT-12) contains the correction. Together, the notice and its footnote form a two-beat joke: (1) a claim of legal authority, (2) an admission that no such authority exists, followed by (3) instructions to pretend it does.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-11: Footnote — Surface Clarification
**Treatment:** Very small text, footnote format. The asterisk callback.

**Placement:** Bottom zone of front face, footnote area

> * Surfaces only. We've had this conversation.

**Design note:** The footnote implies that someone, at some point, applied a Turf Tags sticker to something that was not a surface. The fact that this had to be stated explicitly — and that the phrasing suggests it's come up more than once — is the joke. Brad doesn't elaborate on what happened. The reader's imagination does better work than specifics would. "We've had this conversation" is Brad's exhausted HR voice.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-12: Footnote — Legal Disclaimer
**Treatment:** Very small text, footnote format. The dagger callback.

**Placement:** Below the asterisk footnote

> † It does not. Act like it does.

**Design note:** Four words of legal disclaimer followed by four words of Brad's actual advice. The footnote contradicts the deployment notice it references — these markers do NOT constitute a legally binding claim. Brad knows this. He doesn't care. His instruction is to act as if they do, because territorial confidence is more important than territorial legality. This is Brad's entire philosophy compressed into eight words.

**Status:** Draft.

**Treatment:** Very small text, bottom of the front face. The final escalation. Reads as a disclaimer that got past legal review because nobody was paying attention.

**Design note:** The escalation structure: "3 workplace disputes" is plausible and funny — someone stuck a Turf Tags sticker on a shared printer and it caused a thing. In his mind, if the product caused a divorce, it means the product worked — someone's territorial boundaries were finally enforced. The specificity of the numbers (3, 1) makes it sound like Brad actually tracked this data. He did.

The bottom half is Brad, appearing as the Chief Territorial Officer. ### TT-15: Form Header
**Treatment:** Bold, institutional, centered. Reads like the header on a government assessment form. Horizontal rules above and below.

**Placement:** Top of surface

> **TERRITORIAL ASSESSMENT REPORT**
> Form WPL-7 | Elevate Co. — Bureau of Territorial Affairs

**Design note:** "Form WPL-7" implies there are at least six previous forms in the Workspace Personalization Labels filing system. There aren't. Brad started at 7 because he thinks higher form numbers signal an established bureaucracy. The pipe separator and division name make this look exactly like a county or federal form header. Brad designed this form. Brad is the only person who has ever filled one out.

### TT-16: Status Assessment Block
**Treatment:** Two-column status field with checkbox formatting. Reads like the top section of an inspection report — checkboxes, status indicators, threat levels.

**Placement:** Upper half of surface, below form header

> **WORKSPACE STATUS:** [x] VULNERABLE
> **THREAT LEVEL:** ELEVATED
> **PRIOR CLAIMS ON FILE:** NONE RECOGNIZED
> **RECOMMENDED ACTION:** IMMEDIATE DEPLOYMENT

**Design note:** The assessment block is formatted like a facility inspection report. "WORKSPACE STATUS: VULNERABLE" uses the checkbox to indicate the assessment is complete — the workspace has been evaluated and found wanting. "THREAT LEVEL: ELEVATED" borrows directly from the national security threat level system (the DHS color-coded alert scale). Brad is applying homeland security terminology to a desk in a WeWork. "PRIOR CLAIMS ON FILE: NONE RECOGNIZED" is the territorial kicker — even if someone else thinks that desk is theirs, their claim is not recognized by the Bureau of Territorial Affairs. "RECOMMENDED ACTION: IMMEDIATE DEPLOYMENT" is Brad prescribing stickers with the urgency of an emergency response directive.

**Status:** Draft.

### TT-17: Expected Outcomes Section
**Treatment:** A structured data block with percentages. Reads like the projected results section of an impact assessment — quantified, specific, escalating from plausible to absurd.

**Placement:** Middle of back face, below status block

> **EXPECTED OUTCOMES:**
> 75% of office territory claimed within 6 weeks
> 100% of coworkers will have an opinion about it

**Design note:** Three percentages. Three beats. The first (75%) is plausible — it sounds like a real market penetration metric. A territorial consultant would project a 75% claim rate. The second (100%) escalates — every single coworker will notice. The third (0%) is the punchline — nobody will confront you directly. The joke is the gap between "everyone has an opinion" and "nobody will voice it." This is how office territorial disputes actually work. Brad presented passive-aggressive avoidance as a positive outcome metric. He tracked it as a success indicator.

**Status:** Draft.

## SURFACE 3 — TOP LONG EDGE (5.0" x 0.5")

*The narrow strip visible when the box is sitting in the tray. This is the standout surface — the single best panel on the box. Five IKEA-style instruction icons in a horizontal deployment procedure strip. The format is universally recognizable (IKEA assembly instructions), the first two steps are real, and the last three escalate into pure absurdity. This is the read-aloud moment — the surface someone picks up and shows to whoever is nearby.*

---

### TT-19: Deployment Procedure Strip — IKEA Instruction Icons
**Treatment:** Five sequential icons in clean black line art on yellow background, arranged left to right with thin vertical dividers between each panel. Each icon has a single-word label underneath. The visual language is IKEA assembly instructions — minimal, universally understood, deadpan. Numbered steps (1 through 5) in small text above each icon.

**Placement:** Full width of the top long edge, evenly spaced

> **1. PEEL** [Icon: hand peeling corner of a sticker from backing sheet]
>
> **2. STICK** [Icon: hand pressing sticker flat onto a surface — fingers splayed, applying pressure]
>
> **3. HOLD COURT** [Icon: figure seated on a throne, two smaller figures bowing on either side — medieval royal audience scene rendered in IKEA-flat style]
>
> **4. ACCEPT PARADE** [Icon: figure standing in a convertible car, waving to crowd, confetti falling — ticker-tape parade rendered in IKEA-flat style]
>
> **5. EXIT** [Icon: figure climbing a rope ladder to a helicopter departing from the roof of an office building — dramatic extraction rendered in IKEA-flat style]

**Design note:** This is the best surface on the box. The IKEA instruction format is one of the most universally recognized visual languages in the world — everyone has stared at those wordless assembly diagrams. Using it for sticker deployment is already funny. The escalation is what makes it exceptional.

Steps 1-2 are real. You actually do peel the sticker and stick it somewhere. The instructions are accurate. This grounds the sequence in reality and makes the reader nod along — yes, peel, yes, stick.

Step 3 is the turn. You've stuck a sticker on your monitor and now you're holding court like medieval royalty. The bowing figures are the coworkers who must now acknowledge your territorial sovereignty. The leap from "press sticker onto surface" to "receive fealty from subjects" is the entire joke of the product compressed into one panel transition.

Step 4 escalates further. You've moved from throne room to ticker-tape parade. The convertible. The waving. The confetti. Brad genuinely believes that applying a vinyl sticker to a desk lamp warrants a public celebration. The IKEA flat-style rendering of a ticker-tape parade is visually absurd in the best way.

Step 5 is the exit. A helicopter extraction from the roof of an office building. You peeled a sticker, stuck it on a filing cabinet, held court, accepted your parade, and now you're being extracted by helicopter. The helicopter exit as the final panel is the chef's kiss — it implies that after achieving total territorial dominance, the only move left is to leave via the most dramatic exit possible. Mission complete.

The five-panel strip should be readable at arm's length. The icons must be simple enough to register at the narrow edge's 0.5" height, but detailed enough that the throne, convertible, and helicopter are identifiable. IKEA's actual instruction style is the reference — minimal line weight, no shading, flat perspective, stick figures with just enough detail to communicate the action.

This surface alone justifies keeping the box as a desk object after the stickers are deployed.

**Status:** Draft.

*Narrow strip on the underside. The safety/legal warning surface. Played completely straight — reads as a genuine product liability disclaimer, with content that escalates into territorial absurdity.*

### TT-20: Safety & Compliance Warning
**Treatment:** Very small text, full width. Uses the standard safety-warning format — the kind of fine print on the bottom of any consumer product. Black text on yellow. The universal warning triangle icon (!) at the left edge.

**Placement:** Full width of the bottom long edge, centered vertically

> **!** WARNING: Not responsible for disputes, confrontations, passive-aggressive Post-it responses, or anyone bigger than you. Apply at your own risk. Elevate Co. assumes no liability for territorial outcomes.

**Design note:** The warning format is played completely straight — the triangle icon, the bold "WARNING," the liability disclaimer language. The content escalates in the standard three-beat pattern: "disputes" (plausible), "confrontations" (escalation), "passive-aggressive Post-it responses" (specific and real — this is exactly how office territorial disputes actually play out), "anyone bigger than you" (physical threat played as a compliance consideration). "Apply at your own risk" is the double meaning — apply the sticker, but also apply this philosophy. "Elevate Co. assumes no liability for territorial outcomes" sounds exactly like a real product liability statement. Brad's legal department wrote this. Brad is the legal department.

## SURFACE 5 — SHORT SIDE A (3.5" x 0.5")

*One of the two narrow ends. Visible when the box sits sideways in the tray. A territorial statement rendered as an official stamp or seal.*

---

### TT-21: Territorial Declaration Stamp
**Treatment:** Centered text, formatted as an official rubber-stamp impression or compliance seal. Bold, compact, institutional.

**Placement:** Full width, centered vertically

> **SHARING IS ~~CARING~~ OVER.**

**Design note:** The box's thesis statement in four words. "Sharing is caring" is a childhood platitude everyone learned in kindergarten. The strikethrough kills "caring" and replaces it with "over." The entire territorial philosophy of Turf Tags — that the era of communal workspace harmony has ended and individual claims must now be enforced — is compressed into a single visual edit. The strikethrough is the design element that makes this work. The reader sees the original platitude, registers the correction, and laughs. This is the most efficient joke on the box.

The placement on a short side means it's a discovery moment — the recipient has to rotate the box to find it. Discovery jokes reward close reading.

**Status:** Draft.

## SURFACE 7 — TUCK FLAP (interior, visible when box is opened)

*The first text the recipient sees when they open the box. One line. The deployment order. This is the threshold between reading about territorial marking and actually doing it.*

---

### TT-23: Opening Moment — Deployment Order
**Treatment:** Clean, centered, bold black text on the inside of the tuck flap. Direct. Imperative. No warmth — this is an instruction.

**Placement:** Centered on tuck flap interior

> **START WITH THE FRIDGE.**

**Design note:** Five words. A specific deployment instruction. When the recipient opens the box, this is the first thing they read — and it tells them exactly where to put the first sticker. Not "wherever you want." Not "use your judgment." The fridge. The office fridge is the most contested territory in any workplace. Food theft, passive-aggressive labeling, the Tupperware graveyard on the bottom shelf — the fridge is a war zone everyone recognizes. Brad is telling you to plant your flag there first because it's the highest-value target.

The specificity is what makes this work. After an entire box of institutional assessment language, compliance forms, IKEA instruction panels, and Brad's self-mythology, the tuck flap gives one concrete, actionable order. It primes deployment. The recipient is already thinking about the fridge before they've even looked at the stickers. By the time they see the sticker sheet, they're not deciding IF they'll use one — they're deciding which sticker goes on the fridge.

This is the most important line on the box for the product's downstream goal (stickers actually getting deployed in the wild). Everything else makes you laugh. This makes you act.

**Status:** Draft.
