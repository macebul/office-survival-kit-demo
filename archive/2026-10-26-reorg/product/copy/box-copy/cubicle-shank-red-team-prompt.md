## 1.1 THE WORLD

**The office is a prison. This is not a metaphor.**

Nobody in this universe thinks the office/prison comparison is clever or funny. There's nothing to compare — the office just IS this way. You serve your sentence. You do your time. You survive your shift. The language of incarceration IS the language of corporate life. It's the water everyone swims in.

This is what makes the product work: the reader recognizes their OWN reality, not a joke about their reality.

### Shared Vocabulary (used without comment, as the default language)

| This world's term | What it refers to | Tone |
|---|---|---|
| Doing time / serving your sentence | Working at a company | Matter-of-fact |
| Your cell | Your cubicle or desk | Normal |
| The yard / yard time | Break room, common areas, breaks | Normal |
| Mess hall | Break room / kitchen | Normal |
| Solitary | Working alone, noise-canceling headphones, bathroom hiding | Normal |
| Contraband | Anything that makes the day bearable but isn't officially sanctioned | Normal |
| Lifers | Long-tenured employees | Respectful, even |
| Doing a bid | A stint at a company | Normal |
| Release day | Last day, retirement, quitting | Aspirational |
| Parole | Notice period, transitioning out | Normal |
| The warden | Upper management, HR, the boss | Normal |
| Intake / processing | Onboarding | Normal |
| Commissary | Vending machines, the office supply closet | Normal |
| Shank / shiv | Your pen, your one reliable tool | Affectionate |
| Ink | Tattoos that mark your time served | Earned |
| Turf | Your desk, your claimed spaces | Territorial |
| Snitch | The person who reports everything to management | Known hazard |
| Rap sheet | Your performance review history | Dreaded |

**Rules:**
- This vocabulary is used EVERYWHERE. Stepping outside it should feel like stepping out of character.
- The world is played straight. Nobody winks. Nobody says "get it?" The moment anyone acknowledges the comparison as a comparison, the world breaks.
- Absurdity comes from **specificity within the premise**, not from the premise itself.
- New terms can emerge naturally. The test: would someone in this world use it without thinking twice?

### The World's Tone

**Sardonic, not cynical.** The people in this world haven't given up — they've adapted. Brad saw a market. The reader is getting through it with humor and solidarity. Nobody is nihilistic. The attitude is: "this is absurd, we all know it's absurd, and here's how we get through it together."

That warmth underneath the dark humor is what makes this a gift someone gives to a coworker they actually like. It's not mean. It's validating.

## 1.2 THE TWO BRADS

Brad Sterling operates in two distinct modes, and the comedy comes from the fact that both exist on the same product, written by the same person, with zero self-awareness about the gap between them.

### Brad Mode 1: Product Copy Brad
**When Brad is writing about the office, the product, the jokes**

This Brad is actually **competent**. He knows his audience. He can write a sardonic Nutrition Facts label, a deadpan IT Asset Tag, a perfectly absurd Care & Storage warning. This is Brad the marketer — he understands what's funny about office life and packages it well. This is why Elevate Co. exists at all. The product works.

**Voice:** Dry, institutional, straight-faced. The humor comes from the format (official labels, FDA layouts, QC stickers) played totally straight while the content escalates into absurdity. Brad executes this competently.

**Examples (from existing exterior copy):**
- "PROPERTY OF: NOBODY ANYMORE"
- "Serving Size: 1 Monday / Servings Per Container: Too Many"
- "DO NOT REMOVE UNDER PENALTY OF MONDAY"
- "Not a significant source of work-life balance, upward mobility, or the feeling that any of this matters."

**Why it works:** The reader laughs WITH the copy. It's genuinely funny. Brad knows how to sell a gag gift.

### Brad Mode 2: Personal Brand Brad
**When Brad is writing about Brad**

This Brad is a disaster. The moment the subject becomes himself — his story, his vision, his credentials, his photo — every ounce of self-awareness evaporates. He becomes a hustle-culture LinkedIn post given physical form. He is completely, unshakably sincere. He never winks. He thinks this is what leadership looks like.

**Voice:** Aspirational, self-mythologizing, motivational-speaker cadence. Everything is "premium," "vision," "disrupt," "elevate." Trademark symbols on everything.

**Examples:**
- "He didn't see contraband. He saw potential."
- "Premium workplace tools, premium packaging, and a premium price point."
- "Three-time nominee for the International Workplace Innovation Awards and an occasional keynote speaker at conferences he organizes himself."
- "A Brad Sterling Experience(TM)"

**Why it works:** The reader laughs AT Brad.

### The Gap IS the Joke

The single funniest thing about the product is that both Brads are on the same packaging. The reader encounters competent product humor AND unhinged self-promotion, realizes the same person created both, and that realization is its own punchline.

**CRITICAL: Don't sabotage the product copy to make Brad look dumber.** The BETTER the product copy is, the MORE absurd Brad's founder bio becomes by contrast.

### Brad's Comedy Gradient

| Brad is talking about... | Self-awareness level | Comedy type |
|---|---|---|
| Office life / the product | High — he knows what's funny | Genuinely good jokes in straight-faced formats |
| Himself / his brand | Zero — complete delusion | The gap between his self-image and reality |
| How he built the business | Negative — he confesses without knowing it | Accidental honesty framed as achievement |

### Brad Writing Patterns

**The Format/Content Escalation:**
1. First line reads as completely plausible
2. Second line has a slight edge
3. Third line is clearly a joke but the format stays serious
4. Final line is full absurd but still formatted perfectly

**The Confessional Drop:** Brad buries self-incriminating admissions in corporate language. He thinks they sound transparent and visionary. They sound like testimony.

**The Credential Stack:** Start almost-real, end obviously self-generated, present all with equal weight.

**The Trademark Reflex:** Brad trademarks everything. The (TM) symbol is his nervous tic.

**The Premium Stack:** Brad uses "premium" the way other people use commas.

**The Oblivious Dual-Name:** Brad switches between the prison-world name and corporate rebrand without noticing any tonal difference.

### Brad NEVER:
- Winks at the camera or acknowledges the joke
- Shows genuine expertise about office survival (he's never worked in one)
- Recognizes the prison aesthetic for what it is
- Appears in handwriting — Brad is always printed, polished, designed
- Questions why his "workplace wellness brand" sounds like a correctional facility

## 1.3 THE COMEDY MECHANICS

### The Core Rule

> **"Format stays buttoned-up. Content gets unleashed."**

The typography, layout, label designs, FDA formatting, corporate letterhead, award seals — all stay polished and straight-faced. That's the straight man. The actual words inside those formats go for laughs on every line.

### The 6 Humor Elements

Every piece of copy needs **at least 2** of these 6 elements. "Recognizable" should almost always be one of them:

| Element | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Clever** | Wordplay, structural comedy, double meanings, hidden layers | A badge that works on 3 levels simultaneously |
| **Bizarre** | Unexpected combinations, absurd juxtapositions | A coroner reviewing a pen and giving it 5 stars |
| **Cruel** | Dark humor, uncomfortable truths, schadenfreude | "Grip tested across relaxed hands, writing hands, and one we'd describe as 'determined.'" |
| **Naughty** | Taboo-adjacent, things you shouldn't say, the loophole | "We put the pen in penitentiary." |
| **Recognizable** | "That's MY office" — specific details the reader has lived | Kevin "borrowing" your labeled lunch |
| **Cute** | Charm, warmth, unexpected tenderness inside dark comedy | "Bold. Brave. Washable." (deflation with affection) |

### The Escalation Pattern

Line 1: Completely plausible
Line 2: Slight edge
Line 3: Clearly a joke, format stays serious
Line 4: Full absurd, format still perfectly intact

**The format NEVER cracks.**

### The 7 Mechanical Rules

1. **Joke lands on the LAST word or phrase.** Nothing after the punchline.
2. **Setup is a REAL product copy format.** Feature bullet, spec, disclaimer, FAQ, care instruction, badge, stamp.
3. **Punchline word must be CONCRETE and UNCOMFORTABLE.** "Denim" is funny. "Materials" is not.
4. **Talk TO the buyer.** "Your ___." "You'll ___." Make the reader complicit.
5. **Be CHEERFUL about something you shouldn't be cheerful about.**
6. **Never instruct the reader to be threatening.** Describe the product so the threat is OBVIOUS without directing it.
7. **Rule of three when using lists.** Two normal items, third breaks the pattern with a REAL THING.

### Additional Mechanics

- Strongest lines are 5-13 words
- Ratio: ~60-70% normal product copy, 30-40% absurd
- The second sentence makes the first worse
- Short beats long
- No puns — Brad is dry
- No exclamation points — keep it institutional and flat
- Every insult needs absurd specificity

## 1.4 CRAFT STANDARDS

### Sentences
- Simple. Like a witty email to a friend.
- Prune words that don't serve the humor.
- Short beats long. Always.

### Imagery
- Write visually. The reader should see a scene.
- Only include details that make the picture funnier.
- Leave room for the reader's imagination.

### Word Choice
- Use funny words: familiar but rarely used, often with hard consonants.
- Use pop culture references the target audience (25-45 office workers) recognizes.
- Use animal analogies when stuck.

### Exaggeration
- Find the worst case, multiply by ten.
- The exaggeration must have near-logic underneath it.

### Density
- Every surface should reward re-reading.
- Surface scan = one laugh. Close read = escalating laughs.

### Callbacks
- End pieces by referencing something that already got a laugh.
- Callbacks create a sense of craftsmanship and closure.

### Specificity Over Puns
- Humor comes from specific details and observations, not wordplay.

## 1.5 THE VOICE — WITTY YETI ENERGY

### What the consumer-facing voice IS

A **warm, cheerful, slightly conspiratorial friend** who is genuinely excited to sell you something ridiculous. Think: your fun, slightly irresponsible buddy who found something amazing and can't wait to show you.

The brand **KNOWS what it's selling.** It found a loophole. It's cheerfully proud. It's not confused, coy, or playing dumb.

**Tone markers:** "Look," "Seriously," "Trust us," "We're not going to ask," "We believe in you," "Which is to say."

### What the voice is NOT
- NOT dry, clinical, or neutral
- NOT professional or corporate-sounding
- NOT a one-liner comedian
- NOT confused about its own product
- NOT instructing the reader to be threatening
- NOT observational office humor ("meetings suck")

### The Peppiness Requirement (CRITICAL)

We repeatedly fall into writing copy that's too professional, too neutral. It reads like a competent copywriter instead of an enthusiastic friend. The copy needs enthusiasm, warmth, personality, levity, and casual conversational delivery.

**TOO NEUTRAL:** "Won't bleed through paper. Can't speak for anything else."
**CLOSER TO RIGHT:** "Other pens leak in your pocket. This one doesn't leak anywhere. Not in your pocket. Not in your hand. Not in Kevin. Completely leak-proof design."

The second version is chatty, building, listing things off excitedly. Kevin just happens to be on the list. That's the energy.

## 1.7 KILL ON SIGHT

- **Puns.** Brad is dry.
- **Exclamation points in product copy.**
- **Winking at the reader.**
- **Actual workplace advice.**
- **Making Brad self-aware.**
- **Generic insults.** Every insult needs absurd specificity.
- **Acknowledging the office/prison comparison as a comparison.**
- **Buttons/tags/reactions after the punchline.**
- **Abstract/corporate punchline words.** "Concerns" = vague. "Denim" = concrete.
- **Observational office humor.** "Meetings suck" isn't copy.
- **Three items from the same category in a rule-of-three.** Two normal, one crazy.
- **Leading with violence and pivoting to product.** Lead with product.
- **Repeating the same structural trick across variations.**
- **Explaining the joke.**
- **Writing the brand as confused about its own product.**
- **Cap references on this pen.** There is NO CAP on this pen.

## 1.10 BOSS FEEDBACK HISTORY

### Approved with enthusiasm
- Leak-proof line (see material below) — "really good"
- "Does it come in red?" / "Eventually." — "excellent"

### Structural feedback
- Rule of three: "Normal, normal, crazy. Three of the same category isn't funny."
- Verified purchaser reviews: "A user who is using the product normally, and then it turns [dark] is the right setup/structure."
- Boss wants it DARK, delivered with warmth. Not tame.

### Universal rejection
- v01 of all items was rejected: "not funny enough, doesn't meet the standards set by existing Witty Yeti products." Too serious, too professional, not enough personality.

### Lessons from development sessions
- **Badges need layered comedy.** "Janitor Verified" works because of THREE layers. Single-mechanic labels fail.
- **Quantity lines need subversion.** Cross-category comparison with specific flaws per competitor.
- **Feature bullets need playful energy.** Dark without warmth fails. Absurd material escalation works.
- **Stop at the punchline.** "Determined" was the punchline. Everything after killed it.
- **Physical product facts are non-negotiable.** No cap = no cap jokes.
- **Second lines can diminish the first.** Sometimes the best revision is deleting the follow-up.
- **Weapon-spec double-meaning lines are a narrow comedy lane.** One approach, not the whole toolkit.

# PART 4: YOUR TASK — RED TEAM REVIEW

## What "Red Team" means here

You are a quality-obsessed comedy editor. Your job is to be ruthlessly honest about what's actually funny and what's just... fine. "Fine" doesn't ship. Every line on this packaging needs to make an office worker laugh out loud or nod with dark recognition.

## The review process

For EVERY piece of material above:

1. **Read it cold.** Did you laugh, smile, or nod? Or did you just think "that's clever"? "Clever" isn't enough. It needs to hit.

2. **Run the checklist** (Section 1.8) against it. Does it actually pass?

3. **Check for these specific failure modes:**
   - Is it trying too hard? (Overthinking kills comedy)
   - Is the joke in the structure but not in the words? (Format-joke without funny content)
   - Is it too similar to another line we already have? (Redundancy)
   - Does it rely on a trick that's already been used? (Diminishing returns)
   - Is it dark without being warm? (Misses the WY energy)
   - Does it instruct instead of describe? (Rule 6 violation)
   - Is the punchline actually the last word? (Rule 1 check)
   - Would you show this to a coworker, or just appreciate it intellectually?

4. **Rate each piece:**
   - **KEEP** — Actually funny. Would make someone laugh.
   - **CUT** — Not meeting the bar. Explain why.
   - **REVISE** — Has a kernel but needs specific work. Say what.
   - **REDUNDANT** — Too similar to something better. Say which one wins.

5. **For each category (subtitles, taglines, etc.), rank the survivors** from strongest to weakest.

6. **Flag any gaps** — elements that are missing or underrepresented.

## Output format

Organize your review by element category. For each:

- List of REVISE items with what needs to change
- Ranked order of survivors
